Introduction: The Intellectual Behind the Legend

To the popular imagination, Napoleon Bonaparte (15 August 1769 – 5 May 1821) is remembered primarily as a military genius, a master tactician, and the conqueror of Europe.

However, his military victories were only one facet of a deeply complex, hyper-active intellect. Napoleon was first and foremost an intellectual, a reformer, and an insatiable consumer of knowledge.

His rise from a minor Corsican noble to Emperor of the French was fueled not just by ambition, but by a rigorous self-education, an extraordinary cognitive framework, a legendary work ethic, and a lifelong devotion to reading and writing.

This article explores the private mind of Napoleon: his intense work habits, his structured reading systems, his writing projects, and the mental architecture that allowed him to govern millions of citizens while simultaneously conducting complex military campaigns.

1. The Insatiable Reader: Napoleon’s Literary World

From his early childhood in Ajaccio, Corsica, and his lonely school days at the military academy of Brienne-le-Château, Napoleon was a bookworm. While his peers played outside, he retreated to the library, immersing himself in history, mathematics, and geography.

A. The Traveling Library (La Bibliothèque Portative)

Napoleon could not function without books. Wherever he went—whether campaigning in the deserts of Egypt, the snows of Russia, or the palace of Saint-Cloud—he brought a massive library with him.

He commissioned a specialized “traveling library” (bibliothèque portative). Hundreds of books were bound in thin leather, stripped of blank margins to save weight, and packed into custom-designed oak chests lined with green velvet. When he traveled in his carriage, he would read constantly, throwing books out of the window once he had finished them.

In 1808, frustrated by the limitations of standard book sizes, Napoleon instructed his librarian, Antoine-Alexandre Barbier, to design a mobile library of 1,000 volumes printed in a compact 12mo format, specifically divided into: * 40 volumes on religion * 40 volumes of drama * 40 volumes of poetry * 60 volumes of novels * 60 volumes of history * The remainder containing memoirs, biography, and geography

B. What Napoleon Read

Napoleon’s reading preferences reveal his pragmatic approach to knowledge. He read to learn, to govern, and to understand the psychology of the nations he conquered.

  • The Classics: He was obsessed with antiquity. He read Plutarch’s Parallel Lives repeatedly, drawing inspiration from the leadership of Alexander the Great, Julius Caesar, and Lycurgus. He read Homer’s Iliad and Virgil’s Aeneid.
  • Political Philosophy: In his youth, he devoured the works of Jean-Jacques Rousseau (particularly The Social Contract), Montesquieu’s The Spirit of the Laws, and John Locke. While he later grew cynical of abstract “ideologues,” their concepts of legal equality and state structure heavily influenced his reforms.
  • Science and Mathematics: Unlike many contemporary leaders who were educated purely in the humanities, Napoleon was a mathematician. He read treatises on geometry, military engineering, physics, and chemistry.
  • Literature and Poetry: His favorite work of fiction was Goethe’s The Sorrows of Young Werther, which he claimed to have read seven times. During his meeting with Goethe in Erfurt in 1808, the two spent hours discussing the novel’s structure. He was also a passionate admirer of the pseudo-ancient Celtic poetry of Ossian (compiled by James Macpherson), carrying a copy with him on his campaigns.

2. Work Ethic and Cognitive Systems

Napoleon’s ability to manage a vast empire and direct multiple armies simultaneously was the result of a highly disciplined mind and a work ethic that pushed the boundaries of human endurance.

A. The “Drawers of the Mind”

Napoleon possessed a rare capacity for deep focus. He explained his cognitive organization using the metaphor of a chest of drawers:

“Different subjects and different affairs are arranged in my head as in a cupboard. When I wish to interrupt one train of thought, I close its drawer and open another. They do not mix, and they never fatigue or embarrass me. When I want to sleep, I close all the drawers, and I am fast asleep.”

This mental compartmentalization allowed him to shift from drafting the civil code to planning the logistics of an artillery division within minutes, giving each task his undivided attention.

B. Sleep Habits and Daily Routine

Napoleon’s sleep habits were irregular and highly efficient. * The 4-Hour Sleep Cycle: He rarely slept for more than three or four consecutive hours. He typically retired around 10:00 PM, woke up at 2:00 AM or 3:00 AM when his mind was freshest, worked for several hours, and then took a short bath or a catnap in the afternoon. * Catnaps: Napoleon possessed the ability to fall asleep instantly on command. During battles, he would lay down his cloak, sleep for ten to fifteen minutes amidst the noise of artillery, and wake up fully alert. * The Early Morning Working Hours: He believed that the early hours of the morning were the most productive. He would review administrative reports, finance accounts, and foreign intelligence while the palace was completely silent.

C. Dictation and the Army of Secretaries

Napoleon’s thoughts moved faster than ink could flow. He did not write his official correspondence or decrees by hand; instead, he dictated them. * Simultaneous Dictation: He famously walked pacing back and forth in his study, dictating to three or four secretaries at the same time. Each secretary would write down a different letter, and Napoleon would rotate between them without losing his train of thought. * Speed Requirements: His secretaries had to develop shorthand codes. If a secretary asked him to repeat a sentence, Napoleon would grow furious, stating that his thoughts were a flowing stream that could not be interrupted. * Logistical Attention to Detail: He personally reviewed the smallest administrative details. He checked the prices of bread for his troops, the number of horses in reserve, the condition of boots, and the exact budgets of provincial hospitals. He believed that control over details was the foundation of strategic success.

3. What Napoleon Wrote

Napoleon was a prolific writer throughout his life, transitioning from romantic prose in his youth to administrative genius in his prime, and finally to self-mythologizing memoirs in exile.

A. Romantic Novelist: Clisson et Eugénie

In 1795, as a frustrated 26-year-old artillery officer, Napoleon wrote a short romantic novella titled “Clisson et Eugénie”. * The Plot: The story is a thinly veiled autobiography reflecting his real-life romance with Désirée Clary (who later married Jean-Baptiste Bernadotte and became Queen of Sweden). The protagonist, Clisson, is a brilliant military officer who retreats to the countryside, falls in love with Eugénie, but is eventually called back to war, where he dies heroically after discovering Eugénie’s infidelity. * The Style: Written in the sentimental, melancholy style of Rousseau, the novella showcases a young Napoleon wrestling with themes of honor, love, isolation, and disillusionment.

B. The Imperial Correspondence

The sheer volume of Napoleon’s written output is staggering. The Fondation Napoléon has cataloged over 33,000 surviving letters dictated or written by him. * The Scope: His letters cover everything from passionate, codependent love letters to Joséphine de Beauharnais during the Italian Campaign, to micro-managing instructions to his brothers whom he placed on European thrones, to detailed treaties. * The Tone: His military letters are terse, imperious, and direct. His letters to Joséphine, by contrast, are raw, emotionally volatile, and deeply intimate, revealing a highly passionate temperament.

C. The Exile Memoirs: Mémorial de Sainte-Hélène

Following his defeat at Waterloo in 1815, Napoleon was exiled to the remote South Atlantic island of Saint Helena. With no battles left to fight, he fought his final war on paper, aiming to shape his historical legacy.

  • The Dictations: Over several years, Napoleon dictated his memoirs, reflections, and historical justifications to his companion in exile, Emmanuel de Las Cases.
  • The Legacy Project: The resulting book, the Mémorial de Sainte-Hélène, became an instant bestseller across Europe after Napoleon’s death. In it, he reframed his reign not as a military dictatorship, but as a crusade to spread the liberal ideals of the French Revolution (equality, meritocracy, the rule of law) across a feudal Europe. This work laid the foundation for the political ideology of Bonapartism.

4. Key Learnings and Administrative Legacies

Napoleon’s intellectual habits translated directly into practical governance. He considered his administrative reforms to be more permanent than his military victories:

“My true glory is not to have won forty battles… Waterloo will erase the memory of so many victories. But what nothing will destroy, what will live forever, is my Civil Code.” — Napoleon in exile

The Pillars of his Legacy

  1. The Napoleonic Code (Code Civil des Français): Introduced in 1804, this unified system of civil law replaced the chaotic patchwork of feudal laws. It established the equality of all citizens before the law, protected property rights, codified religious freedom, and abolished feudal privileges.
  2. Meritocracy and the Lycées: Napoleon reformed French education by establishing the lycées (selective secondary schools) designed to train a class of civil servants and military officers based on intellectual merit rather than aristocratic birth.
  3. The Egyptian Expedition (1798): When Napoleon invaded Egypt, he did not just take soldiers; he took a “Commission of Sciences and Arts” consisting of 167 scientists, mathematicians, engineers, cartographers, and artists (the savants). This expedition led to:
    • The mapping of Egypt’s topography and ancient monuments.
    • The discovery of the Rosetta Stone, which allowed Jean-François Champollion to decipher Egyptian hieroglyphs.
    • The publication of the multi-volume Description de l’Égypte, which effectively founded the modern discipline of Egyptology.
