The Michelson Interferometer, LIGO, and the Mathematics of Black Hole Collisions

From nineteenth-century ether drift experiments to detecting sub-proton spacetime strain and calculating binary black hole chirp dynamics.

Physics
Optics
Astrophysics
General Relativity
Published

September 8, 2026

In 1887, Albert A. Michelson and Edward W. Morley used a beam of light split along two perpendicular arms to search for the “luminiferous ether”—the hypothetical medium through which light was thought to propagate. Their experiment yielded a famous null result, dismantling classical ether theory and paving the path for Einstein’s theory of Special Relativity.

Nearly 130 years later, on September 14, 2015, an evolution of that exact same optical configuration—the Laser Interferometer Gravitational-Wave Observatory (LIGO)—made the first direct detection of gravitational waves (GW150914). Originating from the catastrophic collision of two black holes 1.3 billion light-years away, these ripples in spacetime distorted LIGO’s 4-kilometer arms by less than a ten-thousandth the diameter of a proton.

This article explores the optical principles of the Michelson interferometer, the ultra-precision engineering of Advanced LIGO, and the mathematical physics governing binary black hole mergers.

1. The Classical Michelson Interferometer

The basic Michelson interferometer divides a coherent light beam into two orthogonal paths, reflects them off flat mirrors, and recombines them at a photodetector to produce an optical interference pattern.

Optical Configuration

  1. Light Source: Monochromatic laser of wavelength \(\lambda\).
  2. Beamsplitter: A 50:50 partially silvered glass plate inclined at \(45^\circ\), splitting the incident beam into:
    • Arm 1 (length \(L_1\), transmitted)
    • Arm 2 (length \(L_2\), reflected)
  3. End Mirrors (\(M_1, M_2\)): Perfectly perpendicular high-reflectivity mirrors reflecting the light back toward the beamsplitter.
  4. Photodetector: Records the combined light intensity.

Path Difference and Interference Equations

The round-trip travel distance in each arm is \(2L_1\) and \(2L_2\). The Optical Path Difference (OPD) is: \[\Delta s = 2(L_1 - L_2)\]

The resulting phase difference \(\Delta \phi\) between the two recombined electromagnetic waves is: \[\Delta \phi = k \Delta s = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda} \Delta s = \frac{4\pi}{\lambda} (L_1 - L_2)\]

Assuming equal electric field amplitudes \(E_0\) from both arms, the recombined field is: \[E_{\text{det}} = E_0 e^{i\omega t} + E_0 e^{i(\omega t + \Delta \phi)}\]

The detected optical intensity \(I \propto |E_{\text{det}}|^2\) obeys: \[I = 2 I_0 \left[ 1 + \cos(\Delta \phi) \right] = 4 I_0 \cos^2\left( \frac{\Delta \phi}{2} \right) = 4 I_0 \cos^2\left( \frac{2\pi (L_1 - L_2)}{\lambda} \right)\]

  • Constructive Interference (Bright Fringe): \(\Delta s = m \lambda \implies L_1 - L_2 = m \frac{\lambda}{2}\) (\(m \in \mathbb{Z}\)).
  • Destructive Interference (Dark Fringe): \(\Delta s = \left(m + \frac{1}{2}\right) \lambda \implies L_1 - L_2 = \left(m + \frac{1}{2}\right) \frac{\lambda}{4}\).

By holding the arms near a dark fringe (a “dark port” condition), any microscopic shift in path length \(\delta L = \delta(L_1 - L_2)\) produces a measurable change in transmitted laser power: \[\frac{dI}{d(\delta L)} \propto \sin\left(\frac{4\pi \delta L}{\lambda}\right)\]

2. The LIGO Experiment: Detecting Spacetime Strain

Gravitational waves are propagating quadrupolar perturbations of the spacetime metric \(g_{\mu\nu} = \eta_{\mu\nu} + h_{\mu\nu}\), predicted by Albert Einstein in 1916.

Quadrupole Nature and Arm Distortion

For a gravitational wave propagating along the \(z\)-axis with plus polarization (\(h_+\)), the spacetime line element is: \[ds^2 = -c^2 dt^2 + (1 + h_+) dx^2 + (1 - h_+) dy^2 + dz^2\]

As the wave passes, it simultaneously stretches space along the \(x\)-axis while compressing space along the \(y\)-axis: \[\Delta L_x = +\frac{1}{2} h L, \qquad \Delta L_y = -\frac{1}{2} h L\]

The differential displacement between the two orthogonal arms is: \[\Delta L = \Delta L_x - \Delta L_y = h L\]

The dimensionless gravitational wave strain is defined as: \[\boxed{h = \frac{\Delta L}{L}}\]

The Sensitivity Challenge: Sub-Proton Precision

For typical binary black hole mergers occurring hundreds of megaparsecs away, the strain reaching Earth is extraordinarily faint: \[h \sim 10^{-21}\]

LIGO’s physical arm length is \(L = 4\text{ km} = 4,000\text{ m}\). The physical displacement that LIGO must measure is: \[\Delta L = h \cdot L \approx (10^{-21}) \times (4,000\text{ m}) = 4 \times 10^{-18}\text{ m}\]

To put this in perspective: * Diameter of an atom: \(\approx 10^{-10}\text{ m}\) * Diameter of a proton: \(\approx 10^{-15}\text{ m}\) * LIGO displacement \(\Delta L\): \(\approx \frac{1}{10,000}\text{th the diameter of a proton}\)!

Optical Architecture of Advanced LIGO

A simple Michelson interferometer with a \(4\text{ km}\) arm cannot detect such minute displacements. Advanced LIGO introduces four revolutionary optical enhancements:

  1. Fabry-Pérot Arm Cavities: Partially transmitting mirrors at the entrance of each arm trap the laser light, causing it to bounce back and forth \(\approx 300\) times before escaping. This boosts the effective arm length from \(4\text{ km}\) to \(L_{\text{eff}} \approx 1,200\text{ km}\), increasing the accumulated phase shift by a factor of 300.
  2. Power Recycling Cavity: Because the interferometer is operated on a destructive dark fringe, nearly all laser light is reflected backwards toward the laser input. A power recycling mirror reflects this light back into the interferometer in phase, boosting the initial \(200\text{ W}\) laser input to over \(750\text{ kW}\) of circulating optical power inside the arms. Higher laser power reduces photon shot noise (\(\Delta h_{\text{shot}} \propto 1/\sqrt{P}\)).
  3. Signal Recycling Cavity: An extra mirror placed at the output dark port creates an additional resonant cavity that broadens and tunes the interferometer’s frequency response across the target astrophysical band (\(10\text{ Hz} - 2,000\text{ Hz}\)).
  4. Quantum Squeezed Vacuum Injection: Injecting specialized quantum “squeezed light” states into the dark port redistributes quantum uncertainty, lowering shot noise without proportionally increasing radiation pressure noise, operating past the classical Standard Quantum Limit (SQL).

3. Mathematical Physics of Binary Black Hole Collisions

When two black holes of masses \(m_1\) and \(m_2\) orbit each other, they violently churn spacetime, shedding orbital energy and angular momentum exclusively in the form of gravitational radiation.

The Einstein Quadrupole Formula

In linearized General Relativity, the gravitational wave luminosity (power radiated) by a mass distribution with second mass moment tensor \(I_{ij} = \int \rho(t, \mathbf{x}) x_i x_j d^3x\) is: \[\mathcal{L}_{\text{GW}} = -\frac{dE}{dt} = \frac{G}{5 c^5} \left\langle \dddot{I}_{ij} \dddot{I}_{ij} - \frac{1}{3} (\dddot{I}_{kk})^2 \right\rangle\]

For two point masses in a quasi-circular orbit of separation \(R\) and orbital frequency \(\omega_{\text{orb}}\), Kepler’s third law states: \[\omega_{\text{orb}}^2 = \frac{G M_{\text{tot}}}{R^3} \quad (\text{where } M_{\text{tot}} = m_1 + m_2)\]

Because the quadrupole moment rotates at twice the orbital frequency, the emitted gravitational wave has frequency: \[\boxed{f_{\text{GW}} = 2 f_{\text{orb}} = \frac{\omega_{\text{orb}}}{\pi}}\]

Evaluating the quadrupole formula yields the radiated power: \[\mathcal{L}_{\text{GW}} = -\frac{dE_{\text{orb}}}{dt} = \frac{32}{5} \frac{G^4}{c^5} \frac{m_1^2 m_2^2 (m_1 + m_2)}{R^5}\]

The Chirp Mass \(\mathcal{M}\)

The orbital energy of the Newtonian binary is: \[E_{\text{orb}} = -\frac{G m_1 m_2}{2 R}\]

Using the chain rule \(\frac{dE_{\text{orb}}}{dt} = \frac{dE_{\text{orb}}}{df_{\text{GW}}} \frac{df_{\text{GW}}}{dt}\) and equating to \(-\mathcal{L}_{\text{GW}}\), the individual masses collapse into a single universal parameter called the Chirp Mass \(\mathcal{M}\): \[\boxed{\mathcal{M} = \frac{(m_1 m_2)^{3/5}}{(m_1 + m_2)^{1/5}} = \mu^{3/5} M_{\text{tot}}^{2/5}}\] where \(\mu = \frac{m_1 m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\) is the reduced mass.

Frequency Evolution: The Characteristic “Chirp”

The rate at which the gravitational wave frequency accelerates upward is governed by: \[\boxed{\frac{df_{\text{GW}}}{dt} = \frac{96}{5} \pi^{8/3} \left( \frac{G \mathcal{M}}{c^3} \right)^{5/3} f_{\text{GW}}^{11/3}}\]

Because \(\frac{df}{dt} \propto f^{11/3}\), as the black holes draw closer, both the frequency and the amplitude rise exponentially, producing a rising audio-frequency tone termed a chirp.

Integrating from time \(t\) up to the merger time \(t_{\text{merger}}\) yields the time remaining until coalescence: \[\tau = t_{\text{merger}} - t = \frac{5}{256} \left( \frac{c^3}{G \mathcal{M}} \right)^{5/3} (\pi f_{\text{GW}})^{-8/3}\]

Measuring both \(f_{\text{GW}}(t)\) and \(\dot{f}_{\text{GW}}(t)\) directly from LIGO’s detector output allows physicists to extract the system’s chirp mass \(\mathcal{M}\) to extraordinary precision without knowing the distance to the source!

The Innermost Stable Circular Orbit (ISCO) and Peak Power

The inspiral phase ends when the separation shrinks to the Innermost Stable Circular Orbit (ISCO). For a non-spinning Schwarzschild black hole of mass \(M_{\text{tot}}\): \[R_{\text{ISCO}} = \frac{6 G M_{\text{tot}}}{c^2} = 3 r_s\]

Beyond \(R_{\text{ISCO}}\), stable orbits cease to exist; the black holes plunge toward each other at near the speed of light and merge into a single perturbed black hole, which vibrates and sheds its deformations via exponentially damped quasinormal ringdown modes.

The gravitational wave frequency at ISCO marks the peak of the inspiral: \[f_{\text{ISCO}} = \frac{c^3}{6^{3/2} \pi G M_{\text{tot}}} \approx \frac{4,396\text{ Hz}}{M_{\text{tot}} / M_\odot}\]

Radiated Energy: The Case of GW150914

In the milestone event GW150914: * Initial masses: \(m_1 \approx 36 M_\odot\), \(m_2 \approx 29 M_\odot\) (\(M_{\text{initial}} = 65 M_\odot\)) * Final remnant black hole mass: \(M_{\text{final}} \approx 62 M_\odot\) * Radiated mass-energy: \[\Delta M = M_{\text{initial}} - M_{\text{final}} \approx \mathbf{3.0 M_\odot}\]

In less than 0.2 seconds, three full solar masses of solid matter were converted entirely into pure gravitational wave energy (\(E = 3 M_\odot c^2 \approx 5.4 \times 10^{47}\text{ Joules}\)).

At its peak, the gravitational wave luminosity reached: \[\mathcal{L}_{\text{peak}} \approx 3.6 \times 10^{49}\text{ Watts} \approx 200,000 M_\odot c^2/\text{second}\]

During those brief fractions of a second, the collision of these two black holes radiated more power than all the stars in the observable universe combined.

4. Programmatic Simulation of the Gravitational Waveform

Code 
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# Time vector leading up to merger (t = 0 is merger)
t_inspiral = np.linspace(-0.25, -0.015, 1000)
t_merger = np.linspace(-0.015, 0.015, 200)
t_ringdown = np.linspace(0.015, 0.08, 500)

# Physical parameters modeled on GW150914 (Chirp Mass ~ 30 M_sun)
t_coal = 0.0 # merger time

# Inspiral Phase: Frequency f ~ (-t)^(-3/8), Amplitude A ~ (-t)^(-1/4)
tau = -t_inspiral
f_gw = 35.0 * (tau / 0.25)**(-3/8)
phi = -2.0 * (tau / 0.25)**(5/8) * 40.0
amp_inspiral = 1.0e-21 * (tau / 0.25)**(-1/4)
h_inspiral = amp_inspiral * np.cos(phi)

# Merger Phase: Smooth transitional peak
tau_m = t_merger
amp_merger = 1.8e-21 * np.exp(-(tau_m / 0.015)**2)
phi_m = np.linspace(phi[-1], phi[-1] + 6*np.pi, len(t_merger))
h_merger = amp_merger * np.cos(phi_m)

# Ringdown Phase: Damped sinusoid (Quasinormal mode)
tau_r = t_ringdown - 0.015
f_qnm = 250.0 # Hz (damping frequency)
tau_decay = 0.012 # seconds decay time
amp_ringdown = h_merger[-1] * np.exp(-tau_r / tau_decay)
h_ringdown = amp_ringdown * np.cos(2 * np.pi * f_qnm * tau_r + phi_m[-1])

# Combine full waveform
t_full = np.concatenate([t_inspiral, t_merger, t_ringdown])
h_full = np.concatenate([h_inspiral, h_merger, h_ringdown])

# Plotting
fig, (ax1, ax2) = plt.subplots(2, 1, figsize=(10, 7), sharex=True)

# Strain waveform
ax1.plot(t_full * 1000, h_full * 1e21, color='#1f77b4', lw=2, label=r'GW Strain $h(t)$')
ax1.axvline(0, color='#e74c3c', linestyle='--', alpha=0.7, label='Merger ($t = 0$)')
ax1.axvspan(-250, -15, color='#3498db', alpha=0.1, label='Inspiral Phase')
ax1.axvspan(-15, 15, color='#e74c3c', alpha=0.15, label='Merger (ISCO / Plunge)')
ax1.axvspan(15, 80, color='#2ecc71', alpha=0.1, label='Ringdown (QNM)')
ax1.set_ylabel(r'Strain $h(t) \times 10^{-21}$', fontsize=11, fontweight='bold')
ax1.set_title(r'Binary Black Hole Coalescence: The Gravitational Wave "Chirp"', fontsize=13, fontweight='bold')
ax1.grid(True, linestyle=':', alpha=0.6)
ax1.legend(loc='upper left', frameon=True, fontsize=9)

# Frequency evolution
f_full = np.concatenate([f_gw, np.linspace(f_gw[-1], 250, len(t_merger)), np.full_like(t_ringdown, 250)])
ax2.plot(t_full * 1000, f_full, color='#d35400', lw=2.5, label=r'GW Frequency $f_{\text{GW}}(t) \propto (-t)^{-3/8}$')
ax2.axvline(0, color='#e74c3c', linestyle='--', alpha=0.7)
ax2.set_xlabel('Time relative to merger [milliseconds]', fontsize=11, fontweight='bold')
ax2.set_ylabel('Frequency [Hz]', fontsize=11, fontweight='bold')
ax2.grid(True, linestyle=':', alpha=0.6)
ax2.legend(loc='upper left', frameon=True, fontsize=9)

plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

Summary

  1. Optical Principle: The Michelson interferometer translates minute optical path differences into macroscopic fringe shifts via \(I = 4 I_0 \cos^2\left(\frac{2\pi \Delta L}{\lambda}\right)\).
  2. LIGO’s Quadrupole Strain: Gravitational waves stretch one arm while compressing the other, producing differential strain \(h = \Delta L / L \sim 10^{-21}\), requiring sub-proton precision (\(\Delta L \sim 10^{-18}\text{ m}\)).
  3. Advanced Optical Cavities: Fabry-Pérot cavities and power recycling boost the effective arm length to \(1,200\text{ km}\) and the intracavity laser power to over \(750\text{ kW}\).
  4. The Gravitational Chirp: The rate of frequency evolution \(\frac{df}{dt} \propto \mathcal{M}^{5/3} f^{11/3}\) uniquely isolates the system’s chirp mass \(\mathcal{M}\), providing a direct cosmic ruler to measure black hole collisions billions of light-years away.
