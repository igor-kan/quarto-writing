Sound Waves: Continuum Mechanics, Thermodynamics, and Acoustic Energy

Deriving the acoustic wave equation from continuity and Euler equations, Laplace’s adiabatic correction, and the thermodynamics of acoustic impedance.

Physics
Acoustics
Thermodynamics
Fluid Dynamics
Published

September 8, 2026

We experience sound as pitch, timbre, and volume. Mechanically, sound is a longitudinal pressure wave propagating through an elastic medium. But describing how a sound wave travels through air is not purely an exercise in classical mechanics—it is intimately bound to thermodynamics.

In fact, when Sir Isaac Newton attempted to calculate the speed of sound from first principles in the Principia, his prediction was off by more than 15%. Over a century passed before Pierre-Simon Laplace resolved Newton’s error by realizing that sound waves do not compress air isothermally, but adiabatically.

This article covers the first-principles derivation of the acoustic wave equation, Laplace’s thermodynamic correction, acoustic energy equipartition, and specific acoustic impedance.

1. The Fluid Mechanics of Sound

Consider a compressible, non-viscous fluid with equilibrium mass density \(\rho_0\) and background hydrostatic pressure \(p_0\). As a sound wave passes, it perturbs the density, pressure, and velocity fields: \[\rho(\mathbf{r}, t) = \rho_0 + \rho_1(\mathbf{r}, t), \qquad |\rho_1| \ll \rho_0\] \[p(\mathbf{r}, t) = p_0 + p_1(\mathbf{r}, t), \qquad |p_1| \ll p_0\] \[\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{r}, t) = \mathbf{0} + \mathbf{v}_1(\mathbf{r}, t), \qquad |\mathbf{v}_1| \ll c_s\]

Here, the subscript \(1\) denotes small first-order acoustic perturbations.

1. Conservation of Mass (Continuity Equation)

The exact continuity equation is: \[\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \boldsymbol{\nabla} \cdot (\rho \mathbf{v}) = 0\]

Substituting the perturbed quantities and neglecting second-order terms (\(\rho_1 \mathbf{v}_1 \approx 0\)): \[\boxed{\frac{\partial \rho_1}{\partial t} + \rho_0 \boldsymbol{\nabla} \cdot \mathbf{v}_1 = 0}\]

2. Conservation of Momentum (Euler’s Equation)

Newton’s second law for an inviscid fluid element is given by Euler’s equation: \[\rho \left[ \frac{\partial \mathbf{v}}{\partial t} + (\mathbf{v} \cdot \boldsymbol{\nabla})\mathbf{v} \right] = -\boldsymbol{\nabla} p\]

Linearizing by dropping the convective acceleration term \((\mathbf{v}_1 \cdot \boldsymbol{\nabla})\mathbf{v}_1 \sim \mathcal{O}(v_1^2)\): \[\boxed{\rho_0 \frac{\partial \mathbf{v}_1}{\partial t} = -\boldsymbol{\nabla} p_1}\]

Taking the divergence of the Euler equation and substituting the time derivative of the continuity equation: \[\boldsymbol{\nabla} \cdot \left( \rho_0 \frac{\partial \mathbf{v}_1}{\partial t} \right) = \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \left( \rho_0 \boldsymbol{\nabla} \cdot \mathbf{v}_1 \right) = -\frac{\partial^2 \rho_1}{\partial t^2}\] \[-\nabla^2 p_1 = -\frac{\partial^2 \rho_1}{\partial t^2} \implies \boxed{\nabla^2 p_1 = \frac{\partial^2 \rho_1}{\partial t^2}}\]

We now have a relationship between pressure \(p_1\) and density \(\rho_1\). To close the system and obtain a self-contained wave equation, we need a thermodynamic equation of state.

2. The Thermodynamics: Newton’s Error vs. Laplace’s Correction

We must relate acoustic pressure perturbations to density variations: \[p_1 = \left( \frac{\partial p}{\partial \rho} \right) \rho_1\]

Newton’s Flawed Assumption: Isothermal Compression

Newton assumed that heat flows instantaneously between compressed (warmer) regions and rarefied (cooler) regions of the wave, maintaining a strictly constant temperature (\(T = \text{const}\)).

For an ideal gas undergoing isothermal compression: \[p = \rho \frac{R T}{M} \implies \left( \frac{\partial p}{\partial \rho} \right)_T = \frac{p_0}{\rho_0}\]

Newton’s predicted speed of sound was: \[c_{\text{Newton}} = \sqrt{\frac{p_0}{\rho_0}}\]

For dry air at standard temperature and pressure (\(p_0 = 101,325\text{ Pa}, \rho_0 = 1.293\text{ kg/m}^3\)): \[c_{\text{Newton}} = \sqrt{\frac{101,325}{1.293}} \approx \mathbf{280 \text{ m/s}}\]

However, experimental measurements consistently yielded \(\approx 330 - 340\text{ m/s}\). Newton’s theoretical value was off by over 15%—an enormous discrepancy that troubled physicists for over a century.

Laplace’s Resolution: The Adiabatic / Isentropic Process (1816)

Pierre-Simon Laplace identified Newton’s mistake: sound oscillations occur far too rapidly for heat conduction to take place.

The characteristic thermal diffusion time across a sound half-wavelength \(\lambda/2\) is: \[\tau_{\text{diffusion}} \sim \frac{(\lambda/2)^2}{\alpha_{\text{thermal}}}\] where \(\alpha_{\text{thermal}} \approx 2 \times 10^{-5}\text{ m}^2/\text{s}\) is the thermal diffusivity of air. For audible frequencies (\(100\text{ Hz} - 10\text{ kHz}\)), \(\tau_{\text{diffusion}}\) is on the order of seconds, whereas the acoustic wave cycle period is \(T \sim 10^{-4} - 10^{-2}\text{ seconds}\).

Because \(\tau_{\text{period}} \ll \tau_{\text{diffusion}}\), no significant heat transfer occurs between adjacent wave crests and troughs. The compression and expansion cycles are strictly adiabatic and reversible—in other words, isentropic (\(s = \text{constant}\)).

For an ideal gas undergoing an isentropic process: \[p V^\gamma = \text{const} \iff p \rho^{-\gamma} = \text{const}\] where \(\gamma = C_p / C_v\) is the adiabatic index (heat capacity ratio). For diatomic gases like air (\(\text{N}_2, \text{O}_2\)), \(\gamma \approx 1.40\).

Differentiating isentropically: \[\left( \frac{\partial p}{\partial \rho} \right)_s = \gamma \frac{p}{\rho} = \gamma \frac{R T}{M}\]

Thus, Laplace’s adiabatic speed of sound is: \[\boxed{c_s = \sqrt{\left( \frac{\partial p}{\partial \rho} \right)_s} = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma p_0}{\rho_0}} = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma R T}{M}}}\]

Substituting \(\gamma = 1.40\) into air at \(20^\circ\text{C}\) (\(T = 293.15\text{ K}\)): \[c_s = \sqrt{1.40 \times \frac{101,325}{1.204}} \approx \mathbf{343.2 \text{ m/s}}\]

Laplace’s thermodynamic correction matches physical experiments with stunning accuracy.

3. The 3D Acoustic Wave Equation

Using the adiabatic relation \(p_1 = c_s^2 \rho_1\), we substitute \(\rho_1 = p_1 / c_s^2\) into our fluid equation \(\nabla^2 p_1 = \frac{\partial^2 \rho_1}{\partial t^2}\):

\[\boxed{\nabla^2 p_1 - \frac{1}{c_s^2} \frac{\partial^2 p_1}{\partial t^2} = 0}\]

Because fluid velocity and velocity potential \(\Phi\) (\(\mathbf{v}_1 = -\boldsymbol{\nabla}\Phi\)) satisfy the same linear relations, the density, velocity, and displacement fields all obey the exact same homogeneous wave equation.

1D Plane Wave Solution

For a plane wave propagating in the \(+x\) direction: \[p_1(x, t) = P_0 \cos(kx - \omega t)\] where \(\omega = c_s k\).

Using Euler’s linearized momentum equation \(\rho_0 \frac{\partial v_1}{\partial t} = -\frac{\partial p_1}{\partial x} = k P_0 \sin(kx - \omega t)\): \[v_1(x, t) = \frac{k}{\omega \rho_0} P_0 \cos(kx - \omega t) = \frac{P_0}{\rho_0 c_s} \cos(kx - \omega t)\]

Crucial Property: In an acoustic traveling plane wave, the pressure perturbation \(p_1\) and particle velocity \(v_1\) are precisely in phase!

4. Acoustic Energy, Intensity, and Impedance

Energy Equipartition

Acoustic energy consists of two forms: 1. Kinetic Energy Density (\(u_k\)): The macroscopic kinetic energy of the vibrating fluid parcel: \[u_k = \frac{1}{2} \rho_0 v_1^2\] 2. Potential Energy Density (\(u_p\)): The elastic thermodynamic energy stored in compressing the fluid parcel: \[u_p = \frac{p_1^2}{2 \rho_0 c_s^2} = \frac{p_1^2}{2 \gamma p_0}\]

For a traveling plane wave where \(v_1 = p_1 / (\rho_0 c_s)\): \[u_k = \frac{1}{2} \rho_0 \left( \frac{p_1}{\rho_0 c_s} \right)^2 = \frac{p_1^2}{2 \rho_0 c_s^2} = u_p\]

Acoustic waves exhibit exact instantaneous equipartition of energy: \[\boxed{u_{\text{total}} = u_k + u_p = \frac{p_1^2}{\rho_0 c_s^2} = \rho_0 v_1^2}\]

Time-averaging over a wave period: \[\langle u \rangle = \frac{P_0^2}{2 \rho_0 c_s^2} = \frac{p_{\text{rms}}^2}{\rho_0 c_s^2}\]

Specific Acoustic Impedance (\(Z_0\))

The ratio of acoustic pressure to particle velocity is defined as the specific acoustic impedance: \[\boxed{Z_0 = \frac{p_1}{v_1} = \rho_0 c_s}\]

Impedance measures the resistance of a medium to being vibrated by a given acoustic pressure: * Air (\(20^\circ\text{C}\)): \(Z_{\text{air}} = (1.204\text{ kg/m}^3)(343\text{ m/s}) \approx \mathbf{413 \text{ Pa}\cdot\text{s/m}}\) (Rayls). * Water (\(20^\circ\text{C}\)): \(Z_{\text{water}} = (1,000\text{ kg/m}^3)(1,482\text{ m/s}) \approx \mathbf{1.48 \times 10^6 \text{ Rayls}}\).

The Pressure Reflection Coefficient

When sound encounters an interface between medium 1 and medium 2 at normal incidence: \[R = \frac{Z_2 - Z_1}{Z_2 + Z_1}\] The power reflection coefficient is \(\mathcal{R} = R^2\): \[\mathcal{R} = \left( \frac{Z_{\text{water}} - Z_{\text{air}}}{Z_{\text{water}} + Z_{\text{air}}} \right)^2 \approx \left( \frac{1.48 \times 10^6 - 413}{1.48 \times 10^6 + 413} \right)^2 \approx \mathbf{0.9989} = \mathbf{99.89\%}\]

Because the acoustic impedance of water is over 3,500 times larger than air, 99.9% of sound energy is reflected at the air-water boundary. This explains why underwater swimmers cannot hear people talking on the pool deck, and why medical ultrasound probes require acoustic impedance-matching gel to eliminate the air gap against human skin.

Acoustic Intensity and the Decibel Scale

The instantaneous energy flux vector is \(\mathbf{I} = p_1 \mathbf{v}_1\). For a plane wave, the time-averaged acoustic intensity is: \[I = \langle p_1 v_1 \rangle = \frac{p_{\text{rms}}^2}{\rho_0 c_s} = \frac{P_0^2}{2 \rho_0 c_s} \quad [\text{W/m}^2]\]

Because the human ear perceives loudness logarithmically (Weber-Fechner law), sound intensity is quantified on the Sound Pressure Level (SPL) decibel scale: \[L_p = 20 \log_{10} \left( \frac{p_{\text{rms}}}{p_{\text{ref}}} \right) = 10 \log_{10} \left( \frac{I}{I_{\text{ref}}} \right) \quad [\text{dB}]\] where the reference threshold of human hearing at \(1\text{ kHz}\) is: \[p_{\text{ref}} = 20 \ \mu\text{Pa} = 2 \times 10^{-5}\text{ Pa}, \qquad I_{\text{ref}} = 10^{-12}\text{ W/m}^2\]

  • \(0\text{ dB}\): Threshold of human hearing (\(p_{\text{rms}} = 20\ \mu\text{Pa}\)).
  • \(60\text{ dB}\): Normal conversation (\(p_{\text{rms}} = 0.02\text{ Pa}\)).
  • \(120\text{ dB}\): Rock concert / siren (\(p_{\text{rms}} = 20\text{ Pa}\)).
  • \(140\text{ dB}\): Threshold of pain / jet engine take-off (\(p_{\text{rms}} = 200\text{ Pa}\)).

Notice that atmospheric pressure is \(101,325\text{ Pa}\). Even a deafening \(120\text{ dB}\) sound wave represents a microscopic pressure fluctuation of only \(\Delta p / p_0 \approx 20 / 101,325 \approx 0.02\%\)! Linearized acoustic theory is remarkably accurate across virtually all natural sound volumes.

Code 
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

x = np.linspace(0, 4, 1000)
wavelength = 2.0
k = 2 * np.pi / wavelength

# Normalized amplitudes for plane wave
p1 = np.cos(k * x)
v1 = np.cos(k * x)
u_kin = 0.5 * v1**2
u_pot = 0.5 * p1**2
u_tot = u_kin + u_pot

fig, (ax1, ax2) = plt.subplots(2, 1, figsize=(10, 6), sharex=True)

# Pressure and Particle Velocity
ax1.plot(x, p1, color='#1f77b4', lw=2.2, label=r'Acoustic Pressure $p_1(x) / P_0$')
ax1.plot(x, v1, color='#2ca02c', lw=2, linestyle='--', label=r'Particle Velocity $v_1(x) / v_0$ (In Phase)')
ax1.axhline(0, color='gray', linestyle=':', alpha=0.6)
ax1.set_ylabel('Normalized Amplitude', fontsize=10, fontweight='bold')
ax1.set_title('Acoustic Traveling Wave: Pressure and Velocity Phase Coherence', fontsize=12, fontweight='bold')
ax1.grid(True, linestyle=':', alpha=0.5)
ax1.legend(loc='upper right', frameon=True, fontsize=9)

# Energy Densities
ax2.plot(x, u_tot, color='#e74c3c', lw=2.5, label=r'Total Energy Density $u(x)$')
ax2.plot(x, u_pot, color='#3498db', lw=1.5, linestyle=':', label=r'Potential Energy $u_p(x)$')
ax2.plot(x, u_kin, color='#2ecc71', lw=1.5, linestyle=':', label=r'Kinetic Energy $u_k(x)$')
ax2.axhline(0.5, color='#d35400', linestyle='--', alpha=0.7, label=r'Time-Average $\langle u \rangle = 0.5$')
ax2.set_xlabel('Position $x$ [wavelengths]', fontsize=10, fontweight='bold')
ax2.set_ylabel('Energy Density', fontsize=10, fontweight='bold')
ax2.set_title('Acoustic Energy Density Distribution', fontsize=12, fontweight='bold')
ax2.grid(True, linestyle=':', alpha=0.5)
ax2.legend(loc='upper right', frameon=True, fontsize=9)

plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

Summary

  1. Continuity + Momentum = Sound: Linearizing mass conservation (\(\partial_t \rho_1 = -\rho_0 \boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf{v}_1\)) and Euler’s momentum equation (\(\rho_0 \partial_t \mathbf{v}_1 = -\boldsymbol{\nabla} p_1\)) yields \(\nabla^2 p_1 = \partial_t^2 \rho_1\).
  2. Laplace’s Adiabatic Law: Sound waves oscillate too fast for thermal diffusion to occur (\(\tau_{\text{diff}} \gg \tau_{\text{period}}\)). The compression is isentropic (\(p\rho^{-\gamma} = \text{const}\)), solving Newton’s 15% error and yielding \(c_s = \sqrt{\gamma p_0 / \rho_0}\).
  3. Acoustic Equipartition: Kinetic energy density (\(u_k = \frac{1}{2}\rho_0 v_1^2\)) and elastic potential energy density (\(u_p = \frac{p_1^2}{2\rho_0 c_s^2}\)) are identically equal in traveling plane waves.
  4. Impedance Governs Boundaries: The characteristic impedance \(Z_0 = \rho_0 c_s\) dictates transmission and reflection. The vast mismatch between air (\(413\text{ Rayls}\)) and water (\(1.48 \times 10^6\text{ Rayls}\)) causes 99.9% reflection at water surfaces.
