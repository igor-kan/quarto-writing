On Learning Physics, Math, and Statistics: Philosophies and Anecdotes

Learning advanced disciplines like physics, mathematics, and statistics is often a daunting endeavor that forces us to examine how we learn. Is it about memorizing formulas and derivations? Or is it about building an intuitive framework and knowing where to look things up?

Historically, brilliant minds have held differing opinions on the best approach to acquiring knowledge. Here, we’ll explore some famous anecdotes and techniques that shed light on how to tackle these challenging subjects.

Einstein vs. Edison: To Memorize or Not to Memorize?

A famous anecdote from 1921 perfectly captures the debate between rote memorization and conceptual understanding. During Albert Einstein’s first visit to the United States, he was presented with a job-screening questionnaire created by the American inventor Thomas Edison. Edison used this test to assess the factual knowledge of prospective employees.

One of the questions was simple: “What is the speed of sound?”

Einstein declined to answer, famously stating:

“I don’t know. I don’t burden my memory with such facts that I can easily find in any textbook.”

This quote (often paraphrased as “never memorize what you can look up in a book”) highlights a fundamental difference in philosophy. Einstein believed the value of education lies not in accumulating miscellaneous facts, but in the training of the mind to think.

In modern terms, you might say, “I’m sorry I did not memorize the derivation, but I know exactly which shelf I can find the book it’s in.” (Or today, “I can find it on Wikipedia!”).

Edison, on the other hand, valued encyclopedic, practical knowledge and felt that individuals should have immediate mental access to a wide array of facts. While both men were undeniable geniuses, their approaches to learning were fundamentally opposed. When learning complex math or physics, Einstein’s approach is often more sustainable: focus on the why and the how, and leave the trivial facts to the reference books.

The Legend of Ed Witten and Jackson’s Electrodynamics

While some advocate for slow, methodical understanding, there are outliers who defy conventional learning timelines. The story of Edward Witten, one of the most prominent theoretical physicists of our time, is a legendary example.

Witten originally graduated as a journalism and history major. When he later decided to pivot to theoretical physics, the story goes that a professor—perhaps to test his resolve or discourage him—handed him a copy of J.D. Jackson’s Classical Electrodynamics.

Jackson is a notoriously difficult, math-heavy graduate-level textbook. It assumes a high level of mathematical maturity in vector calculus, PDEs, and special functions. A typical physics graduate student might spend a year agonizing over its dense problems and skipped algebraic steps.

According to the anecdote, Witten returned in just a few weeks having mastered the material, prompting the stunned professor to ask, “What’s next?”

While Witten’s ability to absorb advanced mathematics and entire graduate-level programs in mere weeks is an extreme outlier, it underscores the power of intense focus and perhaps an innate structural understanding of mathematics that allowed him to bypass the traditional “grind.” For the rest of us, learning the math for Jackson in weeks is impossible without a solid foundation in vector calculus and mathematical methods beforehand.

The Feynman Technique: True Understanding

If we can’t all be Ed Witten, how do we systematically build the kind of understanding Einstein advocated for? Enter the Feynman Technique, named after the Nobel Prize-winning physicist Richard Feynman, known as the “Great Explainer.”

Feynman’s approach to learning was rooted in simplicity and clarity. The technique consists of four steps:

  1. Choose a Concept: Pick the topic you want to understand (e.g., the Boltzmann distribution or Stokes’ Theorem) and write it at the top of a blank page.
  2. Teach it to a Child: Explain the concept in your own words as if you were teaching it to someone without any background knowledge. Avoid jargon. If you must use a complex term, define it simply.
  3. Identify Gaps: When you get stuck, realize you can’t explain something simply, or have to resort to complex jargon, you’ve found a gap in your knowledge. Go back to the source material (textbooks, notes) and review until you can explain it.
  4. Simplify and Refine: Review your explanation. Are there still confusing parts? Use analogies and simple language to smooth them out.

Feynman believed that if you couldn’t explain something simply, you didn’t truly understand it. This method forces you away from the illusion of competence that comes from just reading a textbook, and pushes you toward genuine mastery.

Conclusion

Whether you lean toward Edison’s desire for immediate recall, or Einstein’s preference for structural thinking, learning physics and math requires a personal synthesis. We may not have Ed Witten’s speed, but by using tools like the Feynman technique and focusing on the underlying concepts rather than rote memorization, the steepest learning curves become manageable.

Ultimately, knowing how to find and derive the answer is often far more powerful than just having the answer memorized.
