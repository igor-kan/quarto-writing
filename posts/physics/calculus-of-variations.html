The Calculus of Variations: From Brachistochrones to the Principle of Least Action

A rigorous mathematical exploration of functionals, the Euler-Lagrange equation, the Beltrami identity, isoperimetric constraints, and classical variational problems.

The Calculus of Variations is the mathematical framework for finding extrema (maxima, minima, or stationary points) of functionals. While differential calculus deals with functions that map numbers to numbers (\(f: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}\)), the calculus of variations deals with functionals that map entire curves, paths, or fields to numbers (\(J: \mathcal{C}^2 \to \mathbb{R}\)).

A typical one-dimensional variational problem seeks a curve \(y(x) \in \mathcal{C}^2[x_1, x_2]\) with fixed boundary conditions \(y(x_1) = y_1\) and \(y(x_2) = y_2\) that renders the action functional \(J[y]\) stationary:

\[ J[y] = \int_{x_1}^{x_2} L(x, y(x), y'(x)) \, dx \]

where \(L(x, y, y')\) is the Lagrangian of the system.

1. Rigorous Derivation of the Euler-Lagrange Equation

Consider an optimal path \(y_0(x)\) that extremizes \(J[y]\). We construct a family of perturbed comparison paths:

\[ y(x, \epsilon) = y_0(x) + \epsilon \eta(x) \]

where \(\epsilon \in \mathbb{R}\) is a small parameter, and \(\eta(x) \in \mathcal{C}^2[x_1, x_2]\) is an arbitrary test function satisfying Dirichlet boundary conditions:

\[ \eta(x_1) = 0, \quad \eta(x_2) = 0 \]

The functional becomes a standard single-variable function of \(\epsilon\):

\[ \Phi(\epsilon) = J[y_0 + \epsilon \eta] = \int_{x_1}^{x_2} L(x, y_0 + \epsilon \eta, y_0' + \epsilon \eta') \, dx \]

For \(y_0(x)\) to be a stationary curve, the first variation \(\delta J\) must vanish, which means the derivative of \(\Phi(\epsilon)\) evaluated at \(\epsilon = 0\) must be zero for any admissible \(\eta(x)\):

\[ \left. \frac{d\Phi}{d\epsilon} \right|_{\epsilon=0} = 0 \]

Differentiating inside the integral using the multivariable chain rule:

\[ \left. \frac{d\Phi}{d\epsilon} \right|_{\epsilon=0} = \int_{x_1}^{x_2} \left[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial y} \eta(x) + \frac{\partial L}{\partial y'} \eta'(x) \right] dx = 0 \]

We integrate the second term by parts to factor out \(\eta(x)\):

\[ \int_{x_1}^{x_2} \frac{\partial L}{\partial y'} \eta'(x) \, dx = \left[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial y'} \eta(x) \right]_{x_1}^{x_2} - \int_{x_1}^{x_2} \frac{d}{dx} \left( \frac{\partial L}{\partial y'} \right) \eta(x) \, dx \]

Because \(\eta(x_1) = \eta(x_2) = 0\), the boundary term vanishes identically. Combining the integrals:

\[ \int_{x_1}^{x_2} \left[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial y} - \frac{d}{dx} \left( \frac{\partial L}{\partial y'} \right) \right] \eta(x) \, dx = 0 \]

The Fundamental Lemma of the Calculus of Variations (du Bois-Reymond)

Lemma: If a continuous function \(M(x)\) satisfies \(\int_{x_1}^{x_2} M(x) \eta(x) \, dx = 0\) for all twice continuously differentiable functions \(\eta(x)\) with \(\eta(x_1) = \eta(x_2) = 0\), then \(M(x) \equiv 0\) everywhere on \([x_1, x_2]\).

Applying this lemma immediately yields the Euler-Lagrange Equation:

\[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial y} - \frac{d}{dx} \left( \frac{\partial L}{\partial y'} \right) = 0 \]

2. First Integrals: The Beltrami Identity

When the Lagrangian does not depend explicitly on the independent variable \(x\) (i.e., \(\partial L / \partial x = 0\)), the total derivative with respect to \(x\) is:

\[ \frac{dL}{dx} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial L}{\partial y} y' + \frac{\partial L}{\partial y'} y'' = 0 + \frac{\partial L}{\partial y} y' + \frac{\partial L}{\partial y'} y'' \]

Substituting the Euler-Lagrange equation \(\frac{\partial L}{\partial y} = \frac{d}{dx} \left( \frac{\partial L}{\partial y'} \right)\):

\[ \frac{dL}{dx} = \left[ \frac{d}{dx} \left( \frac{\partial L}{\partial y'} \right) \right] y' + \frac{\partial L}{\partial y'} y'' = \frac{d}{dx} \left( y' \frac{\partial L}{\partial y'} \right) \]

Rearranging yields:

\[ \frac{d}{dx} \left( L - y' \frac{\partial L}{\partial y'} \right) = 0 \]

Integrating directly with respect to \(x\) provides the Beltrami Identity (the variational analogue of energy conservation):

\[ L - y' \frac{\partial L}{\partial y'} = C \]

where \(C\) is a constant of integration. This reduces a second-order nonlinear differential equation to a first-order separable differential equation!

3. The Brachistochrone Problem

In June 1696, Johann Bernoulli challenged European mathematicians to solve the Brachistochrone (Greek: \(\beta\rho\acute{\alpha}\chi\iota\sigma\tau o\varsigma\) shortest, \(\chi\rho\acute{o}\nu o\varsigma\) time):

Given two points \(A\) and \(B\) in a vertical plane, determine the path along which a movable particle will descend by gravity from \(A\) to \(B\) in the shortest possible time, starting from rest without friction.

Derivation

Orient the coordinate system such that point \(A\) is at the origin \((0, 0)\) and the vertical axis \(y\) points downward in the direction of gravitational acceleration \(g\).

By conservation of energy: \[ \frac{1}{2} m v^2 = m g y \implies v = \sqrt{2gy} \]

The differential arc length is \(ds = \sqrt{dx^2 + dy^2} = \sqrt{1 + (y')^2} \, dx\). The total transit time is:

\[ T[y] = \int_{A}^{B} \frac{ds}{v} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2g}} \int_{0}^{x_B} \sqrt{\frac{1 + (y')^2}{y}} \, dx \]

The Lagrangian is \(L(y, y') = \sqrt{\frac{1 + (y')^2}{y}}\). Because \(\partial L / \partial x = 0\), we invoke the Beltrami identity:

\[ L - y' \frac{\partial L}{\partial y'} = \sqrt{\frac{1 + (y')^2}{y}} - y' \cdot \frac{y'}{\sqrt{y(1 + (y')^2)}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{y(1 + (y')^2)}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2R}} \]

where \(2R\) is a positive constant. Inverting and squaring both sides:

\[ y [1 + (y')^2] = 2R \implies y' = \sqrt{\frac{2R - y}{y}} \]

To solve this nonlinear first-order ODE, apply the trigonometric substitution: \[ y = R(1 - \cos\theta) = 2R \sin^2\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right) \]

Differentiating with respect to the parameter \(\theta\): \[ dy = R \sin\theta \, d\theta \]

From the differential equation: \[ dx = \frac{dy}{y'} = \frac{R \sin\theta \, d\theta}{\sqrt{\frac{2R - R(1 - \cos\theta)}{R(1 - \cos\theta)}}} = \frac{R \sin\theta \, d\theta}{\cot(\theta/2)} = \frac{2R \sin(\theta/2)\cos(\theta/2) \, d\theta}{\cos(\theta/2)/\sin(\theta/2)} = 2R \sin^2\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right) d\theta \]

Using the identity \(2\sin^2(\theta/2) = 1 - \cos\theta\): \[ dx = R(1 - \cos\theta) \, d\theta \]

Integrating with the initial condition \(x(0) = 0\): \[ x(\theta) = R(\theta - \sin\theta) \] \[ y(\theta) = R(1 - \cos\theta) \]

This is the exact parametric equation of a Cycloid—the trajectory traced out by a fixed point on the rim of a wheel of radius \(R\) rolling along a flat horizontal ceiling!

4. Programmatic Comparison: Cycloid vs. Line vs. Parabola

Below is an explicit numerical simulation verifying that the cycloid minimizes descent time compared to a straight line and a parabolic curve connecting the same two points.

Code 
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy.integrate import quad
from scipy.optimize import root_scalar

g = 9.81
# Target coordinates
x_target, y_target = 3.0, 1.5

# 1. Solve for Cycloid parameters R and theta_target
def cycloid_target(theta):
    # Ratio y/x = (1 - cos(theta)) / (theta - sin(theta))
    return (1 - np.cos(theta)) / (theta - np.sin(theta)) - (y_target / x_target)

res = root_scalar(cycloid_target, bracket=[0.1, 2*np.pi])
theta_max = res.root
R = y_target / (1 - np.cos(theta_max))

theta = np.linspace(0.001, theta_max, 300)
x_cyc = R * (theta - np.sin(theta))
y_cyc = R * (1 - np.cos(theta))

# Time for Cycloid: ds = 2R sin(theta/2) dtheta, v = sqrt(2gy) = sqrt(4gR) sin(theta/2)
# dt = ds / v = sqrt(R/g) dtheta -> T = theta_max * sqrt(R/g)
T_cyc = theta_max * np.sqrt(R / g)

# 2. Straight line: y(x) = (y_target / x_target) * x
m = y_target / x_target
# v(x) = sqrt(2g m x), ds = sqrt(1 + m^2) dx
# T = sqrt(1+m^2)/sqrt(2g m) * int_0^x_target x^(-1/2) dx = sqrt(2 * (1+m^2) * x_target / (g * m))
T_line = np.sqrt(2 * (1 + m**2) * x_target / (g * m))

# 3. Parabola: y(x) = y_target * sqrt(x / x_target)
# Numerical quadrature for parabola
def dt_parabola(x):
    if x == 0: return 0
    y = y_target * np.sqrt(x / x_target)
    y_prime = y_target / (2 * np.sqrt(x * x_target))
    ds = np.sqrt(1 + y_prime**2)
    v = np.sqrt(2 * g * y)
    return ds / v

T_parab, _ = quad(dt_parabola, 1e-6, x_target)

# Plotting
x_dense = np.linspace(0, x_target, 300)
y_line = m * x_dense
y_parab = y_target * np.sqrt(x_dense / x_target)

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(9, 5))
ax.plot(x_cyc, y_cyc, label=f'Brachistochrone Cycloid (T = {T_cyc:.3f} s)', color='#1f77b4', lw=2.5)
ax.plot(x_dense, y_parab, label=f'Square-root Curve (T = {T_parab:.3f} s)', color='#2ca02c', lw=2, linestyle='-.')
ax.plot(x_dense, y_line, label=f'Straight Line (T = {T_line:.3f} s)', color='#d62728', lw=2, linestyle='--')

ax.scatter([0, x_target], [0, y_target], color='black', s=60, zorder=5)
ax.text(0, -0.08, 'Start (0, 0)', ha='center', fontweight='bold')
ax.text(x_target, y_target + 0.12, f'Finish ({x_target}, {y_target})', ha='center', fontweight='bold')

ax.invert_yaxis()
ax.set_xlabel('Horizontal Distance [m]')
ax.set_ylabel('Vertical Drop $y$ [m]')
ax.set_title('Descent Time Optimization Under Gravity', fontsize=13, fontweight='bold')
ax.grid(True, linestyle=':', alpha=0.7)
ax.legend(loc='lower left', frameon=True)
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

5. The Tautochrone Problem and Abel’s Integral Equation

The Tautochrone (Greek: \(\tau\alpha\upsilon\tau\acute{o}\) the same, \(\chi\rho\acute{o}\nu o\varsigma\) time) asks for a curve such that the time taken by an object sliding down without friction to its lowest point is independent of its starting elevation \(y_0\).

Let \(y_0\) be the release height. At any subsequent height \(y \le y_0\), velocity is \(v = \sqrt{2g(y_0 - y)}\). The travel time to the bottom (\(y=0\)) is given by the integral:

\[ T(y_0) = \int_{0}^{y_0} \frac{ds/dy}{\sqrt{2g(y_0 - y)}} \, dy \]

Let \(f(y) = \frac{ds}{dy}\). For \(T(y_0) = T_0 = \text{constant}\), this is a singular Volterra integral equation of the first kind (Abel’s Integral Equation):

\[ \int_{0}^{y_0} \frac{f(y)}{\sqrt{y_0 - y}} \, dy = T_0 \sqrt{2g} \]

Multiplying both sides by \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\xi - y_0}}\) and integrating with respect to \(y_0\) from \(0\) to \(\xi\):

\[ \int_{0}^{\xi} \frac{1}{\sqrt{\xi - y_0}} \left( \int_{0}^{y_0} \frac{f(y)}{\sqrt{y_0 - y}} \, dy \right) dy_0 = T_0 \sqrt{2g} \int_{0}^{\xi} \frac{dy_0}{\sqrt{\xi - y_0}} \]

Reversing the order of integration on the left side:

\[ \int_{0}^{\xi} f(y) \left( \int_{y}^{\xi} \frac{dy_0}{\sqrt{(\xi - y_0)(y_0 - y)}} \right) dy = 2 T_0 \sqrt{2g \xi} \]

The inner integral evaluates to the standard constant \(\pi\) via the beta function \(B(1/2, 1/2)\):

\[ \pi \int_{0}^{\xi} f(y) \, dy = 2 T_0 \sqrt{2g \xi} \]

Differentiating with respect to \(\xi\):

\[ \pi f(\xi) = \frac{T_0 \sqrt{2g}}{\sqrt{\xi}} \implies \frac{ds}{dy} = \sqrt{\frac{2R}{y}} \quad \text{where } R = \frac{g T_0^2}{\pi^2} \]

Since \((ds/dy)^2 = 1 + (dx/dy)^2 = 2R/y\), we obtain:

\[ \frac{dx}{dy} = \sqrt{\frac{2R - y}{y}} \]

This is precisely the differential equation of the Cycloid! Hence, the cycloid is both the Brachistochrone (fastest path) and the Tautochrone (equal-time path).

6. Constrained Variations: The Isoperimetric Problem

In many physical systems, an extremum is sought subject to an integral constraint \(\int_{x_1}^{x_2} G(x, y, y') \, dx = C\).

Using Lagrange multipliers, we construct the augmented Lagrangian:

\[ L^*(x, y, y', \lambda) = L(x, y, y') + \lambda G(x, y, y') \]

The stationarity condition becomes:

\[ \frac{\partial L^*}{\partial y} - \frac{d}{dx}\left( \frac{\partial L^*}{\partial y'} \right) = 0 \]

Queen Dido’s Classical Problem

Find the closed curve of fixed perimeter \(P\) that encloses the maximum area \(A\). In Cartesian coordinates, Green’s Theorem expresses the area as \(A = \frac{1}{2} \oint (x dy - y dx)\), and the perimeter constraint is \(P = \oint \sqrt{\dot{x}^2 + \dot{y}^2} \, dt\). Solving the variational equations shows that the curvature \(\kappa\) must be constant everywhere along the curve, rigorously proving that the circle is the unique maximal geometry.

7. Generalization to Classical Mechanics and Field Theory

Hamilton’s Principle of Stationary Action

In analytical mechanics, a system of \(N\) degrees of freedom is specified by generalized coordinates \(q_i(t)\) (\(i = 1, \dots, N\)). The physical path traversed by the system between times \(t_1\) and \(t_2\) makes the action \(S\) stationary:

\[ \delta S = \delta \int_{t_1}^{t_2} L(q_i, \dot{q}_i, t) \, dt = 0 \]

This yields the system of coupled Lagrange equations:

\[ \frac{d}{dt}\left( \frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{q}_i} \right) - \frac{\partial L}{\partial q_i} = 0, \quad i = 1, \dots, N \]

Noether’s Theorem

Every continuous variational symmetry of the action functional implies an exact conservation law: * Time invariance (\(\partial L / \partial t = 0\)) \(\implies\) Conservation of Energy (\(H = \sum \dot{q}_i p_i - L\)). * Spatial translation invariance (\(\partial L / \partial q_i = 0\)) \(\implies\) Conservation of Linear Momentum (\(p_i = \partial L / \partial \dot{q}_i\)). * Rotational invariance \(\implies\) Conservation of Angular Momentum.

Relativistic Field Theory

In continuum field theory, coordinates become continuous spacetime variables \(x^\mu = (ct, \mathbf{x})\), and the dynamical variables are fields \(\phi^a(x^\mu)\). The action is an integral over spacetime volume:

\[ S[\phi] = \int \mathcal{L}\left( \phi^a, \partial_\mu \phi^a \right) d^4 x \]

The Euler-Lagrange field equations take the covariant 4-divergence form:

\[ \partial_\mu \left( \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial (\partial_\mu \phi^a)} \right) - \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \phi^a} = 0 \]

From Maxwell’s equations in electrodynamics to the Dirac equation in quantum field theory and the Einstein field equations of general relativity, modern physics is written in the language of the calculus of variations.
