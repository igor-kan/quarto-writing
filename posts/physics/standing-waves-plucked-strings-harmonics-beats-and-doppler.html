A guitar string, a violin string, and a piano string are all solutions to the same partial differential equation under different boundary conditions and initial shapes. The modes they support are the harmonics, the reason instruments sound different is the mixture of those harmonics, the shimmer of two out-of-tune notes is beats, and the changing pitch of a passing siren is the Doppler effect.

This article develops the physics from the wave equation to the musical and acoustic consequences.

1 Waves on a String

A stretched string of tension \(T\) and linear density \(\mu\) (mass per length) obeys the one-dimensional wave equation

\[ \frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial t^2}=v^2\frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial x^2}, \qquad v=\sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}}. \]

General solutions are superpositions of traveling waves \(f(x-vt)\) and \(g(x+vt)\). This is why the string’s pitch rises with tension (tuning) and falls with mass per unit length (thicker strings are lower). A reflection at a boundary inverts (fixed) or preserves (free) the wave, and the interference of incoming and reflected waves creates standing waves.

1.1 Standing Waves

Superposing two counter-propagating sinusoidal waves gives a standing wave: \[ y(x,t)=2A\sin(kx)\cos(\omega t),\qquad k=\frac{2\pi}{\lambda},\ \omega=2\pi f. \] The spatial pattern \(\sin(kx)\) is fixed; only the amplitude oscillates. Points of zero motion are nodes; points of maximum motion are antinodes. The distance between adjacent nodes is \(\lambda/2\).

2 Normal Modes and Boundary Conditions

The allowed modes are set by the boundary conditions.

2.1 Fixed at Both Ends

\(y(0,t)=y(L,t)=0\) requires \(\sin(kL)=0\), so \(k_n=n\pi/L\): \[ \lambda_n=\frac{2L}{n},\qquad f_n=\frac{nv}{2L}=n f_1,\qquad n=1,2,3,\dots \]

The fundamental is \(f_1=v/(2L)\) and every integer multiple is present: the full harmonic series.

2.2 Fixed at One End, Free at the Other

A free end is an antinode (\(\partial y/\partial x=0\)), giving \(\cos(kL)=0\), so \(k_n=(2n-1)\pi/(2L)\): \[ \lambda_n=\frac{4L}{2n-1},\qquad f_n=\frac{(2n-1)v}{4L},\qquad n=1,2,3,\dots \]

Only odd harmonics appear (a tube closed at one end, a string fixed at one end). The fundamental is \(v/(4L)\), half the fixed-fixed fundamental for the same length.

2.3 Free at Both Ends

\(\partial y/\partial x=0\) at both ends again gives \(k_n=n\pi/L\), so the frequencies are the same as fixed-fixed, \(f_n=nv/(2L)\), but the end points are antinodes (the mode shapes are cosines).

Boundary Allowed \(\lambda\) Frequencies Harmonics
fixed–fixed \(2L/n\) \(n v/(2L)\) all
fixed–free \(4L/(2n-1)\) \((2n-1)v/(4L)\) odd only
free–free \(2L/n\) \(n v/(2L)\) all

The mode shapes are \(\sin(n\pi x/L)\) (fixed-fixed), \(\sin((2n-1)\pi x/(2L))\) (fixed-free), and \(\cos(n\pi x/L)\) (free-free).

3 The Plucked String and Timbre

A pluck is an initial condition: at \(t=0\) the string has a triangular shape and zero velocity, and then evolves as a superposition of normal modes. For a pluck at position \(x_0\) with height \(h\), the initial shape’s Fourier sine series is \[ y(x,0)=\sum_{n\ge1} b_n\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}, \qquad b_n=\frac{2hL^2}{n^2\pi^2\,x_0(L-x_0)}\sin\frac{n\pi x_0}{L}. \]

Three consequences:

  • All modes oscillate at their own frequencies \(\omega_n=2\pi f_n\), so the sound is a sum of harmonics.
  • Pluck position selects harmonics. Plucking at \(L/n\) places a node of the \(n\)-th harmonic at the pluck point, suppressing it. A center pluck (\(x_0=L/2\)) excites only the odd harmonics; a pluck near the bridge excites many high harmonics, which is why it sounds brighter.
  • Timbre is the harmonic content. The same fundamental with different harmonic weights sounds like a guitar, a piano, or a violin.

The amplitude weights fall like \(1/n^2\) (a triangular shape is continuous but has a corner), so high harmonics are weak but present. Smoother initial shapes decay faster in \(n\); a sharp pluck (or an initial step) excites more high frequencies.

4 Harmonics, Intervals, and Tuning

The harmonic series is the physical basis of pitch relationships:

Harmonic Frequency ratio Musical interval
1 1:1 fundamental (tonic)
2 2:1 octave
3 3:1 octave + perfect fifth
4 4:1 two octaves
5 5:1 two octaves + major third
6 6:1 two octaves + fifth
7 7:1 (near) minor seventh

Just intonation tunes intervals to simple ratios (\(2:1\), \(3:2\), \(4:3\)); equal temperament divides the octave into 12 equal semitones of ratio \(2^{1/12}\), which slightly detunes every interval except the octave but lets every key sound acceptable. The discrepancy between just and tempered fifths is a few cents — enough to hear as beating (below).

5 Beats

When two tones of close frequencies \(f_1\) and \(f_2\) are sounded together, \[ \sin(2\pi f_1 t)+\sin(2\pi f_2 t) =2\cos\!\Bigl(2\pi\frac{f_1-f_2}{2}t\Bigr)\sin\!\Bigl(2\pi\frac{f_1+f_2}{2}t\Bigr). \] The result is a tone at the average frequency whose amplitude is modulated at the beat frequency \[ f_{\text{beat}}=|f_1-f_2|. \]

Beats are the basis of tuning by ear: as two strings approach unison, the beats slow and vanish. (The modulation envelope repeats at \(|f_1-f_2|\), though the amplitude is periodic at \(|f_1-f_2|/2\); the audible loudness pulsation is at \(|f_1-f_2|\).)

Beats also arise from nonlinearity and combination tones, and are used in radio heterodyning, in beating between laser modes, and in interferometry.

6 The Doppler Effect

When a source or observer moves, the observed frequency shifts.

Let \(v\) be the speed of sound, \(v_s\) the source speed, \(v_o\) the observer speed, and \(f_s\) the source frequency. With the standard sign convention (observer moving toward source, source moving toward observer increase the observed frequency):

\[ f_o=f_s\left(\frac{v \pm v_o}{v \mp v_s}\right). \]

  • Moving observer, stationary source: \(f_o=f_s\dfrac{v+v_o}{v}\) (approaching) or \(\dfrac{v-v_o}{v}\) (receding).
  • Moving source, stationary observer: \(f_o=f_s\dfrac{v}{v-v_s}\) (approaching) or \(\dfrac{v}{v+v_s}\) (receding) — the wavelength itself is compressed or stretched.

The shift is \(\Delta f\approx f_s v_s/v\) for slow motion. Applications: police radar (double shift), medical Doppler ultrasound, sonar, weather radar, and the redshift of receding galaxies. For speeds comparable to \(c\), the relativistic Doppler formula replaces the classical one: \[ f_o=f_s\sqrt{\frac{1-\beta}{1+\beta}}\quad(\text{receding}),\qquad \beta=v/c. \]

7.2 Fourier Synthesis of a Plucked String

Code 
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

L, x0, h = 1.0, 0.3, 1.0
x = np.linspace(0, L, 800)

def pluck_coeffs(n_max):
    n = np.arange(1, n_max + 1)
    b = (2 * h * L**2) / (n**2 * np.pi**2 * x0 * (L - x0)) * np.sin(n * np.pi * x0 / L)
    return n, b

def pluck_shape(x, n_max):
    n, b = pluck_coeffs(n_max)
    return sum(b[k] * np.sin((k+1) * np.pi * x / L) for k in range(len(n)))

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(16, 4.8))
axes[0].plot(x, pluck_shape(x, 500), "k--", lw=1, label="target (triangle)")
for n_max, col in [(3, "#95a5a6"), (7, "#2980b9"), (25, "#e74c3c")]:
    axes[0].plot(x, pluck_shape(x, n_max), color=col, lw=1, label=f"{n_max} harmonics")
axes[0].set_title(f"Plucked string, pluck at x0={x0}L")
axes[0].set_xlabel("x/L"); axes[0].set_ylabel("displacement"); axes[0].legend(fontsize=8); axes[0].grid(alpha=0.3)

n, b = pluck_coeffs(20)
axes[1].stem(n, np.abs(b), basefmt=" ")
axes[1].set_title(f"Harmonic amplitudes (pluck {x0}L)")
axes[1].set_xlabel("harmonic n"); axes[1].set_ylabel("|b_n|"); axes[1].grid(alpha=0.3)

n2 = np.arange(1, 21)
b_center = (2 * h * L**2) / (n2**2 * np.pi**2 * 0.5 * 0.5) * np.sin(n2 * np.pi / 2)

7.4 The Doppler Effect

Code 
import numpy as np

v = 343.0   # speed of sound in air, m/s

def doppler(fs, vs=0.0, vo=0.0, approach_source=True, approach_obs=True):
    num = v + (vo if approach_obs else -vo)
    den = v - (vs if approach_source else -vs)
    return fs * num / den

print("=== DOPPLER EFFECT (f_s = 700 Hz ambulance siren, v = 343 m/s) ===")
for vs in [0, 10, 20, 30]:
    f_ap = doppler(700, vs=vs, approach_source=True)
    f_re = doppler(700, vs=vs, approach_source=False)
    print(f"  source {vs:>2} m/s: approaching -> {f_ap:7.2f} Hz, receding -> {f_re:7.2f} Hz, "
          f"shift {f_ap-700:+.2f} Hz")

print("\n=== MOVING OBSERVER (source at 700 Hz) ===")
for vo in [0, 10, 20, 30]:
    print(f"  observer {vo:>2} m/s: approaching -> {doppler(700, vo=vo, approach_obs=True):7.2f} Hz, "
          f"receding -> {doppler(700, vo=vo, approach_obs=False):7.2f} Hz")

print("\n=== RELATIVISTIC DOPPLER (redshift) ===")
for beta in [0.01, 0.1, 0.5, 0.9]:
    f_ratio = np.sqrt((1 - beta) / (1 + beta))
    print(f"  v/c={beta}: f_obs/f_src (receding) = {f_ratio:.4f}, "
          f"shift z = {(1/f_ratio - 1):.4f}")

## Waves on a String

A stretched string of **tension** $T$ and **linear density** $\mu$ (mass per length) obeys the one-dimensional **wave equation**

$$
\frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial t^2}=v^2\frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial x^2},
\qquad
v=\sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}}.
$$

General solutions are superpositions of **traveling waves** $f(x-vt)$ and $g(x+vt)$. This is why the string's pitch rises with tension (tuning) and falls with mass per unit length (thicker strings are lower). A reflection at a boundary inverts (fixed) or preserves (free) the wave, and the interference of incoming and reflected waves creates **standing waves**.

### Standing Waves

Superposing two counter-propagating sinusoidal waves gives a **standing wave**:
$$
y(x,t)=2A\sin(kx)\cos(\omega t),\qquad k=\frac{2\pi}{\lambda},\ \omega=2\pi f.
$$
The spatial pattern $\sin(kx)$ is fixed; only the amplitude oscillates. Points of zero motion are **nodes**; points of maximum motion are **antinodes**. The distance between adjacent nodes is $\lambda/2$.

## Normal Modes and Boundary Conditions

The allowed modes are set by the **boundary conditions**.

### Fixed at Both Ends

$y(0,t)=y(L,t)=0$ requires $\sin(kL)=0$, so $k_n=n\pi/L$:
$$
\lambda_n=\frac{2L}{n},\qquad
f_n=\frac{nv}{2L}=n f_1,\qquad n=1,2,3,\dots
$$

The **fundamental** is $f_1=v/(2L)$ and every **integer multiple** is present: the full **harmonic series**.

### Fixed at One End, Free at the Other

A free end is an antinode ($\partial y/\partial x=0$), giving $\cos(kL)=0$, so $k_n=(2n-1)\pi/(2L)$:
$$
\lambda_n=\frac{4L}{2n-1},\qquad
f_n=\frac{(2n-1)v}{4L},\qquad n=1,2,3,\dots
$$

Only **odd harmonics** appear (a tube closed at one end, a string fixed at one end). The fundamental is $v/(4L)$, half the fixed-fixed fundamental for the same length.

### Free at Both Ends

$\partial y/\partial x=0$ at both ends again gives $k_n=n\pi/L$, so the frequencies are the same as fixed-fixed, $f_n=nv/(2L)$, but the end points are **antinodes** (the mode shapes are cosines).

| Boundary | Allowed $\lambda$ | Frequencies | Harmonics |
|---|---|---|---|
| fixed–fixed | $2L/n$ | $n v/(2L)$ | all |
| fixed–free | $4L/(2n-1)$ | $(2n-1)v/(4L)$ | odd only |
| free–free | $2L/n$ | $n v/(2L)$ | all |

The **mode shapes** are $\sin(n\pi x/L)$ (fixed-fixed), $\sin((2n-1)\pi x/(2L))$ (fixed-free), and $\cos(n\pi x/L)$ (free-free).

## The Plucked String and Timbre

A pluck is an **initial condition**: at $t=0$ the string has a triangular shape and zero velocity, and then evolves as a superposition of normal modes. For a pluck at position $x_0$ with height $h$, the initial shape's Fourier sine series is
$$
y(x,0)=\sum_{n\ge1} b_n\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L},
\qquad
b_n=\frac{2hL^2}{n^2\pi^2\,x_0(L-x_0)}\sin\frac{n\pi x_0}{L}.
$$

Three consequences:

- **All modes oscillate at their own frequencies** $\omega_n=2\pi f_n$, so the sound is a sum of harmonics.
- **Pluck position selects harmonics.** Plucking at $L/n$ places a node of the $n$-th harmonic at the pluck point, suppressing it. A center pluck ($x_0=L/2$) excites only the **odd** harmonics; a pluck near the bridge excites many high harmonics, which is why it sounds brighter.
- **Timbre** is the harmonic content. The same fundamental with different harmonic weights sounds like a guitar, a piano, or a violin.

The amplitude weights fall like $1/n^2$ (a triangular shape is continuous but has a corner), so high harmonics are weak but present. Smoother initial shapes decay faster in $n$; a sharp pluck (or an initial step) excites more high frequencies.

## Harmonics, Intervals, and Tuning

The harmonic series is the physical basis of pitch relationships:

| Harmonic | Frequency ratio | Musical interval |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1:1 | fundamental (tonic) |
| 2 | 2:1 | octave |
| 3 | 3:1 | octave + perfect fifth |
| 4 | 4:1 | two octaves |
| 5 | 5:1 | two octaves + major third |
| 6 | 6:1 | two octaves + fifth |
| 7 | 7:1 | (near) minor seventh |

**Just intonation** tunes intervals to simple ratios ($2:1$, $3:2$, $4:3$); **equal temperament** divides the octave into 12 equal semitones of ratio $2^{1/12}$, which slightly detunes every interval except the octave but lets every key sound acceptable. The discrepancy between just and tempered fifths is a few cents — enough to hear as **beating** (below).

---

## Beats

When two tones of close frequencies $f_1$ and $f_2$ are sounded together,
$$
\sin(2\pi f_1 t)+\sin(2\pi f_2 t)
=2\cos\!\Bigl(2\pi\frac{f_1-f_2}{2}t\Bigr)\sin\!\Bigl(2\pi\frac{f_1+f_2}{2}t\Bigr).
$$
The result is a tone at the **average frequency** whose amplitude is modulated at the **beat frequency**
$$
f_{\text{beat}}=|f_1-f_2|.
$$

Beats are the basis of tuning by ear: as two strings approach unison, the beats slow and vanish. (The modulation envelope repeats at $|f_1-f_2|$, though the amplitude is periodic at $|f_1-f_2|/2$; the audible *loudness* pulsation is at $|f_1-f_2|$.)

Beats also arise from **nonlinearity** and **combination tones**, and are used in radio heterodyning, in beating between laser modes, and in interferometry.

---

## The Doppler Effect

When a source or observer moves, the observed frequency shifts.

Let $v$ be the speed of sound, $v_s$ the source speed, $v_o$ the observer speed, and $f_s$ the source frequency. With the standard sign convention (observer moving toward source, source moving toward observer increase the observed frequency):

$$
f_o=f_s\left(\frac{v \pm v_o}{v \mp v_s}\right).
$$

- **Moving observer, stationary source:** $f_o=f_s\dfrac{v+v_o}{v}$ (approaching) or $\dfrac{v-v_o}{v}$ (receding).
- **Moving source, stationary observer:** $f_o=f_s\dfrac{v}{v-v_s}$ (approaching) or $\dfrac{v}{v+v_s}$ (receding) — the wavelength itself is compressed or stretched.

The shift is $\Delta f\approx f_s v_s/v$ for slow motion. Applications: police radar (double shift), medical Doppler ultrasound, sonar, weather radar, and the **redshift** of receding galaxies. For speeds comparable to $c$, the **relativistic Doppler formula** replaces the classical one:
$$
f_o=f_s\sqrt{\frac{1-\beta}{1+\beta}}\quad(\text{receding}),\qquad \beta=v/c.
$$

### Fourier Synthesis of a Plucked String

```{python}
#| label: fig-pluck
#| fig-cap: "Fourier synthesis of a plucked string. Left: a triangular initial shape (pluck at x0=0.3L) reconstructed by adding sine harmonics; more harmonics sharpen the corner. Middle: the harmonic amplitudes; the envelope falls like 1/n² and a pluck at 0.3L suppresses the harmonics with a node there. Right: a center pluck (x0=0.5L) excites only odd harmonics, which is why plucking at the middle sounds mellow and near the bridge sounds bright."

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

L, x0, h = 1.0, 0.3, 1.0
x = np.linspace(0, L, 800)

def pluck_coeffs(n_max):
    n = np.arange(1, n_max + 1)
    b = (2 * h * L**2) / (n**2 * np.pi**2 * x0 * (L - x0)) * np.sin(n * np.pi * x0 / L)
    return n, b

def pluck_shape(x, n_max):
    n, b = pluck_coeffs(n_max)
    return sum(b[k] * np.sin((k+1) * np.pi * x / L) for k in range(len(n)))

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(16, 4.8))
axes[0].plot(x, pluck_shape(x, 500), "k--", lw=1, label="target (triangle)")
for n_max, col in [(3, "#95a5a6"), (7, "#2980b9"), (25, "#e74c3c")]:
    axes[0].plot(x, pluck_shape(x, n_max), color=col, lw=1, label=f"{n_max} harmonics")
axes[0].set_title(f"Plucked string, pluck at x0={x0}L")
axes[0].set_xlabel("x/L"); axes[0].set_ylabel("displacement"); axes[0].legend(fontsize=8); axes[0].grid(alpha=0.3)

n, b = pluck_coeffs(20)
axes[1].stem(n, np.abs(b), basefmt=" ")
axes[1].set_title(f"Harmonic amplitudes (pluck {x0}L)")
axes[1].set_xlabel("harmonic n"); axes[1].set_ylabel("|b_n|"); axes[1].grid(alpha=0.3)

n2 = np.arange(1, 21)
b_center = (2 * h * L**2) / (n2**2 * np.pi**2 * 0.5 * 0.5) * np.sin(n2 * np.pi / 2)
axes[2].stem(n2, np.abs(b_center), basefmt=" ")
axes[2].set_title("Center pluck (0.5L): odd harmonics only")
axes[2].set_xlabel("harmonic n"); axes[2].set_ylabel("|b_n|"); axes[2].grid(alpha=0.3)
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

print("=== PLUCK AT x0=0.3L: FIRST HARMONICS ===")
n, b = pluck_coeffs(8)
for k in range(len(n)):
    print(f"  n={n[k]}: |b_n| = {abs(b[k]):.4f}")
print(f"\n  center pluck (0.5L) amplitudes: {np.round(np.abs(b_center[:6]), 4)}")
print("  -> even harmonics vanish for a center pluck, as expected.")
```

### Beats

```{python}
#| label: fig-beats
#| fig-cap: "Beats between two close frequencies. Top: two tones at 440 Hz and 444 Hz sum to a tone at the average (442 Hz) whose amplitude is modulated at the difference (4 Hz). Bottom: the envelope ±2cos(πΔf t) makes the pulsation explicit; a tuner listens for the beat rate to slow to zero as two strings reach unison."

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

fs = 8000
t = np.linspace(0, 1.5, int(fs * 1.5))
f1, f2 = 440.0, 444.0
y = np.sin(2 * np.pi * f1 * t) + np.sin(2 * np.pi * f2 * t)
env = 2 * np.abs(np.cos(np.pi * (f2 - f1) * t))

fig, axes = plt.subplots(2, 1, figsize=(11, 6))
axes[0].plot(t, y, lw=0.6, color="#2980b9")
axes[0].set_title(f"Beats: {f1:.0f} Hz + {f2:.0f} Hz  (beat frequency {abs(f2-f1):.0f} Hz)")
axes[0].set_xlabel("time (s)"); axes[0].grid(alpha=0.3)
axes[1].plot(t, y, lw=0.4, color="#95a5a6")
axes[1].plot(t, env, color="#e74c3c", lw=1.5, label="envelope ±2cos(πΔf t)")
axes[1].plot(t, -env, color="#e74c3c", lw=1.5)
axes[1].set_title("Envelope: pulsation at |f1−f2|")
axes[1].set_xlabel("time (s)"); axes[1].legend(); axes[1].grid(alpha=0.3)
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

print("=== BEATS ===")
for a, b in [(440, 444), (440, 441), (261.6, 261.6), (440, 466.16)]:
    print(f"  {a:.1f} Hz + {b:.1f} Hz -> beat = {abs(a-b):6.2f} Hz")
print("  a frequency difference of 0 gives no beats (in tune).")
```

### The Doppler Effect

```{python}
import numpy as np

v = 343.0   # speed of sound in air, m/s

def doppler(fs, vs=0.0, vo=0.0, approach_source=True, approach_obs=True):
    num = v + (vo if approach_obs else -vo)
    den = v - (vs if approach_source else -vs)
    return fs * num / den

print("=== DOPPLER EFFECT (f_s = 700 Hz ambulance siren, v = 343 m/s) ===")
for vs in [0, 10, 20, 30]:
    f_ap = doppler(700, vs=vs, approach_source=True)
    f_re = doppler(700, vs=vs, approach_source=False)
    print(f"  source {vs:>2} m/s: approaching -> {f_ap:7.2f} Hz, receding -> {f_re:7.2f} Hz, "
          f"shift {f_ap-700:+.2f} Hz")

print("\n=== MOVING OBSERVER (source at 700 Hz) ===")
for vo in [0, 10, 20, 30]:
    print(f"  observer {vo:>2} m/s: approaching -> {doppler(700, vo=vo, approach_obs=True):7.2f} Hz, "
          f"receding -> {doppler(700, vo=vo, approach_obs=False):7.2f} Hz")

print("\n=== RELATIVISTIC DOPPLER (redshift) ===")
for beta in [0.01, 0.1, 0.5, 0.9]:
    f_ratio = np.sqrt((1 - beta) / (1 + beta))
    print(f"  v/c={beta}: f_obs/f_src (receding) = {f_ratio:.4f}, "
          f"shift z = {(1/f_ratio - 1):.4f}")
```

The laboratory connects the abstract wave equation to sound: boundary conditions select the harmonics, the pluck position shapes the timbre, close frequencies produce audible beats, and motion shifts frequency through the Doppler effect.

## Summary

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\textbf{String waves: } && y_{tt}=v^2y_{xx},\quad v=\sqrt{T/\mu}. \\[3pt]
&\textbf{Fixed–fixed: } && f_n=n v/(2L)\ \text{(all harmonics); nodes at both ends.} \\[3pt]
&\textbf{Fixed–free: } && f_n=(2n-1)v/(4L)\ \text{(odd only); node/antinode.} \\[3pt]
&\textbf{Free–free: } && f_n=n v/(2L)\ \text{(all); antinodes at both ends.} \\[3pt]
&\textbf{Pluck: } && \text{harmonic amplitudes } b_n\propto n^{-2}\sin(n\pi x_0/L);\ \text{position selects timbre.} \\[3pt]
&\textbf{Beats: } && f_{\text{beat}}=|f_1-f_2|;\ \text{amplitude modulation (tuning).} \\[3pt]
&\textbf{Doppler: } && f_o=f_s\frac{v\pm v_o}{v\mp v_s};\ \text{radar, ultrasound, redshift.}
\end{aligned}
$$

The same Fourier analysis that decomposes a plucked string is the [series analysis of the previous article](../mathematics/series-real-complex-analysis-special-sums-and-closed-forms.qmd): a physical shape, expressed as a sum of harmonics, whose coefficients are the timbre of an instrument and the spectrum of a note.
