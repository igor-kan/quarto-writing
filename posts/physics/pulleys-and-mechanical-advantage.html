Pulleys: Classical Physics, Mechanical Advantage, and Engineering Systems

From geometric string constraints and the Atwood machine to rotational inertia, the Capstan equation, and industrial lifting mechanisms.

Physics
Mechanics
Engineering
Published

September 8, 2026

The pulley is one of the classical simple machines described by Hero of Alexandria in the 1st century AD. In elementary physics textbooks, pulleys are usually idealized as massless, frictionless wheels redirecting tension in an inextensible string. In reality, pulleys are sophisticated mechanical transformers that exchange displacement for force, govern crane designs and elevator counterweights, and exhibit rich dynamics when inertia and friction are accounted for.

This article explores the analytical mechanics of pulleys, from string constraint geometry and compound Atwood machines to the Capstan friction equation and industrial hoisting systems.

1. Principles of Mechanical Advantage and Geometric Constraints

A pulley system operates on the conservation of energy and work: \[W_{\text{in}} = W_{\text{out}} \implies F_{\text{effort}} \cdot d_{\text{effort}} = F_{\text{load}} \cdot d_{\text{load}} \quad (\text{ideal system})\]

We define the primary engineering metrics: * Mechanical Advantage (MA): The factor by which the machine multiplies input force: \[MA = \frac{F_{\text{load}}}{F_{\text{effort}}}\] * Velocity Ratio (VR): The ratio of the distance moved by the effort to the distance moved by the load: \[VR = \frac{d_{\text{effort}}}{d_{\text{load}}} = \frac{v_{\text{effort}}}{v_{\text{load}}}\] * Efficiency (\(\eta\)): The ratio of useful work output to work input: \[\eta = \frac{W_{\text{out}}}{W_{\text{in}}} = \frac{F_{\text{load}} \cdot d_{\text{load}}}{F_{\text{effort}} \cdot d_{\text{effort}}} = \frac{MA}{VR}\] For an idealized frictionless system, \(\eta = 1 \implies MA = VR\).

Fixed vs. Movable Pulleys

  1. Fixed Pulley (\(MA = 1, VR = 1\)): The axle is anchored to a ceiling or beam. It provides no mechanical advantage (\(F_{\text{effort}} = F_{\text{load}}\)); its sole purpose is to change the direction of the tension vector (e.g., allowing a worker to pull downward with gravity to lift a load upward).
  2. Movable Pulley (\(MA = 2, VR = 2\)): The axle moves with the load. The load is supported by two tension segments of the same rope. Thus, \(2T = F_{\text{load}} \implies T = F_{\text{load}} / 2\). Pulling the rope by distance \(2d\) raises the load by distance \(d\).

The Inextensible String Constraint

In multi-body dynamics, pulleys introduce algebraic constraints among coordinates. For an inextensible rope of total length \(L\) wrapping over pulleys: \[L = \sum_{i=1}^N c_i x_i + \text{constant}\] Differentiating once and twice with respect to time yields the velocity and acceleration kinematic constraints: \[\sum_{i=1}^N c_i \dot{x}_i = 0, \qquad \sum_{i=1}^N c_i \ddot{x}_i = 0\]

2. Classical Benchmark Problems

The Standard Atwood Machine (George Atwood, 1784)

Two masses \(m_1 > m_2\) are connected over a massless, frictionless pulley by an inextensible rope.

Let \(x\) be the downward displacement of \(m_1\). By the rope constraint \(x_1 + x_2 = L\), the upward displacement of \(m_2\) is identical (\(\ddot{x}_1 = -\ddot{x}_2 = a\)).

Newton’s second law for each mass: \[m_1 g - T = m_1 a\] \[T - m_2 g = m_2 a\]

Adding the equations eliminates tension \(T\): \[(m_1 - m_2)g = (m_1 + m_2)a \implies \boxed{a = \frac{m_1 - m_2}{m_1 + m_2} g}\]

Substituting \(a\) back yields the string tension: \[\boxed{T = \frac{2 m_1 m_2}{m_1 + m_2} g}\]

Physical Interpretation: The acceleration is scaled down from \(g\) by the dimensionless ratio \((m_1 - m_2)/(m_1 + m_2)\), which allowed 18th-century physicists to measure \(g\) accurately using slow pendulum-timed descents.

The Compound (Double) Atwood Machine

Consider mass \(m_1\) suspended on one side of a primary pulley, while the other side supports a secondary pulley with masses \(m_2\) and \(m_3\) hanging from it.

Let \(x\) be the downward position of \(m_1\), \(y\) be the position of the movable pulley (\(x + y = L_1 \implies \ddot{y} = -\ddot{x}\)), and \(z\) be the position of \(m_2\) relative to the secondary pulley.

Using Lagrangian mechanics: \[T_{\text{kinetic}} = \frac{1}{2}m_1 \dot{x}^2 + \frac{1}{2}m_2 (\dot{y} + \dot{z})^2 + \frac{1}{2}m_3 (\dot{y} - \dot{z})^2\] \[V_{\text{potential}} = -m_1 gx - m_2 g(y + z) - m_3 g(y - z)\] Substituting \(y = -x\) and applying the Euler-Lagrange equations \(\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{q}} - \frac{\partial L}{\partial q} = 0\) for coordinates \(x\) and \(z\) yields the accelerations directly, demonstrating the power of generalized coordinates over free-body diagrams.

3. Real Pulleys: Rotational Inertia and Friction

In practical engineering, pulleys have mass and rotational inertia. The tension on either side of the pulley is not equal because a net torque is required to angularly accelerate the pulley wheel.

Massive Pulley with Moment of Inertia \(I\)

Let a pulley have radius \(R\), mass \(M\), and moment of inertia \(I = \frac{1}{2} M R^2\) (solid disk). The rope does not slip on the rim, enforcing the no-slip condition: \[a = \alpha R \implies \alpha = \frac{a}{R}\]

Equations of motion for an Atwood machine with a massive pulley: 1. Mass 1: \(m_1 g - T_1 = m_1 a\) 2. Mass 2: \(T_2 - m_2 g = m_2 a\) 3. Pulley rotation: \(\tau_{\text{net}} = (T_1 - T_2)R = I \alpha = I \frac{a}{R} \implies T_1 - T_2 = \frac{I}{R^2} a\)

Adding all three equations: \[(m_1 - m_2)g = \left( m_1 + m_2 + \frac{I}{R^2} \right) a\]

For a uniform solid disk (\(I/R^2 = \frac{1}{2} M\)): \[\boxed{a = \frac{m_1 - m_2}{m_1 + m_2 + \frac{1}{2}M} g}\]

The pulley’s mass contributes an effective inertia of \(\frac{1}{2}M\) to the denominator, slowing the system’s acceleration.

Belt Friction: The Capstan Equation (Eytelwein’s Formula)

When a rope wraps around a fixed, non-rotating cylinder or seized pulley with contact angle \(\theta\) (in radians) and friction coefficient \(\mu\), the tension increases exponentially from the holding side (\(T_{\text{hold}}\)) to the load side (\(T_{\text{load}}\)).

Consider an infinitesimal rope segment spanning angle \(d\theta\): * Radial equilibrium: \(d N = T d\theta\) * Tangential friction: \(d T = \mu \, d N = \mu T \, d\theta\)

Separating variables and integrating across total wrap angle \(\theta\): \[\int_{T_{\text{hold}}}^{T_{\text{load}}} \frac{dT}{T} = \mu \int_0^\theta d\theta \implies \boxed{T_{\text{load}} = T_{\text{hold}} e^{\mu \theta}}\]

Code 
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# Number of turns from 0 to 4 turns (theta = 2 * pi * turns)
turns = np.linspace(0, 4, 400)
theta = 2 * np.pi * turns

# Friction coefficients: smooth steel (0.1), nylon/wood (0.3), rubber/rough (0.5)
mus = [0.1, 0.25, 0.4]
colors = ['#3498db', '#f39c12', '#e74c3c']

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(9, 5))

for mu, col in zip(mus, colors):
    ratio = np.exp(mu * theta)
    ax.plot(turns, ratio, label=rf'$\mu = {mu}$', color=col, lw=2.5)

ax.set_yscale('log')
ax.set_xlabel('Number of Turns Around Drum ($n$)', fontsize=11, fontweight='bold')
ax.set_ylabel(r'Tension Ratio $T_{\text{load}} / T_{\text{hold}}$ (Log Scale)', fontsize=11, fontweight='bold')
ax.set_title(r'Capstan Effect: $T_{\text{load}} / T_{\text{hold}} = e^{2\pi \mu n}$', fontsize=13, fontweight='bold')
ax.grid(True, which='both', linestyle=':', alpha=0.6)
ax.legend(frameon=True, fontsize=10)

plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

Engineering Impact: With \(\mu = 0.4\), wrapping a mooring line around a capstan winch just 3 full turns (\(n = 3 \implies \theta = 6\pi \approx 18.85\text{ rad}\)) yields: \[\frac{T_{\text{load}}}{T_{\text{hold}}} = e^{0.4 \times 18.85} \approx e^{7.54} \approx \mathbf{1,880}\] A single sailor pulling with a gentle grip of 50 N (about 11 lbs) can securely hold a massive ship exerting 94,000 N (~10 tons) of mooring force!

4. Engineering Applications

  1. Block and Tackle (Crane Hoists): Arranging multiple sheaves in two blocks yields \(MA = n\), where \(n\) is the number of rope segments supporting the moving block. Industrial heavy-lift cranes use \(n = 16\) or \(32\) to lift locomotives and bridge spans with modest motor torque.
  2. Elevator Counterweight Systems: Modern passenger traction elevators use a counterweight matching the empty cabin mass plus \(\approx 40\% - 50\%\) of the maximum rated payload. The motor only needs to lift the marginal weight difference, reducing energy consumption and brake wear by over \(80\%\).
  3. Weston Differential Pulley: Uses two concentric pulleys of slightly different radii (\(R_1 > R_2\)) joined on the same axle. A continuous loop chain provides enormous mechanical advantage without needing miles of rope: \[MA = \frac{2 R_1}{R_1 - R_2}\] As \(R_1 \to R_2\), \(MA \to \infty\), creating a self-locking hoist that will not slip downward when the operator releases the chain.
