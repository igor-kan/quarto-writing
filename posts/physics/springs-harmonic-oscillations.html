Springs, Hooke’s Law, and Harmonic Oscillations: From Simple Systems to Coupled Normal Modes

A complete mathematical and physical exploration of elastic forces, phase-space trajectories, resonance, and the matrix eigenvalue problem of coupled multi-degree-of-freedom oscillators.

Physics

Oscillations

Oscillations are among the most universal physical phenomena in nature. From the vibration of carbon-carbon bonds in organic molecules and the suspension coils of an automobile to acoustic waves in musical instruments and alternating current in electrical circuits, almost every stable physical system displaced slightly from equilibrium behaves as a harmonic oscillator.

This article covers the analytical physics of elastic systems: Hooke’s law, series and parallel spring topologies, simple harmonic motion (SHO), energy conservation and phase space, damped and driven resonance, and the matrix formulation of coupled oscillators and normal modes.

1. Hooke’s Law and Elastic Potential Energy

In 1678, Robert Hooke published his law of elasticity as the Latin anagram ceiiinosssttuv, meaning Ut tensio, sic vis (“As the extension, so the force”): \[\mathbf{F}_s = -k \mathbf{x}\] where \(k\) is the spring constant (stiffness, in \(\text{N/m}\)) and \(\mathbf{x}\) is the displacement from the un-stretched equilibrium position. The negative sign represents the restoring nature of the force.

Elastic Potential Energy

The work required to stretch a spring from \(x = 0\) to \(x = X\) against this restoring force is: \[U(x) = -\int_0^x \mathbf{F}_s \cdot d\mathbf{x}' = \int_0^x k x' dx' = \boxed{\frac{1}{2} k x^2}\]

Combination of Springs

  1. Springs in Parallel: When two springs share the same displacement \(x\), their forces add: \[F_{\text{net}} = F_1 + F_2 = -(k_1 x + k_2 x) = -(k_1 + k_2) x \implies \boxed{k_{\text{parallel}} = k_1 + k_2}\]
  2. Springs in Series: When two springs are joined end-to-end, they experience the identical tension \(F\), but their displacements add (\(x_{\text{tot}} = x_1 + x_2\)): \[x_{\text{tot}} = \frac{F}{k_1} + \frac{F}{k_2} = F \left( \frac{1}{k_1} + \frac{1}{k_2} \right) \implies \boxed{\frac{1}{k_{\text{series}}} = \frac{1}{k_1} + \frac{1}{k_2} \iff k_{\text{series}} = \frac{k_1 k_2}{k_1 + k_2}}\]
  3. Cutting a Spring: If a uniform spring of stiffness \(k_0\) and resting length \(L_0\) is cut into two pieces of lengths \(L_1\) and \(L_2\), the stiffness of each piece scales inversely with length: \[k \propto \frac{1}{L} \implies k_1 = k_0 \frac{L_0}{L_1}\] Physical reason: Shorter springs experience less extension per coil under the same applied tensile force.

2. The Simple Harmonic Oscillator (SHO)

Attaching a mass \(m\) to a spring on a frictionless horizontal plane gives the canonical second-order linear differential equation: \[m \ddot{x} + kx = 0 \implies \ddot{x} + \omega_0^2 x = 0\] where the natural angular frequency is: \[\omega_0 = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}, \qquad f_0 = \frac{\omega_0}{2\pi} = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}, \qquad T = \frac{1}{f_0} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{k}}\]

Kinematic Solution

The general solution is: \[x(t) = A \cos(\omega_0 t + \phi)\] \[v(t) = \dot{x}(t) = -\omega_0 A \sin(\omega_0 t + \phi)\] \[a(t) = \ddot{x}(t) = -\omega_0^2 A \cos(\omega_0 t + \phi) = -\omega_0^2 x(t)\]

where \(A\) is the oscillation amplitude and \(\phi\) is the initial phase determined by initial conditions \(x(0) = x_0\) and \(v(0) = v_0\): \[A = \sqrt{x_0^2 + \left(\frac{v_0}{\omega_0}\right)^2}, \qquad \tan\phi = -\frac{v_0}{\omega_0 x_0}\]

Energy Invariance and the Virial Theorem

The total mechanical energy \(E\) at any instant is: \[E = K(t) + U(t) = \frac{1}{2} m v^2 + \frac{1}{2} k x^2\] \[E = \frac{1}{2} m \omega_0^2 A^2 \sin^2(\omega_0 t + \phi) + \frac{1}{2} k A^2 \cos^2(\omega_0 t + \phi)\] Since \(m\omega_0^2 = k\): \[E = \frac{1}{2} k A^2 \left[ \sin^2(\omega_0 t + \phi) + \cos^2(\omega_0 t + \phi) \right] = \boxed{\frac{1}{2} k A^2 = \text{constant}}\]

Averaging over one full oscillation period \(T\): \[\langle K \rangle = \frac{1}{T} \int_0^T K(t) dt = \frac{1}{4} k A^2, \qquad \langle U \rangle = \frac{1}{T} \int_0^T U(t) dt = \frac{1}{4} k A^2\] \[\boxed{\langle K \rangle = \langle U \rangle = \frac{1}{2} E}\] Energy perpetually oscillates between pure potential (at the turning points \(x = \pm A, v = 0\)) and pure kinetic (at equilibrium \(x = 0, v = \pm v_{\text{max}}\)).

3. Damping and Driven Resonance

Real mechanical springs experience frictional dissipation: \[m \ddot{x} + b \dot{x} + kx = F_{\text{ext}}(t)\] where \(b\) is the damping coefficient. Dividing by \(m\) and defining \(\beta = \frac{b}{2m}\) and \(\omega_0^2 = \frac{k}{m}\): \[\ddot{x} + 2\beta \dot{x} + \omega_0^2 x = \frac{F_0}{m} \cos(\omega t)\]

Homogeneous Regimes (\(F_{\text{ext}} = 0\))

  1. Underdamped (\(\beta < \omega_0\)): Oscillations decay exponentially with quasi-frequency \(\omega_d = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \beta^2}\): \[x(t) = A e^{-\beta t} \cos(\omega_d t + \phi)\]
  2. Critically Damped (\(\beta = \omega_0\)): Returns to equilibrium in the fastest possible time without overshoot. Crucial for car shock absorbers and galvanometers: \[x(t) = (C_1 + C_2 t) e^{-\beta t}\]
  3. Overdamped (\(\beta > \omega_0\)): Motion is sluggish and non-oscillatory, governed by real exponential decays.

Steady-State Driven Resonance

When driven at frequency \(\omega\), the steady-state response amplitude is: \[A(\omega) = \frac{F_0 / m}{\sqrt{(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)^2 + 4\beta^2 \omega^2}}\] Resonance occurs near \(\omega \approx \omega_0\), where the energy transfer from the driver to the oscillator is maximized.

4. Coupled Oscillators and Normal Modes

Consider two identical masses \(m_1 = m_2 = m\) connected between fixed walls by three identical springs of stiffness \(k\).

Let \(x_1\) and \(x_2\) be the horizontal displacements of the two masses from their respective equilibrium positions: * Force on mass 1: \(F_1 = -k x_1 + k(x_2 - x_1) = -2k x_1 + k x_2\) * Force on mass 2: \(F_2 = -k x_2 - k(x_2 - x_1) = k x_1 - 2k x_2\)

Matrix Formulation

In vector-matrix form: \[\mathbf{M} \ddot{\mathbf{x}} + \mathbf{K} \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}\] \[\begin{pmatrix} m & 0 \\ 0 & m \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \ddot{x}_1 \\ \ddot{x}_2 \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} 2k & -k \\ -k & 2k \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}\]

Assuming normal mode solutions where both masses oscillate at the same frequency \(\omega\): \(\mathbf{x}(t) = \mathbf{a} e^{i\omega t}\): \[(\mathbf{K} - \omega^2 \mathbf{M}) \mathbf{a} = \mathbf{0}\]

Nontrivial solutions require the characteristic determinant to vanish: \[\det \begin{pmatrix} 2k - m\omega^2 & -k \\ -k & 2k - m\omega^2 \end{pmatrix} = 0\] \[(2k - m\omega^2)^2 - k^2 = 0 \implies 2k - m\omega^2 = \pm k\]

The Two Normal Modes

  1. Mode 1 (Symmetric / In-Phase): \[2k - m\omega_1^2 = +k \implies \boxed{\omega_1 = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}}\] Eigenvector: \(\mathbf{a}_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \implies x_1(t) = x_2(t)\). The two masses move in lockstep in the same direction. The central coupling spring is neither stretched nor compressed!
  2. Mode 2 (Antisymmetric / Out-of-Phase): \[2k - m\omega_2^2 = -k \implies \boxed{\omega_2 = \sqrt{\frac{3k}{m}}}\] Eigenvector: \(\mathbf{a}_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix} \implies x_1(t) = -x_2(t)\). The two masses move in opposite directions, maximally compressing and expanding the central spring, resulting in a higher restoring force and higher frequency (\(\omega_2 = \sqrt{3} \omega_1\)).

Beats and Energy Transfer

If we displace mass 1 by distance \(A\) while holding mass 2 at rest (\(x_1(0) = A, x_2(0) = 0\)), the initial state is a linear superposition of both normal modes: \[\mathbf{x}(t) = \frac{A}{2} \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \cos(\omega_1 t) + \frac{A}{2} \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix} \cos(\omega_2 t)\]

Expanding the components using trigonometric sum-to-product identities: \[x_1(t) = A \cos\left( \frac{\omega_2 - \omega_1}{2} t \right) \cos\left( \frac{\omega_1 + \omega_2}{2} t \right)\] \[x_2(t) = A \sin\left( \frac{\omega_2 - \omega_1}{2} t \right) \sin\left( \frac{\omega_1 + \omega_2}{2} t \right)\]

Code 
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# Parameters
m = 1.0
k = 10.0
omega1 = np.sqrt(k / m)
omega2 = np.sqrt(3 * k / m)

# Time array
t = np.linspace(0, 20, 1000)

# Amplitude envelope and fast carrier
omega_mod = (omega2 - omega1) / 2.0
omega_car = (omega1 + omega2) / 2.0

x1 = np.cos(omega_mod * t) * np.cos(omega_car * t)
x2 = np.sin(omega_mod * t) * np.sin(omega_car * t)

fig, (ax1, ax2) = plt.subplots(2, 1, figsize=(10, 6), sharex=True)

# Mass 1 trajectory
ax1.plot(t, x1, color='#1f77b4', lw=1.8, label='Mass 1 Displacement $x_1(t)$')
ax1.plot(t, np.cos(omega_mod * t), color='#e74c3c', linestyle='--', lw=1.5, alpha=0.8, label='Modulation Envelope')
ax1.plot(t, -np.cos(omega_mod * t), color='#e74c3c', linestyle='--', lw=1.5, alpha=0.8)
ax1.set_ylabel('Displacement [m]', fontsize=10, fontweight='bold')
ax1.set_title('Energy Exchange Between Coupled Oscillators', fontsize=12, fontweight='bold')
ax1.grid(True, linestyle=':', alpha=0.6)
ax1.legend(loc='upper right', frameon=True, fontsize=9)

# Mass 2 trajectory
ax2.plot(t, x2, color='#2ca02c', lw=1.8, label='Mass 2 Displacement $x_2(t)$')
ax2.plot(t, np.sin(omega_mod * t), color='#e74c3c', linestyle='--', lw=1.5, alpha=0.8, label='Modulation Envelope')
ax2.plot(t, -np.sin(omega_mod * t), color='#e74c3c', linestyle='--', lw=1.5, alpha=0.8)
ax2.set_xlabel('Time [seconds]', fontsize=11, fontweight='bold')
ax2.set_ylabel('Displacement [m]', fontsize=10, fontweight='bold')
ax2.grid(True, linestyle=':', alpha=0.6)
ax2.legend(loc='upper right', frameon=True, fontsize=9)

plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

Energy sloshes completely back and forth between Mass 1 and Mass 2 with beat period \(T_{\text{beat}} = \frac{2\pi}{\omega_2 - \omega_1}\). When Mass 1 is completely stationary at \(t = \frac{\pi}{\omega_2 - \omega_1}\), Mass 2 possesses 100% of the system’s mechanical energy.
