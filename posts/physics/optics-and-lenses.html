A comprehensive mathematical treatise on geometrical optics: Fermat’s Principle, Snell’s Law, chromatic dispersion, spherical surfaces, the Lensmaker’s equation, and ABCD matrix ray tracing.

Geometrical optics treats light propagation in terms of idealized straight lines—rays—which are perpendicular to the wavefronts of the underlying electromagnetic wave. When the wavelength of light (\(\lambda \approx 400 - 700\text{ nm}\)) is much smaller than the characteristic physical apertures and macroscopic dimensions of lenses, ray optics serves as an exact, high-precision asymptotic limit of Maxwell’s electrodynamics.

1. The Refractive Index and Phase Velocity

In vacuum, electromagnetic waves propagate at \(c = 299{,}792{,}458\text{ m/s}\). In a physical dielectric medium, the electric field of the photon induces oscillations in bound atomic electrons. These oscillating charges re-radiate secondary electromagnetic waves with a phase delay. The superposition of the original and induced waves yields a macroscopic wave propagating with a reduced phase velocity \(v\).

The index of refraction \(n\) is defined as:

\[ n = \frac{c}{v} = \sqrt{\epsilon_r \mu_r} \]

where \(\epsilon_r\) is the relative permittivity (dielectric constant) and \(\mu_r \approx 1\) is the relative magnetic permeability at optical frequencies.

Dispersion: The Wavelength Dependence

The refractive index is not a constant; it depends on frequency/wavelength \(\lambda\). In transparent materials far from absorption bands, this is quantified by Cauchy’s equation:

\[ n(\lambda) = A + \frac{B}{\lambda^2} + \frac{C}{\lambda^4} + \dots \]

or more rigorously by the Sellmeier dispersion formula:

\[ n^2(\lambda) = 1 + \sum_i \frac{B_i \lambda^2}{\lambda^2 - C_i} \]

Because shorter wavelengths (blue light) experience higher refractive indices than longer wavelengths (red light), glass prisms disperse white light into a spectrum, leading to chromatic aberration in uncorrected lenses.

2. Derivation of Snell’s Law from Fermat’s Principle

Fermat’s Principle of Least Time (1662) states: > The actual path between two points taken by a light beam is the one that traverses the path in stationary time.

Consider light traveling from point \(A(0, a)\) in medium 1 (index \(n_1\)) to point \(B(d, -b)\) in medium 2 (index \(n_2\)), crossing the planar boundary \(y = 0\) at coordinate \((x, 0)\).

The total optical travel time \(t(x)\) is:

\[ t(x) = \frac{\ell_1}{v_1} + \frac{\ell_2}{v_2} = \frac{n_1}{c} \sqrt{x^2 + a^2} + \frac{n_2}{c} \sqrt{(d - x)^2 + b^2} \]

To find the path of stationary time, set \(\frac{dt}{dx} = 0\):

\[ \frac{dt}{dx} = \frac{n_1}{c} \frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2 + a^2}} - \frac{n_2}{c} \frac{d - x}{\sqrt{(d - x)^2 + b^2}} = 0 \]

Recognizing the geometric definitions of the incident angle \(\theta_1\) and refracted angle \(\theta_2\) relative to the surface normal:

\[ \sin\theta_1 = \frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2 + a^2}}, \quad \sin\theta_2 = \frac{d - x}{\sqrt{(d - x)^2 + b^2}} \]

Substituting these into the derivative immediately yields Snell’s Law of Refraction:

\[ n_1 \sin\theta_1 = n_2 \sin\theta_2 \]

3. Total Internal Reflection and Evanescent Waves

When light propagates from an optically denser medium into a rarer medium (\(n_1 > n_2\)), the refracted ray bends away from the normal (\(\theta_2 > \theta_1\)). The angle of incidence for which \(\theta_2 = 90^\circ\) is the critical angle \(\theta_c\):

\[ n_1 \sin\theta_c = n_2 \sin(90^\circ) \implies \theta_c = \arcsin\left(\frac{n_2}{n_1}\right) \]

For \(\theta_1 > \theta_c\), no real solution exists for \(\theta_2\). All incident power is reflected back into medium 1—a phenomenon known as Total Internal Reflection (TIR), which forms the physical foundation of fiber optic telecommunications and endoscopy.

Quantum Optical Tunneling

Beyond \(\theta_c\), electromagnetic boundary conditions require the continuity of electric fields across the interface. Maxwell’s equations reveal that an evanescent field penetrates into medium 2:

\[ \mathbf{E}_2(z) = \mathbf{E}_0 e^{-\kappa z} e^{i(k_x x - \omega t)} \]

where the attenuation coefficient is \(\kappa = k_0 \sqrt{n_1^2 \sin^2\theta_1 - n_2^2}\). Although this field carries zero time-averaged energy across an isolated interface, placing a third medium of index \(n_3\) within a few nanometers allows light to “tunnel” across the air gap (Frustrated Total Internal Reflection), exactly analogous to quantum mechanical particle tunneling through a finite potential barrier!

4. Refraction at a Spherical Surface and the Lensmaker’s Formula

Consider a convex spherical dielectric surface of radius of curvature \(R\) separating medium \(n_1\) from medium \(n_2\). Under the paraxial approximation (where angles \(\alpha, \beta, \gamma \ll 1\) so \(\sin\theta \approx \tan\theta \approx \theta\)):

\[ \frac{n_1}{s_o} + \frac{n_2}{s_i} = \frac{n_2 - n_1}{R} \]

where \(s_o\) is the object distance and \(s_i\) is the image distance.

The Lensmaker’s Equation

For a thick lens of index \(n\) and thickness \(d\) surrounded by air (\(n_0 = 1\)), with front surface radius \(R_1\) and rear surface radius \(R_2\), chaining two consecutive spherical refractions yields:

\[ \frac{1}{f} = (n - 1) \left[ \frac{1}{R_1} - \frac{1}{R_2} + \frac{(n - 1)d}{n R_1 R_2} \right] \]

In the limit of a thin lens (\(d \ll R_1, R_2\)), the thickness term vanishes:

\[ \frac{1}{f} = (n - 1) \left( \frac{1}{R_1} - \frac{1}{R_2} \right) \]

This leads directly to the Gaussian Thin Lens Formula:

\[ \frac{1}{d_o} + \frac{1}{d_i} = \frac{1}{f} \]

and the transverse lateral magnification:

\[ m = \frac{h_i}{h_o} = -\frac{d_i}{d_o} \]

Sign Convention (Cartesian): * \(d_o > 0\) for real objects in front of the lens. * \(d_i > 0\) for real images behind the lens (inverted); \(d_i < 0\) for virtual images (upright). * \(f > 0\) for converging (convex) lenses; \(f < 0\) for diverging (concave) lenses.

5. Matrix Optics: ABCD Ray Transfer Analysis

In complex optical setups with multiple cascaded elements (microscopes, telescopes, laser resonators), tracing rays geometrically becomes tedious. We use ABCD Matrix Ray Optics.

A paraxial ray at optical axis coordinate \(z\) is completely described by a 2D vector:

\[ \mathbf{r} = \begin{bmatrix} y \\ \theta \end{bmatrix} \]

where \(y\) is the transverse displacement from the optical axis and \(\theta \approx \frac{dy}{dz}\) is the ray angle.

Every linear optical element is represented by a \(2 \times 2\) matrix with \(\det(\mathbf{M}) = \frac{n_{\text{in}}}{n_{\text{out}}}\):

  1. Free Space Propagation over distance \(d\): \[ \mathbf{M}_{\text{space}} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & d \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

  2. Thin Lens of Focal Length \(f\): \[ \mathbf{M}_{\text{lens}} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -\frac{1}{f} & 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

  3. Planar Interface between index \(n_1\) and \(n_2\): \[ \mathbf{M}_{\text{interface}} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & \frac{n_1}{n_2} \end{bmatrix} \]

  4. Curved Dielectric Interface of radius \(R\): \[ \mathbf{M}_{\text{curved}} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -\frac{n_2 - n_1}{n_2 R} & \frac{n_1}{n_2} \end{bmatrix} \]

For a cascade of elements, the system transfer matrix is obtained via matrix multiplication in reverse order: \[ \mathbf{M}_{\text{system}} = \mathbf{M}_k \mathbf{M}_{k-1} \cdots \mathbf{M}_2 \mathbf{M}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} A & B \\ C & D \end{bmatrix} \]

6. Programmatic Geometric Ray Tracing

Below is a complete ray-tracing diagram generated in Python showing the three principal rays forming a real, inverted image through a converging thin lens.

Code 
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

f = 40.0       # Focal length [mm]
d_o = 70.0     # Object distance [mm]
h_o = 25.0     # Object height [mm]

# Compute image position and height using thin lens formula
d_i = 1.0 / (1.0/f - 1.0/d_o)
m = -d_i / d_o
h_i = m * h_o

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(10, 5))

# Optical axis
ax.axhline(0, color='black', lw=1.2, linestyle='-', alpha=0.7)

# Lens plane
ax.axvline(0, color='#1f77b4', lw=2.5, linestyle='-', label='Thin Lens')
ax.plot([0, 0], [-45, 45], color='#1f77b4', lw=3)
# Lens double-arrow symbols
ax.annotate('', xy=(0, 45), xytext=(0, 35), arrowprops=dict(arrowstyle="->", color='#1f77b4', lw=2))
ax.annotate('', xy=(0, -45), xytext=(0, -35), arrowprops=dict(arrowstyle="->", color='#1f77b4', lw=2))

# Focal points
ax.scatter([-f, f], [0, 0], color='crimson', s=50, zorder=5, label=f'Focal Points ($f = {f}$ mm)')
ax.text(-f, -4, '$F_1$', ha='center', color='crimson', fontweight='bold')
ax.text(f, -4, '$F_2$', ha='center', color='crimson', fontweight='bold')

# Object (Arrow)
ax.annotate('', xy=(-d_o, h_o), xytext=(-d_o, 0),
            arrowprops=dict(facecolor='darkgreen', edgecolor='darkgreen', width=2, headwidth=8))
ax.text(-d_o, h_o + 3, f'Object\n($d_o={d_o}$, $h_o={h_o}$)', ha='center', color='darkgreen', fontweight='bold')

# Image (Inverted Arrow)
ax.annotate('', xy=(d_i, h_i), xytext=(d_i, 0),
            arrowprops=dict(facecolor='purple', edgecolor='purple', width=2, headwidth=8))
ax.text(d_i, h_i - 8, f'Real Image\n($d_i={d_i:.1f}$, $h_i={h_i:.1f}$)', ha='center', color='purple', fontweight='bold')

# Ray 1: Parallel to axis, then through focal point F2
ax.plot([-d_o, 0], [h_o, h_o], color='orange', lw=1.8, label='Ray 1: Parallel $\\to$ Focal')
ax.plot([0, d_i + 20], [h_o, h_o - (h_o/f)*(d_i + 20)], color='orange', lw=1.8)

# Ray 2: Through optical center (undeviated)
slope_center = -h_o / d_o
ax.plot([-d_o, d_i + 20], [h_o, slope_center * (d_i + 20)], color='blue', lw=1.8, linestyle='--', label='Ray 2: Central (Undeviated)')

# Ray 3: Through front focal point F1, exits parallel
slope_f1 = (0 - h_o) / (-f - (-d_o))
y_at_lens = h_o + slope_f1 * d_o
ax.plot([-d_o, 0], [h_o, y_at_lens], color='green', lw=1.8, linestyle='-.', label='Ray 3: Focal $\\to$ Parallel')
ax.plot([0, d_i + 20], [y_at_lens, y_at_lens], color='green', lw=1.8, linestyle='-.')

ax.set_xlim(-d_o - 25, d_i + 35)
ax.set_ylim(-45, 45)
ax.set_xlabel('Optical Axis $z$ [mm]')
ax.set_ylabel('Transverse Height $y$ [mm]')
ax.set_title('Thin Lens Imaging: The Three Principal Rays', fontsize=13, fontweight='bold')
ax.grid(True, linestyle=':', alpha=0.6)
ax.legend(loc='upper right', frameon=True, fontsize='small')
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()
