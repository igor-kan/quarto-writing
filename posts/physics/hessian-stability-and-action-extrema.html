The Hessian, Eigenvalues, and the Classification of Stationary Paths in Classical Mechanics

From normal mode oscillations and stability matrices to the second variation of action, Legendre conditions, Jacobi conjugate points, and Morse theory.

Physics
Classical Mechanics
Mathematics

In classical mechanics, two fundamental questions arise whenever we analyze a physical system: 1. Statics & Small Oscillations: Given an equilibrium configuration, is it stable, unstable, or neutral, and what are its characteristic vibrational frequencies? 2. Dynamics & Hamilton’s Principle: When a system traverses a trajectory between two points in spacetime, Hamilton’s principle states that the action \(S[q] = \int L \, dt\) is stationary (\(\delta S = 0\)). But is that true path a true minimum, a saddle point, or even a maximum? And why does classical mechanics effortlessly find stationary curves, while proving that they are minima is notoriously difficult?

This article examines the mathematical machinery that answers both questions: the Hessian matrix, the generalized eigenvalue problem, the second variation of functionals, the Legendre condition, Jacobi’s conjugate point theorem, and Morse theory.

1. Equilibrium, Stability, and the Hessian Matrix

Consider an autonomous conservative system with \(N\) generalized coordinates \(\mathbf{q} = (q_1, q_2, \dots, q_N)^T\) and Lagrangian \(L = T - V\).

The kinetic energy in generalized coordinates is quadratic in velocities: \[T = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i,j=1}^N M_{ij}(\mathbf{q}) \dot{q}_i \dot{q}_j = \frac{1}{2} \dot{\mathbf{q}}^T \mathbf{M}(\mathbf{q}) \dot{\mathbf{q}}\] where \(\mathbf{M}\) is the symmetric, positive-definite mass matrix.

The potential energy \(V(\mathbf{q})\) depends solely on coordinates. An equilibrium point \(\mathbf{q}_0\) occurs where generalized forces vanish: \[\mathbf{F}_{\text{gen}} = -\left. \boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\mathbf{q}} V \right|_{\mathbf{q}_0} = \mathbf{0} \iff \left. \frac{\partial V}{\partial q_i} \right|_{\mathbf{q}_0} = 0 \quad \forall i\]

Multivariable Taylor Expansion

Expanding \(V(\mathbf{q})\) in a Taylor series around \(\mathbf{q}_0\) for small perturbations \(\mathbf{\eta} = \mathbf{q} - \mathbf{q}_0\): \[V(\mathbf{q}) = V(\mathbf{q}_0) + \sum_{i=1}^N \underbrace{\left. \frac{\partial V}{\partial q_i} \right|_{\mathbf{q}_0}}_{= 0} \eta_i + \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i,j=1}^N \left. \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial q_i \partial q_j} \right|_{\mathbf{q}_0} \eta_i \eta_j + \mathcal{O}(\|\mathbf{\eta}\|^3)\]

Shifting the arbitrary potential zero (\(V(\mathbf{q}_0) = 0\)), the potential energy near equilibrium is governed entirely by the Hessian matrix \(\mathbf{H} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}\): \[H_{ij} = \left. \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial q_i \partial q_j} \right|_{\mathbf{q}_0}\] \[V(\mathbf{\eta}) \approx \frac{1}{2} \mathbf{\eta}^T \mathbf{H} \mathbf{\eta}\]

Because mixed partial derivatives commute (Schwarz’s theorem), the Hessian matrix is real and symmetric: \(\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{H}^T\).

The Generalized Eigenvalue Problem

The linearized equations of motion become: \[\mathbf{M} \ddot{\mathbf{\eta}} + \mathbf{H} \mathbf{\eta} = \mathbf{0}\]

Assuming synchronous harmonic normal modes \(\mathbf{\eta}(t) = \mathbf{a} e^{i\omega t}\): \[\boxed{(\mathbf{H} - \omega^2 \mathbf{M}) \mathbf{a} = \mathbf{0}}\]

Nontrivial solutions require the characteristic secular determinant to vanish: \[\det(\mathbf{H} - \omega^2 \mathbf{M}) = 0\]

Stability Classification via Eigenvalues

Because \(\mathbf{M}\) is positive-definite, the generalized eigenvalues \(\lambda = \omega^2\) are real numbers:

  1. Stable Equilibrium (Local Minimum):
    • \(\mathbf{H}\) is strictly positive-definite (\(\mathbf{\eta}^T \mathbf{H} \mathbf{\eta} > 0\) for all \(\mathbf{\eta} \neq \mathbf{0}\), or all eigenvalues \(\lambda_k > 0\)).
    • All frequencies \(\omega_k = \sqrt{\lambda_k}\) are purely real.
    • Motion consists of bounded, non-decaying sinusoidal oscillations (normal modes).
  2. Unstable Equilibrium (Saddle Point or Local Maximum):
    • \(\mathbf{H}\) has at least one negative eigenvalue (\(\lambda_k < 0\)).
    • The corresponding frequency is imaginary: \(\omega_k = \pm i \kappa_k\) (where \(\kappa_k = \sqrt{|\lambda_k|}\)).
    • Solutions contain exponential runaway terms: \(e^{i\omega_k t} = e^{\kappa_k t}\). Any perturbation along the corresponding eigenvector explodes exponentially in time.
  3. Marginal / Neutral Equilibrium:
    • \(\mathbf{H}\) has zero eigenvalues (\(\lambda_k = 0\)). Higher-order terms in the Taylor series (\(\mathcal{O}(\eta^3), \mathcal{O}(\eta^4)\)) dictate stability.

2. Stationary Paths vs. True Minima in the Calculus of Variations

In classical mechanics, Hamilton’s principle is often informally dubbed the “Principle of Least Action.” However, this is a misnomer: it is strictly the Principle of Stationary Action.

The action functional is: \[S[q] = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} L(t, q, \dot{q}) \, dt\]

The physical trajectory \(q(t)\) satisfies: \[\delta S[q] = 0\]

Why Classical Mechanics Readily Finds Stationary Paths

Setting \(\delta S = 0\) is mathematically straightforward because it is a first-order condition.

Under a variation \(q(t) \to q(t) + \epsilon \eta(t)\) with fixed endpoints (\(\eta(t_1) = \eta(t_2) = 0\)): \[\delta S = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} \left( \frac{\partial L}{\partial q} - \frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{q}} \right) \eta(t) \, dt = 0\]

Because \(\eta(t)\) is arbitrary, the Fundamental Lemma of the Calculus of Variations converts this global functional condition into a set of local differential equations—the Euler-Lagrange equations: \[\frac{\partial L}{\partial q_i} - \frac{d}{dt}\left( \frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{q}_i} \right) = 0\]

Differential equations are local in time. Once initial conditions \((q(0), \dot{q}(0))\) are supplied, Cauchy-Kowalevski and Picard-Lindelöf theorems guarantee a unique physical trajectory. Classical mechanics does not need to search through all infinite possible paths; it simply integrates the Euler-Lagrange equations forward in time.

3. Why Classifying Extrema in Function Space is Difficult

In ordinary scalar calculus, determining whether a critical point \(f'(x) = 0\) is a minimum, maximum, or inflection point requires only evaluating the scalar second derivative: \[f''(x) > 0 \implies \text{Minimum}, \qquad f''(x) < 0 \implies \text{Maximum}\]

In the calculus of variations, the “second derivative” is the second variation \(\delta^2 S\), which is an infinite-dimensional quadratic functional: \[S[q + \eta] = S[q] + \delta S[q, \eta] + \delta^2 S[q, \eta] + \mathcal{O}(\|\eta\|^3)\]

When \(\delta S = 0\): \[\delta^2 S = \frac{1}{2} \int_{t_1}^{t_2} \left[ \frac{\partial^2 L}{\partial q^2} \eta^2 + 2 \frac{\partial^2 L}{\partial q \partial \dot{q}} \eta \dot{\eta} + \frac{\partial^2 L}{\partial \dot{q}^2} \dot{\eta}^2 \right] dt\]

Integrating the middle term by parts yields the standard canonical form: \[\boxed{\delta^2 S[\eta] = \frac{1}{2} \int_{t_1}^{t_2} \left[ P(t) \dot{\eta}^2 + Q(t) \eta^2 \right] dt}\] where: \[P(t) = \frac{\partial^2 L}{\partial \dot{q}^2}, \qquad Q(t) = \frac{\partial^2 L}{\partial q^2} - \frac{d}{dt}\left( \frac{\partial^2 L}{\partial q \partial \dot{q}} \right)\]

For \(q(t)\) to be a true local minimum, we must prove that \(\delta^2 S[\eta] > 0\) for every possible non-zero perturbation function \(\eta(t)\) vanishing at the endpoints. In an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space, this is vastly more complex than checking the sign of a single number.

1. The Action is Never a True Local Maximum

Can a physical trajectory ever maximize action? No.

Consider adding an ultra-high-frequency perturbation: \[\eta_n(t) = \frac{\epsilon}{n} \sin\left( n \pi \frac{t - t_1}{t_2 - t_1} \right)\] As \(n \to \infty\), the displacement \(\|\eta_n\|_\infty = \frac{\epsilon}{n} \to 0\) (the path remains arbitrarily close to \(q(t)\)). However, its derivative is: \[\dot{\eta}_n(t) = \mathcal{O}(1) \implies \int \dot{\eta}_n^2 \, dt \propto n^0 = \text{finite}\]

If we scale \(\eta(t)\) with frequency, because standard kinetic energy is quadratic (\(T = \frac{1}{2} m \dot{q}^2\)), high-velocity wiggles can make the kinetic contribution arbitrarily large, driving \(S \to +\infty\).

Therefore, unconstrained physical trajectories are either local minima or saddle points in function space; they are never local maxima.

2. The Legendre Condition (Necessary Condition)

Adrien-Marie Legendre (1786) sought the condition under which \(\delta^2 S \ge 0\). By considering localized high-frequency wiggles where \(\dot{\eta}^2 \gg \eta^2\), the \(P(t) \dot{\eta}^2\) term dominates the integral.

This yields Legendre’s Condition: \[\boxed{P(t) = \frac{\partial^2 L}{\partial \dot{q}^2} \ge 0 \quad \forall t \in [t_1, t_2]}\]

For standard mechanical systems with \(L = \frac{1}{2} m \dot{q}^2 - V(q)\): \[\frac{\partial^2 L}{\partial \dot{q}^2} = m > 0\] The Legendre condition is trivially satisfied whenever mass is positive. However, Legendre mistakenly believed this was sufficient to guarantee a minimum. It is not!

3. The Jacobi Condition and Conjugate Points (Sufficient Condition)

Carl Gustav Jacob Jacobi (1837) resolved the flaw in Legendre’s proof by discovering the phenomenon of conjugate points.

To test if \(\delta^2 S[\eta] > 0\) for all \(\eta(t)\), Jacobi solved the variational problem of minimizing \(\delta^2 S\) itself, leading to the Jacobi Accessory Equation: \[\boxed{-\frac{d}{dt}\left( P(t) \dot{u} \right) + Q(t) u = 0}\]

Definition of a Conjugate Point

Two points \(t_1\) and \(t^*\) along a trajectory are called conjugate points if there exists a non-trivial solution \(u(t)\) to the Jacobi equation such that: \[u(t_1) = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad u(t^*) = 0 \quad (\text{with } u(t) \not\equiv 0)\]

Geometrically, a conjugate point is a location where adjacent stationary paths emanating from \(t_1\) intersect each other again.

Jacobi’s Theorem

Let \(q(t)\) be a stationary path satisfying the strengthened Legendre condition (\(P(t) > 0\)): * True Local Minimum: If the interval \([t_1, t_2]\) contains no points conjugate to \(t_1\), then \(q(t)\) is a strict local minimum of the action \(S\). * Saddle Point: If the interval \((t_1, t_2)\) contains at least one conjugate point, the action \(S[q]\) is NOT a minimum; it is a saddle point in function space!

4. The Canonical Geometric Example: Geodesics on a Sphere

The profound distinction between stationary paths, minima, and saddle points is illustrated by the geometry of great circles on a two-dimensional sphere (such as Earth).

By Fermat’s/Hamilton’s principle, geodesics (paths of shortest distance) on a sphere are great circle arcs. Let point \(A\) be the North Pole (\(t_1 = 0\)).

  1. Before the South Pole (\(t_2 < \pi\)): Draw a great circle path to a city on the equator (e.g., Quito).
    • There are no other intersecting great circles between \(A\) and the equator.
    • The path contains no conjugate points.
    • The arc is a true minimum distance.
  2. At the South Pole (\(t^* = \pi\)): All meridians of longitude emanating from the North Pole simultaneously converge and intersect at the South Pole!
    • The South Pole is the conjugate point of the North Pole.
    • Infinitesimally neighboring geodesics refocus here.
  3. Beyond the South Pole (\(t_2 > \pi\)): Continue along the same meridian past the South Pole and back up into the Eastern Hemisphere.
    • The path is still an exact solution to the geodesic differential equations (\(\delta S = 0\); it is still a great circle).
    • However, because it has passed the conjugate point, it is no longer a minimum!
    • It is a saddle point. Shorter paths exist (simply traveling in the opposite direction from the North Pole).

5. Morse Theory and the Morse Index

In modern differential topology and mathematical physics, this classification is formalized by Morse Theory, developed by Marston Morse (1934).

For any stationary trajectory \(q(t)\), the second variation operator \(\mathcal{A} = -\frac{d}{dt}(P \frac{d}{dt}) + Q\) has a discrete spectrum of eigenvalues: \[\mathcal{A} u_k = \mu_k u_k\]

The Morse Index \(\iota\) of a trajectory is defined as the number of strictly negative eigenvalues of \(\mathcal{A}\): \[\iota = \text{number of } \mu_k < 0\]

  • \(\iota = 0 \iff\) The path is a strict local minimum (no conjugate points).
  • \(\iota = 1 \iff\) The path has passed \(1\) conjugate point (a saddle point with \(1\) unstable direction in function space).
  • \(\iota = k \iff\) The path has crossed \(k\) conjugate points.

The Morse Index Theorem

\[\boxed{\iota = \text{Total number of conjugate points along the interior of the trajectory } (t_1, t_2)}\]

This theorem connects pure differential topology with classical dynamics: simply by counting how many focal points a trajectory passes through, one immediately knows the topological index of the action functional in infinite-dimensional space.

Summary

  1. The Hessian Encodes Stability: Near equilibrium, \(V \approx \frac{1}{2}\mathbf{\eta}^T \mathbf{H} \mathbf{\eta}\). Positive-definite \(\mathbf{H} \succ 0\) guarantees real vibrational frequencies (stable normal modes), while negative eigenvalues generate exponential divergence.
  2. Euler-Lagrange Solves Stationary Points: First-order calculus (\(\delta S = 0\)) effortlessly converts global path optimization into local differential equations solvable forward in time.
  3. Function Spaces Have Infinite Dimensions: Testing for a minimum requires \(\delta^2 S[\eta] > 0\) for all infinite possible shapes of \(\eta(t)\). Actions never have true maxima because high-frequency wiggles make kinetic energy unbounded.
  4. Conjugate Points Create Saddle Points: The Legendre condition (\(m > 0\)) is merely necessary. By Jacobi’s theorem, a trajectory ceases to be a minimum the moment it passes its first conjugate point, transitioning into a saddle point as governed by Morse theory.
