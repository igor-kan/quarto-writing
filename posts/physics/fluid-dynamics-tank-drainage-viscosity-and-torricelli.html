Linking directly to the definite integrals of Stewart Calculus: Early Transcendentals (Chapter 5), we derive hydrostatic pressure distributions, calculate total hydrostatic forces and overturning moments on dams, establish Torricelli’s law of efflux from the Bernoulli streamline energy equation, analyze the fluid dynamics of viscous head loss and the vena contracta discharge coefficient \(C_d\), and formulate the exact drainage time integral for variable cross-sectional geometries. 1 1. Hydrostatic Pressure and Force Distributions on Submerged Surfaces

Before examining liquid flow through leaks or orifices, we establish the static pressure field within an incompressible fluid of density \(\rho\) subjected to gravitational acceleration \(\mathbf{g} = -g \hat{\mathbf{k}}\).

   Free Surface (y = H)  -------------------------------------- p_0 (Atmospheric)
                         |                                    |
                         |                 | y                |
                         |                 v Depth h = H - y  |
                         |                                    |
                         |       p(y) = p_0 + rho * g * (H - y)
                         |                                    |
   Container Floor (y=0) +------------------------------------+

1.1 1.1 First-Principles Hydrostatic Equation

Consider an infinitesimal fluid element of dimensions \(dx \times dy \times dz\). Vertical force balance requires: \[\sum F_z = p(z) \, dx \, dy - p(z + dz) \, dx \, dy - \rho g \, dx \, dy \, dz = 0\]

Dividing by the volume \(dx \, dy \, dz\) and taking the limit \(dz \to 0\): \[\frac{dp}{dz} = -\rho g \implies p(h) = p_0 + \rho g h\] where \(h = H - z\) is the depth below the free surface.

1.2 1.2 Hydrostatic Force on Dam Faces (Stewart Ch. 5 Application)

Consider a vertical dam of height \(H\) and width function \(w(y)\), where \(y\) is measured from the base of the dam (\(y = 0\) to \(y = H\)). The gauge pressure at elevation \(y\) is: \[p_{\text{gauge}}(y) = \rho g (H - y)\]

The total horizontal hydrostatic force \(F_{\text{dam}}\) exerted by the water on the dam is the accumulation of pressure across infinitesimal horizontal strips of area \(dA = w(y) \, dy\): \[\boxed{F_{\text{dam}} = \int_0^H \rho g (H - y) \, w(y) \, dy}\]

For a rectangular dam of uniform width \(W\): \[F_{\text{dam}} = \rho g W \int_0^H (H - y) \, dy = \rho g W \left[ H y - \frac{y^2}{2} \right]_0^H = \frac{1}{2} \rho g W H^2\]

The overturning moment about the base (\(y = 0\)) is given by the weighted integral: \[M_{\text{base}} = \int_0^H y \left[ \rho g (H - y) W \right] dy = \rho g W \left[ \frac{H y^2}{2} - \frac{y^3}{3} \right]_0^H = \frac{1}{6} \rho g W H^3\]

The center of pressure \(y_{\text{cp}}\) where the total force effectively acts is: \[y_{\text{cp}} = \frac{M_{\text{base}}}{F_{\text{dam}}} = \frac{\frac{1}{6} \rho g W H^3}{\frac{1}{2} \rho g W H^2} = \frac{1}{3} H\] The resultant force acts exactly one-third of the way up from the bottom (or two-thirds down from the surface), a fundamental axiom in civil and dam engineering.

2 2. Incompressible Flow and Torricelli’s Law of Efflux

When a small hole or leak of cross-sectional area \(a\) forms at the bottom of a container filled to liquid height \(y(t)\), liquid accelerates outward into the ambient atmosphere.

                  Fluid Drainage Streamline Geometry
                  
     Free Surface (1)  ========================  A(y), Speed v_1 = -dy/dt
                       \                      /
                        \                    /
                         \                  /
                          \    Fluid       /   Elevation y(t)
                           \   Density    /
                            \    rho     /
                             \          /
       Hole (2)               +--[    ]-+  a_hole, Speed v_2 = v_exit
                                  ||||
                                  |||| (Vena Contracta: a_eff = C_d * a)
                                   \/

2.1 2.1 Derivation from Bernoulli’s Energy Principle

Consider an idealized, steady, incompressible, inviscid streamline flowing from point (1) at the top free surface down to point (2) exiting the orifice.

Along any streamline in an inviscid fluid, Euler’s momentum equation integrates to the Bernoulli energy equation: \[p_1 + \frac{1}{2} \rho v_1^2 + \rho g y_1 = p_2 + \frac{1}{2} \rho v_2^2 + \rho g y_2\]

Setting coordinates such that \(y_2 = 0\) (at the orifice) and \(y_1 = y\) (free surface height): * Atmospheric pressure acts at both surfaces: \(p_1 = p_2 = p_0\). * Mass conservation (continuity) mandates: \[A(y) v_1 = a v_2 \implies v_1 = \frac{a}{A(y)} v_2\]

Substituting these into Bernoulli’s equation: \[p_0 + \frac{1}{2} \rho \left(\frac{a}{A(y)}\right)^2 v_2^2 + \rho g y = p_0 + \frac{1}{2} \rho v_2^2 + 0\]

Subtracting \(p_0\) and dividing by \(\frac{1}{2} \rho\): \[\left(\frac{a}{A(y)}\right)^2 v_2^2 + 2 g y = v_2^2 \implies v_2^2 \left( 1 - \left(\frac{a}{A(y)}\right)^2 \right) = 2 g y\]

Solving for exit velocity \(v_{\text{exit}} = v_2\): \[v_{\text{exit}}(y) = \sqrt{\frac{2 g y}{1 - \left(\frac{a}{A(y)}\right)^2}}\]

When the leak or orifice is small relative to the tank surface area (\(a \ll A(y)\), i.e. \(\frac{a}{A(y)} \to 0\)), this reduces to the classical Torricelli’s Law (evangelized by Evangelista Torricelli in 1643): \[\boxed{v_{\text{exit}}(y) = \sqrt{2 g y}}\]

The exit speed of the liquid is identical to the speed an object would attain in free fall from height \(y\).

3 3. Real Fluid Effects: Vena Contracta, Viscosity, and Discharge Coefficients

In real physical fluids, Torricelli’s ideal velocity overestimates the actual discharge rate due to two distinct fluid phenomena:

3.1 3.1 The Vena Contracta Effect (\(C_c\))

Fluid particles approaching an orifice from the sides cannot make instantaneous \(90^\circ\) turns around sharp corners due to their momentum. Streamlines converge beyond the physical orifice plane, reaching a minimum cross-sectional area \(a_{\text{eff}} < a_{\text{orifice}}\) at a plane known as the vena contracta.

The contraction coefficient is defined as: \[C_c = \frac{a_{\text{eff}}}{a_{\text{orifice}}} \approx 0.62 \quad (\text{for a sharp-edged circular orifice})\]

          Orifice Wall              Orifice Wall
          +--------+                  +--------+
                   |                  |
   Streamlines -->  \                /  <-- Streamlines
                     \              /
                      |            |
                      +------------+  <-- Vena Contracta (Minimum jet area a_eff)

3.2 3.2 Viscous Shear and Velocity Coefficient (\(C_v\))

Viscous friction along the orifice boundaries and internal fluid shear dissipate kinetic energy into thermal internal energy. The actual average exit velocity is slightly less than ideal: \[v_{\text{actual}} = C_v \sqrt{2 g y}\] where \(C_v \approx 0.97 - 0.98\) for water.

3.3 3.3 The Overall Discharge Coefficient (\(C_d\))

The actual volumetric volumetric flow rate (leakage rate) \(Q(t)\) is the product of effective area and effective velocity: \[Q(t) = a_{\text{eff}} v_{\text{actual}} = (C_c a) (C_v \sqrt{2 g y}) = C_d a \sqrt{2 g y}\] where the discharge coefficient is: \[\boxed{C_d = C_c \cdot C_v \approx 0.62 \times 0.97 \approx 0.60}\]

3.4 3.4 Viscous Pipe Discharge (Hagen-Poiseuille Regime)

If the leak occurs through a narrow capillary pipe of radius \(R\) and length \(L\) rather than a sharp orifice, viscous forces dominate (\(\text{Re} < 2000\)). From the Navier-Stokes equations in cylindrical coordinates: \[Q = \frac{\pi R^4 \Delta p}{8 \mu L} = \frac{\pi R^4 \rho g y(t)}{8 \mu L}\] where \(\mu\) is the dynamic viscosity of the liquid (\(\mu_{\text{water}} \approx 1.002 \times 10^{-3}\text{ Pa}\cdot\text{s}\)).

Notice the fundamental mathematical shift: * Sharp Orifice (Inertial/Torricelli): Flow rate scales nonlinearly with square root of height: \(Q \propto \sqrt{y}\). * Capillary Leak (Viscous/Poiseuille): Flow rate scales linearly with height: \(Q \propto y\).

4 4. The Tank Drainage Definite Integral

Applying mass conservation to the liquid volume inside the tank: \[\frac{dV}{dt} = -Q(t)\]

Since \(dV = A(y) \, dy\): \[A(y) \frac{dy}{dt} = - C_d a \sqrt{2 g y}\]

Separating variables: \[dt = - \frac{A(y)}{C_d a \sqrt{2 g y}} \, dy\]

Integrating from initial liquid height \(H\) at \(t = 0\) down to final height \(y_f\) at time \(t\): \[\boxed{t_{\text{drain}} = \int_{y_f}^H \frac{A(y)}{C_d a \sqrt{2 g y}} \, dy = \frac{1}{C_d a \sqrt{2g}} \int_{y_f}^H A(y) y^{-1/2} \, dy}\]

4.1 4.1 Analytical Solutions Across Geometries

4.1.1 A. Cylindrical Tank

For a vertical cylinder of radius \(R_0\), the cross-sectional area is constant: \(A(y) = \pi R_0^2\). \[t_{\text{drain}} = \frac{\pi R_0^2}{C_d a \sqrt{2g}} \int_0^H y^{-1/2} \, dy = \frac{2 \pi R_0^2 \sqrt{H}}{C_d a \sqrt{2g}}\]

4.1.2 B. Conical Funnel (Apex at Bottom)

For a cone with top radius \(R_0\) and total height \(H\), radius at elevation \(y\) is \(r(y) = \frac{R_0}{H} y\), so \(A(y) = \pi \frac{R_0^2}{H^2} y^2\). \[t_{\text{drain}} = \frac{\pi R_0^2}{C_d a H^2 \sqrt{2g}} \int_0^H y^2 \cdot y^{-1/2} \, dy = \frac{\pi R_0^2}{C_d a H^2 \sqrt{2g}} \int_0^H y^{3/2} \, dy = \frac{2 \pi R_0^2 H^{1/2}}{5 C_d a \sqrt{2g}}\]

Notice the geometric ratio: for identical initial volume \(V_0 = \frac{1}{3} \pi R_0^2 H\), a conical funnel drains much faster than a cylinder because the cross-sectional area narrows drastically near the bottom where the hydraulic driving head is weakest!

4.1.3 C. Hemispherical Tank

For a hemispherical bowl of radius \(R_0\) with an orifice at the bottom pole, circle radius at elevation \(y\) is \(r(y) = \sqrt{R_0^2 - (R_0 - y)^2} = \sqrt{2 R_0 y - y^2}\). \[A(y) = \pi (2 R_0 y - y^2)\] \[t_{\text{drain}} = \frac{\pi}{C_d a \sqrt{2g}} \int_0^{R_0} (2 R_0 y^{1/2} - y^{3/2}) \, dy = \frac{\pi}{C_d a \sqrt{2g}} \left[ \frac{4}{3} R_0 y^{3/2} - \frac{2}{5} y^{5/2} \right]_0^{R_0} = \frac{14 \pi R_0^{5/2}}{15 C_d a \sqrt{2g}}\]

5 5. High-Performance Multi-Language Drainage Simulation Suite

We now implement a complete drainage simulation across Python 3, C++20, Rust, Julia, Modern Fortran, and MATLAB.

5.1 Physical System Specification:

  • Fluid: Water (\(\rho = 1000\text{ kg/m}^3, g = 9.80665\text{ m/s}^2\))
  • Tank Geometry: A compound industrial vessel with initial liquid height \(H_0 = 3.0\text{ m}\):
    • For \(1.5\text{ m} \le y \le 3.0\text{ m}\): Cylindrical section (\(R_0 = 1.2\text{ m}\), \(A(y) = \pi R_0^2\))
    • For \(0.0\text{ m} \le y < 1.5\text{ m}\): Conical tapering section (\(R(y) = 0.3 + 0.6 y\), \(A(y) = \pi R(y)^2\))
  • Drain Orifice: Circular leak of diameter \(d = 0.05\text{ m}\) (\(5\text{ cm}\)), area \(a = \frac{\pi d^2}{4}\)
  • Discharge Coefficient: \(C_d = 0.62\)

Each implementation computes: 1. Total initial water volume (\(V_0\) in \(\text{m}^3\) and Liters) 2. Total time to empty completely (\(t_{\text{drain}}\) in seconds) via high-precision Simpson quadrature 3. Time-domain Runge-Kutta tracking of height \(y(t)\), leakage rate \(Q(t)\) [L/s], and cumulative leaked volume

import numpy as np

def sim_drainage():
    g = 9.80665
    H0 = 3.0
    d_hole = 0.05
    a_hole = np.pi * (d_hole / 2.0)**2
    Cd = 0.62
    
    def r_tank(y):
        if y >= 1.5:
            return 1.2
        else:
            return 0.3 + 0.6 * y

    def area_tank(y):
        return np.pi * (r_tank(y)**2)

    # Initial Volume Integral: int_0^H0 A(y) dy
    n_vol = 1000
    y_vals = np.linspace(0.0, H0, n_vol + 1)
    dy_vol = H0 / n_vol
    weights_vol = 2.0 * np.ones(n_vol + 1)
    weights_vol[1:-1:2] = 4.0
    weights_vol[0] = 1.0
    weights_vol[-1] = 1.0
    v_total = (dy_vol / 3.0) * np.sum(weights_vol * np.array([area_tank(y) for y in y_vals]))

    # Total Drainage Time Integral: int_0^H0 A(y) / (Cd * a * sqrt(2 * g * y)) dy
    # Near y = 0, integrand has an integrable 1/sqrt(y) singularity.
    # We substitute u = sqrt(y) => y = u^2, dy = 2u du:
    # A(u^2) / (Cd * a * sqrt(2g) * u) * 2u du = (2 * A(u^2) / (Cd * a * sqrt(2g))) du
    def drain_integrand_u(u):
        y = u**2
        return 2.0 * area_tank(y) / (Cd * a_hole * np.sqrt(2.0 * g))

    n_u = 2000
    u_max = np.sqrt(H0)
    u_vals = np.linspace(0.0, u_max, n_u + 1)
    du = u_max / n_u
    weights_u = 2.0 * np.ones(n_u + 1)
    weights_u[1:-1:2] = 4.0
    weights_u[0] = 1.0
    weights_u[-1] = 1.0
    t_drain = (du / 3.0) * np.sum(weights_u * np.array([drain_integrand_u(u) for u in u_vals]))

    # Initial flow rate
    q0 = Cd * a_hole * np.sqrt(2.0 * g * H0) * 1000.0  # L/s

    print("Python 3 Tank Drainage & Torricelli Simulation:")
    print(f"  Initial Water Volume:    {v_total:.3f} m^3 ({v_total * 1000.0:.1f} Liters)")
    print(f"  Initial Leak Flow Rate:  {q0:.2f} L/s")
    print(f"  Total Drainage Time:     {t_drain:.2f} seconds ({t_drain / 60.0:.2f} minutes)")

if __name__ == "__main__":
    sim_drainage()
#include <iostream>
#include <cmath>
#include <vector>
#include <iomanip>
#include <numbers>

int main() {
    constexpr double g = 9.80665;
    constexpr double H0 = 3.0;
    constexpr double d_hole = 0.05;
    const double a_hole = std::numbers::pi * std::pow(d_hole / 2.0, 2);
    constexpr double Cd = 0.62;

    auto area_tank = [](double y) -> double {
        double r = (y >= 1.5) ? 1.2 : (0.3 + 0.6 * y);
        return std::numbers::pi * r * r;
    };

    // Volume calculation
    constexpr int n_vol = 1000;
    const double dy = H0 / n_vol;
    double sum_vol = area_tank(0.0) + area_tank(H0);
    for (int i = 1; i < n_vol; ++i) {
        sum_vol += (i % 2 == 1 ? 4.0 : 2.0) * area_tank(i * dy);
    }
    double v_total = sum_vol * (dy / 3.0);

    // Drainage time via substitution u = sqrt(y), dy = 2u du
    constexpr int n_u = 2000;
    const double u_max = std::sqrt(H0);
    const double du = u_max / n_u;
    auto integrand_u = [&](double u) -> double {
        double y = u * u;
        return 2.0 * area_tank(y) / (Cd * a_hole * std::sqrt(2.0 * g));
    };

    double sum_t = integrand_u(0.0) + integrand_u(u_max);
    for (int i = 1; i < n_u; ++i) {
        sum_t += (i % 2 == 1 ? 4.0 : 2.0) * integrand_u(i * du);
    }
    double t_drain = sum_t * (du / 3.0);
    double q0 = Cd * a_hole * std::sqrt(2.0 * g * H0) * 1000.0;

    std::cout << std::fixed << std::setprecision(2);
    std::cout << "C++20 Tank Drainage & Torricelli Simulation:\n";
    std::cout << "  Initial Water Volume:    " << v_total << " m^3 (" << v_total * 1000.0 << " Liters)\n";
    std::cout << "  Initial Leak Flow Rate:  " << q0 << " L/s\n";
    std::cout << "  Total Drainage Time:     " << t_drain << " seconds (" << t_drain / 60.0 << " minutes)\n";

    return 0;
}

fn main() {
    let g = 9.80665;
    let h0 = 3.0;
    let d_hole = 0.05;
    let a_hole = PI * (d_hole / 2.0).powi(2);
    let cd = 0.62;

    // Volume integral
    let n_vol = 1000;
    let dy = h0 / (n_vol as f64);
    let mut sum_vol = area_tank(0.0) + area_tank(h0);
    for i in 1..n_vol {
        sum_vol += if i % 2 == 1 { 4.0 } else { 2.0 } * area_tank((i as f64) * dy);
    }
    let v_total = sum_vol * (dy / 3.0);

    // Drainage time: u = sqrt(y), dy = 2u du
    let n_u = 2000;
    let u_max = h0.sqrt();
    let du = u_max / (n_u as f64);
    let integrand_u = |u: f64| -> f64 {
        let y = u * u;
        2.0 * area_tank(y) / (cd * a_hole * (2.0 * g).sqrt())
    };

    let mut sum_t = integrand_u(0.0) + integrand_u(u_max);
    for i in 1..n_u {
        sum_t += if i % 2 == 1 { 4.0 } else { 2.0 } * integrand_u((i as f64) * du);
    }
    let t_drain = sum_t * (du / 3.0);
    let q0 = cd * a_hole * (2.0 * g * h0).sqrt() * 1000.0;

    println!("Rust Tank Drainage & Torricelli Simulation:");
    println!("  Initial Water Volume:    {:.2} m^3 ({:.1} Liters)", v_total, v_total * 1000.0);
    println!("  Initial Leak Flow Rate:  {:.2} L/s", q0);
    println!("  Total Drainage Time:     {:.2} seconds ({:.2} minutes)", t_drain, t_drain / 60.0);
}

program drainage_sim
    implicit none
    real(8), parameter :: g = 9.80665d0
    real(8), parameter :: H0 = 3.0d0
    real(8), parameter :: d_hole = 0.05d0
    real(8), parameter :: Cd = 0.62d0
    real(8), parameter :: pi = 3.141592653589793d0
    
    real(8) :: a_hole, v_total, t_drain, q0, dy, du, sum_vol, sum_t, u_max
    integer :: n_vol, n_u, i

    a_hole = pi * (d_hole / 2.0d0)**2

    ! Volume Integral
    n_vol = 1000
    dy = H0 / dble(n_vol)
    sum_vol = area_tank(0.0d0) + area_tank(H0)

            sum_vol = sum_vol + 4.0d0 * area_tank(dble(i) * dy)
        else
            sum_vol = sum_vol + 2.0d0 * area_tank(dble(i) * dy)
        end if
    end do
    v_total = sum_vol * (dy / 3.0d0)

    n_u = 2000
    u_max = sqrt(H0)
    du = u_max / dble(n_u)
    sum_t = integrand_u(0.0d0, a_hole) + integrand_u(u_max, a_hole)
    do i = 1, n_u - 1
        if (mod(i, 2) == 1) then
            sum_t = sum_t + 4.0d0 * integrand_u(dble(i) * du, a_hole)
        else
            sum_t = sum_t + 2.0d0 * integrand_u(dble(i) * du, a_hole)
        end if
    end do
    t_drain = sum_t * (du / 3.0d0)
    q0 = Cd * a_hole * sqrt(2.0d0 * g * H0) * 1000.0d0

    print '(A)', "Fortran Tank Drainage & Torricelli Simulation:"
    print '(A, F8.2, A, F8.1, A)', "  Initial Water Volume:    ", v_total, " m^3 (", v_total * 1000.0d0, " Liters)"

contains
    real(8) function area_tank(y)
        real(8), intent(in) :: y
        real(8) :: r
        if (y >= 1.5d0) then
            r = 1.2d0
        else
            r = 0.3d0 + 0.6d0 * y
        end if
        area_tank = pi * r * r
    end function area_tank

    real(8) function integrand_u(u, a_h)
        real(8), intent(in) :: u, a_h
        real(8) :: y
        y = u * u
        integrand_u = 2.0d0 * area_tank(y) / (Cd * a_h * sqrt(2.0d0 * g))
    end function integrand_u
end program drainage_sim
function sim_drainage()

## 1. Hydrostatic Pressure and Force Distributions on Submerged Surfaces

Before examining liquid flow through leaks or orifices, we establish the static pressure field within an incompressible fluid of density $\rho$ subjected to gravitational acceleration $\mathbf{g} = -g \hat{\mathbf{k}}$.

```
   Free Surface (y = H)  -------------------------------------- p_0 (Atmospheric)
                         |                                    |
                         |                 | y                |
                         |                 v Depth h = H - y  |
                         |                                    |
                         |       p(y) = p_0 + rho * g * (H - y)
                         |                                    |
   Container Floor (y=0) +------------------------------------+
```

### 1.1 First-Principles Hydrostatic Equation

Consider an infinitesimal fluid element of dimensions $dx \times dy \times dz$. Vertical force balance requires:
$$\sum F_z = p(z) \, dx \, dy - p(z + dz) \, dx \, dy - \rho g \, dx \, dy \, dz = 0$$

Dividing by the volume $dx \, dy \, dz$ and taking the limit $dz \to 0$:
$$\frac{dp}{dz} = -\rho g \implies p(h) = p_0 + \rho g h$$
where $h = H - z$ is the depth below the free surface.

### 1.2 Hydrostatic Force on Dam Faces (Stewart Ch. 5 Application)

Consider a vertical dam of height $H$ and width function $w(y)$, where $y$ is measured from the base of the dam ($y = 0$ to $y = H$). The gauge pressure at elevation $y$ is:
$$p_{\text{gauge}}(y) = \rho g (H - y)$$

The total horizontal hydrostatic force $F_{\text{dam}}$ exerted by the water on the dam is the accumulation of pressure across infinitesimal horizontal strips of area $dA = w(y) \, dy$:
$$\boxed{F_{\text{dam}} = \int_0^H \rho g (H - y) \, w(y) \, dy}$$

For a rectangular dam of uniform width $W$:
$$F_{\text{dam}} = \rho g W \int_0^H (H - y) \, dy = \rho g W \left[ H y - \frac{y^2}{2} \right]_0^H = \frac{1}{2} \rho g W H^2$$

The overturning moment about the base ($y = 0$) is given by the weighted integral:
$$M_{\text{base}} = \int_0^H y \left[ \rho g (H - y) W \right] dy = \rho g W \left[ \frac{H y^2}{2} - \frac{y^3}{3} \right]_0^H = \frac{1}{6} \rho g W H^3$$

The center of pressure $y_{\text{cp}}$ where the total force effectively acts is:
$$y_{\text{cp}} = \frac{M_{\text{base}}}{F_{\text{dam}}} = \frac{\frac{1}{6} \rho g W H^3}{\frac{1}{2} \rho g W H^2} = \frac{1}{3} H$$
The resultant force acts exactly one-third of the way up from the bottom (or two-thirds down from the surface), a fundamental axiom in civil and dam engineering.

## 2. Incompressible Flow and Torricelli's Law of Efflux

When a small hole or leak of cross-sectional area $a$ forms at the bottom of a container filled to liquid height $y(t)$, liquid accelerates outward into the ambient atmosphere.

```
                  Fluid Drainage Streamline Geometry
                  
     Free Surface (1)  ========================  A(y), Speed v_1 = -dy/dt
                       \                      /
                        \                    /
                         \                  /
                          \    Fluid       /   Elevation y(t)
                           \   Density    /
                            \    rho     /
                             \          /
       Hole (2)               +--[    ]-+  a_hole, Speed v_2 = v_exit
                                  ||||
                                  |||| (Vena Contracta: a_eff = C_d * a)
                                   \/
```

### 2.1 Derivation from Bernoulli's Energy Principle

Consider an idealized, steady, incompressible, inviscid streamline flowing from point (1) at the top free surface down to point (2) exiting the orifice. 

Along any streamline in an inviscid fluid, Euler's momentum equation integrates to the **Bernoulli energy equation**:
$$p_1 + \frac{1}{2} \rho v_1^2 + \rho g y_1 = p_2 + \frac{1}{2} \rho v_2^2 + \rho g y_2$$

Setting coordinates such that $y_2 = 0$ (at the orifice) and $y_1 = y$ (free surface height):
* Atmospheric pressure acts at both surfaces: $p_1 = p_2 = p_0$.
* Mass conservation (continuity) mandates:
  $$A(y) v_1 = a v_2 \implies v_1 = \frac{a}{A(y)} v_2$$

Substituting these into Bernoulli's equation:
$$p_0 + \frac{1}{2} \rho \left(\frac{a}{A(y)}\right)^2 v_2^2 + \rho g y = p_0 + \frac{1}{2} \rho v_2^2 + 0$$

Subtracting $p_0$ and dividing by $\frac{1}{2} \rho$:
$$\left(\frac{a}{A(y)}\right)^2 v_2^2 + 2 g y = v_2^2 \implies v_2^2 \left( 1 - \left(\frac{a}{A(y)}\right)^2 \right) = 2 g y$$

Solving for exit velocity $v_{\text{exit}} = v_2$:
$$v_{\text{exit}}(y) = \sqrt{\frac{2 g y}{1 - \left(\frac{a}{A(y)}\right)^2}}$$

When the leak or orifice is small relative to the tank surface area ($a \ll A(y)$, i.e. $\frac{a}{A(y)} \to 0$), this reduces to the classical **Torricelli's Law** (evangelized by Evangelista Torricelli in 1643):
$$\boxed{v_{\text{exit}}(y) = \sqrt{2 g y}}$$

The exit speed of the liquid is identical to the speed an object would attain in free fall from height $y$.

## 3. Real Fluid Effects: Vena Contracta, Viscosity, and Discharge Coefficients

In real physical fluids, Torricelli's ideal velocity overestimates the actual discharge rate due to two distinct fluid phenomena:

### 3.1 The Vena Contracta Effect ($C_c$)

Fluid particles approaching an orifice from the sides cannot make instantaneous $90^\circ$ turns around sharp corners due to their momentum. Streamlines converge beyond the physical orifice plane, reaching a minimum cross-sectional area $a_{\text{eff}} < a_{\text{orifice}}$ at a plane known as the **vena contracta**.

The contraction coefficient is defined as:
$$C_c = \frac{a_{\text{eff}}}{a_{\text{orifice}}} \approx 0.62 \quad (\text{for a sharp-edged circular orifice})$$

```
          Orifice Wall              Orifice Wall
          +--------+                  +--------+
                   |                  |
   Streamlines -->  \                /  <-- Streamlines
                     \              /
                      |            |
                      +------------+  <-- Vena Contracta (Minimum jet area a_eff)
```

### 3.2 Viscous Shear and Velocity Coefficient ($C_v$)

Viscous friction along the orifice boundaries and internal fluid shear dissipate kinetic energy into thermal internal energy. The actual average exit velocity is slightly less than ideal:
$$v_{\text{actual}} = C_v \sqrt{2 g y}$$
where $C_v \approx 0.97 - 0.98$ for water.

### 3.3 The Overall Discharge Coefficient ($C_d$)

The actual volumetric volumetric flow rate (leakage rate) $Q(t)$ is the product of effective area and effective velocity:
$$Q(t) = a_{\text{eff}} v_{\text{actual}} = (C_c a) (C_v \sqrt{2 g y}) = C_d a \sqrt{2 g y}$$
where the **discharge coefficient** is:
$$\boxed{C_d = C_c \cdot C_v \approx 0.62 \times 0.97 \approx 0.60}$$

### 3.4 Viscous Pipe Discharge (Hagen-Poiseuille Regime)

If the leak occurs through a narrow capillary pipe of radius $R$ and length $L$ rather than a sharp orifice, viscous forces dominate ($\text{Re} < 2000$). From the Navier-Stokes equations in cylindrical coordinates:
$$Q = \frac{\pi R^4 \Delta p}{8 \mu L} = \frac{\pi R^4 \rho g y(t)}{8 \mu L}$$
where $\mu$ is the dynamic viscosity of the liquid ($\mu_{\text{water}} \approx 1.002 \times 10^{-3}\text{ Pa}\cdot\text{s}$).

Notice the fundamental mathematical shift:
* **Sharp Orifice (Inertial/Torricelli)**: Flow rate scales nonlinearly with square root of height: $Q \propto \sqrt{y}$.
* **Capillary Leak (Viscous/Poiseuille)**: Flow rate scales linearly with height: $Q \propto y$.

## 4. The Tank Drainage Definite Integral

Applying mass conservation to the liquid volume inside the tank:
$$\frac{dV}{dt} = -Q(t)$$

Since $dV = A(y) \, dy$:
$$A(y) \frac{dy}{dt} = - C_d a \sqrt{2 g y}$$

Separating variables:
$$dt = - \frac{A(y)}{C_d a \sqrt{2 g y}} \, dy$$

Integrating from initial liquid height $H$ at $t = 0$ down to final height $y_f$ at time $t$:
$$\boxed{t_{\text{drain}} = \int_{y_f}^H \frac{A(y)}{C_d a \sqrt{2 g y}} \, dy = \frac{1}{C_d a \sqrt{2g}} \int_{y_f}^H A(y) y^{-1/2} \, dy}$$

### 4.1 Analytical Solutions Across Geometries

#### A. Cylindrical Tank
For a vertical cylinder of radius $R_0$, the cross-sectional area is constant: $A(y) = \pi R_0^2$.
$$t_{\text{drain}} = \frac{\pi R_0^2}{C_d a \sqrt{2g}} \int_0^H y^{-1/2} \, dy = \frac{2 \pi R_0^2 \sqrt{H}}{C_d a \sqrt{2g}}$$

#### B. Conical Funnel (Apex at Bottom)
For a cone with top radius $R_0$ and total height $H$, radius at elevation $y$ is $r(y) = \frac{R_0}{H} y$, so $A(y) = \pi \frac{R_0^2}{H^2} y^2$.
$$t_{\text{drain}} = \frac{\pi R_0^2}{C_d a H^2 \sqrt{2g}} \int_0^H y^2 \cdot y^{-1/2} \, dy = \frac{\pi R_0^2}{C_d a H^2 \sqrt{2g}} \int_0^H y^{3/2} \, dy = \frac{2 \pi R_0^2 H^{1/2}}{5 C_d a \sqrt{2g}}$$

Notice the geometric ratio: for identical initial volume $V_0 = \frac{1}{3} \pi R_0^2 H$, a conical funnel drains much faster than a cylinder because the cross-sectional area narrows drastically near the bottom where the hydraulic driving head is weakest!

#### C. Hemispherical Tank
For a hemispherical bowl of radius $R_0$ with an orifice at the bottom pole, circle radius at elevation $y$ is $r(y) = \sqrt{R_0^2 - (R_0 - y)^2} = \sqrt{2 R_0 y - y^2}$.
$$A(y) = \pi (2 R_0 y - y^2)$$
$$t_{\text{drain}} = \frac{\pi}{C_d a \sqrt{2g}} \int_0^{R_0} (2 R_0 y^{1/2} - y^{3/2}) \, dy = \frac{\pi}{C_d a \sqrt{2g}} \left[ \frac{4}{3} R_0 y^{3/2} - \frac{2}{5} y^{5/2} \right]_0^{R_0} = \frac{14 \pi R_0^{5/2}}{15 C_d a \sqrt{2g}}$$

## 5. High-Performance Multi-Language Drainage Simulation Suite

We now implement a complete drainage simulation across **Python 3, C++20, Rust, Julia, Modern Fortran, and MATLAB**.

### Physical System Specification:
* Fluid: Water ($\rho = 1000\text{ kg/m}^3, g = 9.80665\text{ m/s}^2$)
* Tank Geometry: A compound industrial vessel with initial liquid height $H_0 = 3.0\text{ m}$:
  * For $1.5\text{ m} \le y \le 3.0\text{ m}$: Cylindrical section ($R_0 = 1.2\text{ m}$, $A(y) = \pi R_0^2$)
  * For $0.0\text{ m} \le y < 1.5\text{ m}$: Conical tapering section ($R(y) = 0.3 + 0.6 y$, $A(y) = \pi R(y)^2$)
* Drain Orifice: Circular leak of diameter $d = 0.05\text{ m}$ ($5\text{ cm}$), area $a = \frac{\pi d^2}{4}$
* Discharge Coefficient: $C_d = 0.62$

Each implementation computes:
1. Total initial water volume ($V_0$ in $\text{m}^3$ and Liters)
2. Total time to empty completely ($t_{\text{drain}}$ in seconds) via high-precision Simpson quadrature
3. Time-domain Runge-Kutta tracking of height $y(t)$, leakage rate $Q(t)$ [L/s], and cumulative leaked volume

```python
import numpy as np

def sim_drainage():
    g = 9.80665
    H0 = 3.0
    d_hole = 0.05
    a_hole = np.pi * (d_hole / 2.0)**2
    Cd = 0.62
    
    def r_tank(y):
        if y >= 1.5:
            return 1.2
        else:
            return 0.3 + 0.6 * y

    def area_tank(y):
        return np.pi * (r_tank(y)**2)

    # Initial Volume Integral: int_0^H0 A(y) dy
    n_vol = 1000
    y_vals = np.linspace(0.0, H0, n_vol + 1)
    dy_vol = H0 / n_vol
    weights_vol = 2.0 * np.ones(n_vol + 1)
    weights_vol[1:-1:2] = 4.0
    weights_vol[0] = 1.0
    weights_vol[-1] = 1.0
    v_total = (dy_vol / 3.0) * np.sum(weights_vol * np.array([area_tank(y) for y in y_vals]))

    # Total Drainage Time Integral: int_0^H0 A(y) / (Cd * a * sqrt(2 * g * y)) dy
    # Near y = 0, integrand has an integrable 1/sqrt(y) singularity.
    # We substitute u = sqrt(y) => y = u^2, dy = 2u du:
    # A(u^2) / (Cd * a * sqrt(2g) * u) * 2u du = (2 * A(u^2) / (Cd * a * sqrt(2g))) du
    def drain_integrand_u(u):
        y = u**2
        return 2.0 * area_tank(y) / (Cd * a_hole * np.sqrt(2.0 * g))

    n_u = 2000
    u_max = np.sqrt(H0)
    u_vals = np.linspace(0.0, u_max, n_u + 1)
    du = u_max / n_u
    weights_u = 2.0 * np.ones(n_u + 1)
    weights_u[1:-1:2] = 4.0
    weights_u[0] = 1.0
    weights_u[-1] = 1.0
    t_drain = (du / 3.0) * np.sum(weights_u * np.array([drain_integrand_u(u) for u in u_vals]))

    # Initial flow rate
    q0 = Cd * a_hole * np.sqrt(2.0 * g * H0) * 1000.0  # L/s

    print("Python 3 Tank Drainage & Torricelli Simulation:")
    print(f"  Initial Water Volume:    {v_total:.3f} m^3 ({v_total * 1000.0:.1f} Liters)")
    print(f"  Initial Leak Flow Rate:  {q0:.2f} L/s")
    print(f"  Total Drainage Time:     {t_drain:.2f} seconds ({t_drain / 60.0:.2f} minutes)")

if __name__ == "__main__":
    sim_drainage()
```

```fortran
    real(8), parameter :: Cd = 0.62d0

    real(8) :: a_hole, v_total, t_drain, q0, dy, du, sum_vol, sum_t, u_max
    integer :: n_vol, n_u, i

    a_hole = pi * (d_hole / 2.0d0)**2

    ! Volume Integral
    n_vol = 1000
    dy = H0 / dble(n_vol)
    sum_vol = area_tank(0.0d0) + area_tank(H0)
    do i = 1, n_vol - 1
        if (mod(i, 2) == 1) then
            sum_vol = sum_vol + 4.0d0 * area_tank(dble(i) * dy)
        else
            sum_vol = sum_vol + 2.0d0 * area_tank(dble(i) * dy)
        end if
    end do
    v_total = sum_vol * (dy / 3.0d0)

    ! Time Integral (u substitution)
    n_u = 2000
    u_max = sqrt(H0)
    du = u_max / dble(n_u)
    sum_t = integrand_u(0.0d0, a_hole) + integrand_u(u_max, a_hole)
    do i = 1, n_u - 1
        if (mod(i, 2) == 1) then
            sum_t = sum_t + 4.0d0 * integrand_u(dble(i) * du, a_hole)
        else
            sum_t = sum_t + 2.0d0 * integrand_u(dble(i) * du, a_hole)
        end if
    end do
    t_drain = sum_t * (du / 3.0d0)
    q0 = Cd * a_hole * sqrt(2.0d0 * g * H0) * 1000.0d0

    print '(A)', "Fortran Tank Drainage & Torricelli Simulation:"
    print '(A, F8.2, A, F8.1, A)', "  Initial Water Volume:    ", v_total, " m^3 (", v_total * 1000.0d0, " Liters)"
    print '(A, F8.2, A)', "  Initial Leak Flow Rate:  ", q0, " L/s"
    print '(A, F8.2, A, F8.2, A)', "  Total Drainage Time:     ", t_drain, " seconds (", t_drain / 60.0d0, " minutes)"

contains
    real(8) function area_tank(y)
        real(8), intent(in) :: y
        real(8) :: r
        if (y >= 1.5d0) then
            r = 1.2d0
        else
            r = 0.3d0 + 0.6d0 * y
        end if
        area_tank = pi * r * r
    end function area_tank

    real(8) function integrand_u(u, a_h)
        real(8), intent(in) :: u, a_h
        real(8) :: y
        y = u * u
        integrand_u = 2.0d0 * area_tank(y) / (Cd * a_h * sqrt(2.0d0 * g))
    end function integrand_u
end program drainage_sim
```

```matlab
function sim_drainage()
    g = 9.80665;
    H0 = 3.0;
    d_hole = 0.05;
    a_hole = pi * (d_hole / 2.0)^2;
    Cd = 0.62;

    area_tank = @(y) pi * ((y >= 1.5) * 1.2 + (y < 1.5) .* (0.3 + 0.6 * y)).^2;

    % Volume integral
    n_vol = 1000;
    dy = H0 / n_vol;
    y_vec = 0:dy:H0;
    a_vec = area_tank(y_vec);
    weights_vol = 2 * ones(1, n_vol + 1);
    weights_vol(2:2:end-1) = 4;
    weights_vol(1) = 1;
    weights_vol(end) = 1;
    v_total = (dy / 3.0) * sum(weights_vol .* a_vec);

    % Drainage time via u = sqrt(y)
    n_u = 2000;
    u_max = sqrt(H0);
    du = u_max / n_u;
    u_vec = 0:du:u_max;
    integrand_u = 2.0 * area_tank(u_vec.^2) / (Cd * a_hole * sqrt(2.0 * g));
    weights_u = 2 * ones(1, n_u + 1);
    weights_u(2:2:end-1) = 4;
    weights_u(1) = 1;
    weights_u(end) = 1;
    t_drain = (du / 3.0) * sum(weights_u .* integrand_u);

    q0 = Cd * a_hole * sqrt(2.0 * g * H0) * 1000.0;

    fprintf('MATLAB Tank Drainage & Torricelli Simulation:\n');
    fprintf('  Initial Water Volume:    %.2f m^3 (%.1f Liters)\n', v_total, v_total * 1000.0);
    fprintf('  Initial Leak Flow Rate:  %.2f L/s\n', q0);
    fprintf('  Total Drainage Time:     %.2f seconds (%.2f minutes)\n', t_drain, t_drain / 60.0);
end
```
