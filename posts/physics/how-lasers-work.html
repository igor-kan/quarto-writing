How Lasers Work: The Quantum Mechanics of Coherence, the Helium-Neon Laser, and the True Meaning of Watts

From Einstein’s stimulated emission and population inversion to gas discharge dynamics and the dramatic difference between continuous power and ultrafast peak gigawatts.

Physics
Optics
Quantum Mechanics
Electromagnetism
Published

September 8, 2026

Few 20th-century inventions have transformed modern civilization quite like the LASER. An acronym for Light Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation, the laser is ubiquitous: scanning barcodes, transmitting petabytes across transoceanic fiber-optic cables, cutting thick industrial steel, performing corneal surgery, and probing the ultrafast quantum dynamics of electrons.

Yet to the uninitiated, lasers can seem almost magical. How does an electrical discharge in a small glass tube produce an infinitely straight, monochromatic beam of light? And why can a seemingly puny 5-milliwatt (0.005 W) handheld pointer burn your retina, while a 100-watt household lightbulb illuminating an entire living room is completely harmless to look at?

This article explores the rigorous physics of lasers, the elegant atomic mechanisms powering the classic Helium-Neon (He-Ne) gas laser, and what “watts” actually signify in optical physics.

1. The Core Physics: Einstein and the Three Radiative Processes

In 1917, Albert Einstein laid the theoretical foundation for lasers in his paper “On the Quantum Theory of Radiation”. He demonstrated that when an atom interacts with electromagnetic radiation, exactly three distinct processes can take place between two energy levels \(E_1\) (lower) and \(E_2\) (upper), separated by energy \(\Delta E = E_2 - E_1 = h\nu\):

  1. Stimulated Absorption: An incoming photon with frequency \(\nu = (E_2 - E_1)/h\) is absorbed by an electron in the ground state \(E_1\), elevating it to excited state \(E_2\). The rate of absorption is proportional to the spectral energy density \(\rho(\nu)\) of the radiation and the population density \(N_1\): \[R_{\text{abs}} = B_{12} N_1 \rho(\nu)\]
  2. Spontaneous Emission: An excited electron at \(E_2\) will naturally decay to \(E_1\) after some characteristic lifetime \(\tau\), emitting a photon of energy \(h\nu\). Because this decay occurs randomly due to quantum vacuum fluctuations, the emitted photon has a random direction, random phase, and random polarization. This is the physics of ordinary incoherent light (incandescent filaments, candle flames, fluorescent tubes): \[R_{\text{spon}} = A_{21} N_2\]
  3. Stimulated Emission (The Heart of the Laser): If an incident photon of energy \(h\nu\) encounters an atom that is already in the excited state \(E_2\), it perturbs the electron wave function, inducing the electron to drop to \(E_1\) and release an identical secondary photon. \[\boxed{R_{\text{stim}} = B_{21} N_2 \rho(\nu)}\]

Crucially, the stimulated photon is a quantum clone of the incoming photon: it shares the exact same frequency (\(\nu\)), phase (\(\phi\)), polarization (\(\mathbf{E}\)), and propagation direction (\(\mathbf{k}\)). One photon enters, and two mutually coherent photons emerge.

The Einstein Relations

By balancing these three rates in thermal equilibrium according to the Boltzmann distribution (\(N_2 / N_1 = e^{-\Delta E / k_B T}\)) and equating the result to Planck’s blackbody radiation law, Einstein proved two vital identities: \[B_{12} = B_{21}\] \[\frac{A_{21}}{B_{21}} = \frac{8\pi h \nu^3}{c^3}\]

The equality \(B_{12} = B_{21}\) reveals a profound dilemma: the intrinsic probability of stimulated emission is exactly equal to the probability of absorption.

If a photon travels through a medium: * If it hits an atom in state 1, it is absorbed (light attenuates). * If it hits an atom in state 2, it triggers stimulated emission (light amplifies).

The net change in beam intensity \(I(z)\) as it propagates along path \(z\) is governed by the Beer-Lambert-like gain equation: \[\frac{dI}{dz} = \sigma_{21} (N_2 - N_1) I(z) = \gamma(\nu) I(z)\] where \(\sigma_{21}\) is the transition cross-section and \(\gamma(\nu) = \sigma_{21}(N_2 - N_1)\) is the gain coefficient.

2. Population Inversion and Pumping Schemes

Under normal thermal equilibrium, statistical thermodynamics mandates that lower energy states are always more densely populated than higher energy states: \[\frac{N_2}{N_1} = e^{-(E_2 - E_1)/k_B T} < 1 \implies N_1 > N_2\]

Because \(N_1 > N_2\), the gain coefficient \(\gamma(\nu)\) is strictly negative: absorption always wins over amplification.

To build a laser, we must create a non-equilibrium condition called Population Inversion, where: \[\boxed{N_2 > N_1}\]

When \(N_2 > N_1\), the medium exhibits negative absorption—every photon traversing the material triggers an exponential cascade of coherent stimulated emissions: \[I(z) = I_0 e^{\gamma z} \quad (\text{with } \gamma > 0)\]

Why a Two-Level System Cannot Lase

Can we achieve population inversion simply by shining a strong lamp on a collection of two-level atoms? No.

Because \(B_{12} = B_{21}\), as the pump intensity increases, the rate of upward transitions equals the rate of stimulated downward transitions. In the best-case limit of infinite pump power: \[N_1 \approx N_2\] The medium becomes transparent (optically saturated), but \(N_2\) can never strictly exceed \(N_1\).

To achieve \(N_2 > N_1\), practical lasers use three-level or four-level systems: * Three-Level Laser (e.g., Ruby): Atoms are pumped from ground state \(E_1\) to a broad pump band \(E_3\), from which they rapidly decay non-radiatively to a long-lived metastable state \(E_2\). However, because the lower lasing level \(E_1\) is the ground state, over 50% of all atoms in the crystal must be excited before inversion (\(N_2 > N_1\)) is reached. This requires massive pump thresholds. * Four-Level Laser (e.g., Nd:YAG, He-Ne): The lasing transition occurs between an upper state \(E_3\) and an excited lower state \(E_2\) situated well above the true ground state \(E_1\). Because \(E_2 - E_1 \gg k_B T\), state \(E_2\) is thermally depopulated (\(N_2 \approx 0\)). Thus, any small population buildup in \(E_3\) immediately achieves population inversion (\(N_3 > N_2\)). Four-level lasers require vastly lower threshold pump power.

3. Anatomy of a Laser: The Optical Cavity

Amplification by itself produces only amplified spontaneous emission (a glowing flare). To create a narrow, highly collimated beam, the gain medium is placed inside an optical resonator (typically a Fabry-Pérot cavity).

  1. High Reflector (HR Mirror): A dielectric mirror with reflectivity \(R_1 \approx 99.9\%\).
  2. Output Coupler (OC Mirror): A partially transparent mirror with reflectivity \(R_2 \approx 95\% - 99\%\), allowing a steady \(1\% - 5\%\) fraction of the circulating intracavity power to escape as the external output beam.
  3. Gain Medium: The pumped material generating coherent stimulated photons.

Cavity Resonance Condition

Only light traveling along the optical axis bounces back and forth repeatedly, experiencing repeated passes through the gain medium. For the waves to constructively interfere after one full round-trip (\(2L\)), the cavity length \(L\) must satisfy: \[2L = q \lambda \implies \nu_q = q \frac{c}{2L}\] where \(q\) is an integer (\(q \sim 10^5 - 10^6\)). These discrete resonance frequencies are the longitudinal cavity modes, spaced apart by the free spectral range \(\Delta \nu = c / (2L)\).

4. Deep Dive: The Helium-Neon (He-Ne) Gas Laser

Invented at Bell Labs in 1960 by Ali Javan, William R. Bennett Jr., and Donald Herriott, the Helium-Neon laser was the world’s first gas laser and the first continuously operating (continuous wave, CW) laser.

The lasing gas is a mixture of approximately \(10:1\) Helium to Neon sealed inside a narrow glass capillary tube at low pressure (\(\approx 1 - 3 \text{ Torr}\)).

Why Can’t We Just Use Pure Neon?

If Neon provides the actual lasing electrons, why do we need Helium at all? The answer lies in excitation cross-sections and quantum selection rules:

  1. Direct Electron Collisions in Neon are Inefficient: Direct electron collisions in an electric discharge excite all Neon energy levels indiscriminately. They populate the lower lasing levels just as readily as the upper ones, completely preventing population inversion.

  2. Helium Acts as an Energy Storage Battery: Helium is a simple two-electron atom. In the electric discharge (DC voltage of \(\approx 1000 - 2000 \text{ V}\)), energetic electrons collide with ground-state Helium: \[e^- + \text{He}(1s^2) \to \text{He}^*(2^1S_0 \text{ or } 2^3S_1) + e^-\] Crucially, radiative decay from \(\text{He}^*(2^1S_0)\) back to the \(1s^2\) ground state is strictly dipole-forbidden by quantum selection rules (\(\Delta S = 0, \Delta L = \pm 1\)). As a result, excited Helium atoms cannot easily radiate their energy away; they remain trapped in these long-lived metastable states for milliseconds (a virtual eternity on quantum timescales).

  3. Resonant Energy Transfer: By a stroke of quantum coincidence:

    • \(\text{He}^*(2^1S_0)\) has an excitation energy of \(20.61 \text{ eV}\).
    • Neon’s \(3s_2\) level sits at \(20.66 \text{ eV}\).

    The energy difference is a mere \(0.05 \text{ eV}\)—which is readily supplied by the ambient thermal kinetic energy (\(k_B T \approx 0.025 - 0.04 \text{ eV}\)) of the gas! When an excited Helium atom collides with a ground-state Neon atom, it transfers its excitation energy with near-100% quantum efficiency: \[\text{He}^*(2^1S_0) + \text{Ne}(2p^6) \to \text{He}(1s^2) + \text{Ne}^*(3s_2) - 0.05 \text{ eV}\]

    This selective collisional transfer pumps Neon electrons exclusively into the upper state \(3s_2\), creating a robust, steady-state population inversion over the \(2p_4\) state.

  4. Depopulating the Lower State: For continuous (CW) lasing, the lower state \(2p_4\) must be emptied faster than it fills. Neon’s \(2p\) electrons rapidly decay radiatively to the \(1s\) state. However, the \(1s\) state is itself metastable! If \(1s\) atoms accumulate, radiation trapping repopulates the \(2p\) level, killing the inversion. The only way to clear the \(1s\) state is for the atoms to diffuse to the inner walls of the tube and de-excite via physical collisions with the glass. This explains why He-Ne laser tubes are always built with narrow capillary bores (typically \(1 - 2 \text{ mm}\) in diameter): the atoms must reach the wall rapidly.

5. What Do “Watts” Really Mean in Lasers?

In everyday life, we associate “watts” with power consumption and brightness. A 60 W lightbulb is standard; a 1500 W space heater is hot.

When people learn that standard laboratory He-Ne lasers or handheld laser pointers emit only 1 to 5 milliwatts (0.001 - 0.005 W), they often ask: Why is a 5 mW laser dangerous if a 100 W lightbulb is completely safe to be in the same room with?

The answer lies in two fundamental optical metrics: Irradiance (Power Density) and Temporal Compression.

A. Spatial Concentration: Irradiance (\(W / m^2\))

A 100 W incandescent lightbulb radiates its energy isotropically over a full sphere (\(4\pi \text{ steradians}\)). At a distance of \(r = 1 \text{ meter}\), that power is spread over a spherical surface area of: \[A_{\text{sphere}} = 4\pi r^2 = 4\pi (1)^2 \approx 12.6 \text{ m}^2\]

Assuming an optical efficiency of \(\approx 5\%\) (most power is lost as invisible heat), the visible light power is \(P_{\text{vis}} \approx 5 \text{ W}\). The intensity (irradiance) arriving at your eye is: \[I_{\text{bulb}} = \frac{5 \text{ W}}{12.6 \text{ m}^2} \approx 0.4 \text{ W/m}^2 = \mathbf{0.04 \text{ mW/cm}^2}\]

Now consider a typical 5 mW (0.005 W) red laser pointer: * The beam is strictly collimated into a tiny pencil of radius \(r_{\text{beam}} \approx 1 \text{ mm}\) (\(A = \pi (10^{-3})^2 \approx 3.14 \times 10^{-6} \text{ m}^2\)). * The irradiance of the unexpanded beam is: \[I_{\text{laser}} = \frac{0.005 \text{ W}}{3.14 \times 10^{-6} \text{ m}^2} \approx 1,590 \text{ W/m}^2 = \mathbf{159 \text{ mW/cm}^2}\]

The un-focused laser beam is already ~4,000 times more intense than the light from the 100 W bulb.

The Devastating Effect of the Eye’s Lens

It gets much worse when the beam enters the human eye. Because laser light is spatially coherent (plane waves with a flat phase front), the eye’s cornea and lens focus the parallel rays down to the absolute diffraction limit on the retina: a spot roughly \(d \approx 10 - 20 \ \mu\text{m}\) in diameter!

The focal area of a \(15 \ \mu\text{m}\) spot on the retina is: \[A_{\text{retina}} \approx \pi (7.5 \times 10^{-6} \text{ m})^2 \approx 1.77 \times 10^{-10} \text{ m}^2\]

The power density hitting your retinal photoreceptor cells is: \[I_{\text{retina}} = \frac{0.005 \text{ W}}{1.77 \times 10^{-10} \text{ m}^2} \approx 28,000,000 \text{ W/m}^2 = \mathbf{2,800 \text{ W/cm}^2}!\]

For comparison, the surface irradiance of the sun at the equator is roughly \(0.1 \text{ W/cm}^2\). A humble 5 mW laser pointer focused onto the retina delivers over 20,000 times the intensity of direct midday sunlight on that tiny patch of tissue, flashing water into steam and burning permanent blind spots into the macula in milliseconds.

B. Temporal Concentration: Continuous Wave (CW) vs. Ultrafast Pulsed Lasers

The definition of a watt is strictly one joule per second: \[\text{Power (Watts)} = \frac{\text{Energy (Joules)}}{\text{Time (Seconds)}}\]

This leads to two drastically different regimes of laser power:

The Phenomenon of Peak Power

Consider an ultrafast mode-locked Titanium-Sapphire laser. The average electrical wall-plug consumption might be comparable to a laptop charger (\(100 \text{ W}\)), and the average optical beam power is just \(1 \text{ W}\).

However, that energy is not delivered continuously. It is compressed by an optical cavity technique called mode locking into a train of razor-thin temporal pulses, each lasting only \(\Delta t = 20 \text{ femtoseconds} = 20 \times 10^{-15} \text{ seconds}\).

During that brief flash, the instantaneous peak power is: \[P_{\text{peak}} = \frac{E_{\text{pulse}}}{\Delta t} = \frac{10^{-3} \text{ Joules}}{20 \times 10^{-15} \text{ seconds}} = 50,000,000,000 \text{ Watts} = \mathbf{50 \text{ Gigawatts}}\]

For 20 quadrillionths of a second, the light output of that modest tabletop laboratory laser exceeds the combined electrical generating capacity of the entire electrical grid of the United Kingdom!

At these astronomical power densities, the electric field of the laser light (\(\mathbf{E} \sim 10^{11} \text{ V/m}\)) exceeds the Coulomb binding field holding electrons to atomic nuclei. Atoms in the focal volume are instantaneously stripped of their electrons via tunnel ionization, turning cold matter into relativistic plasma before the nuclei even have time to move. This is the foundation of modern cold-ablation LASIK surgery: the laser vaporizes molecular bonds without transmitting any heat to the surrounding cornea.

6. Laser Safety Classifications

Because human eyes cannot blink fast enough to protect against intense coherent light, lasers are classified internationally (IEC 60825-1) by their output power and safety hazards:

  • Class 1 (< 0.4 \(\mu\text{W}\) or fully enclosed): Completely safe under all conditions. Examples: CD/DVD players, laser printers.
  • Class 2 (< 1 mW, visible only): Safe for accidental exposure. The natural human aversion response (the blink reflex, taking \(\approx 0.25\) seconds) protects the retina. Examples: supermarket barcode scanners.
  • Class 3R (1 mW – 5 mW): Low risk of injury if viewed momentarily, but dangerous if stared into directly or viewed through magnifying optics (telescopes, binoculars). Examples: standard green/red presentation pointers.
  • Class 3B (5 mW – 500 mW): Direct beam and specular reflections cause immediate, permanent eye damage. Diffuse reflections (looking at the dot on a matte white wall) are generally safe. Examples: research lasers, spectroscopy sources, glow-in-the-dark light shows. Laser safety goggles required.
  • Class 4 (> 500 mW): Extremely hazardous. Direct exposure blinds instantly. Even diffuse reflections off a matte wall can permanently damage vision. Capable of igniting flammable materials, causing severe skin burns, and vaporizing toxic materials. Examples: industrial metal cutters, laser surgery scalpels, entertainment stadium lasers.

Summary

  1. Stimulated Emission is Cloning: An incoming photon forces an excited electron to emit an identical twin photon with matching frequency, phase, direction, and polarization.
  2. Population Inversion is Essential: Because absorption and stimulated emission share equal intrinsic cross-sections (\(B_{12} = B_{21}\)), you must have more atoms in the excited state than the lower state (\(N_2 > N_1\)) for net amplification to occur.
  3. He-Ne Lasers Exploit Quantum Resonance: Helium acts as an atomic storage capacitor, transferring its excitation to Neon through near-perfect energy resonance (\(\Delta E = 0.05 \text{ eV}\)).
  4. Watts Depend on Area and Time: A continuous 5 mW laser pointer has orders of magnitude higher retinal irradiance than a 100 W bulb because its spatial divergence is diffraction-limited. In pulsed systems, compressing a fraction of a joule into femtoseconds creates instantaneous peak powers measured in gigawatts and petawatts.
