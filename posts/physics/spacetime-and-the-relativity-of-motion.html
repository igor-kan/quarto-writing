1 Motion Is Relative; What Is Absolute?

Galileo’s ship thought experiment already argued that below decks, no mechanical experiment tells you whether the ship is at rest or moving uniformly. Yet Newton insisted that some motion — acceleration — is absolute. The tension between these two claims is the whole subject of spacetime in classical mechanics.

The question has three parts:

  1. Space: does it exist as a background stage (Newton) or only as relations among bodies (Leibniz)?
  2. Time: is simultaneity universal and time absolute?
  3. Frames: which observers are physically equivalent, and how do we convert between them?

2 Newton’s Absolute Space and Time

Newton’s Principia postulates that:

  • Absolute time “flows equably” and is the same for all observers.
  • Absolute space exists independently of matter, and motion relative to it is real.

The famous evidence is the rotating bucket. A bucket of water, set spinning, first rotates relative to the bucket, then the water surface becomes concave — and the concavity persists even after the water and bucket rotate together, i.e. when there is no relative motion between water and bucket. Newton argued that the concave surface reveals absolute rotation relative to space itself.

The same logic underlies the Foucault pendulum, whose swing plane rotates relative to the Earth, and the equatorial bulge, whose existence can be attributed to rotation “relative to absolute space.”

These are strong, unverifiable-sounding assumptions. They were contested from the beginning.

3 Relationalism: Leibniz and Mach

Leibniz argued that space and time are not substances but relations among events: only relative positions and relative motions are meaningful, and a universe shifted through empty space would be indistinguishable from the original. On this view, the rotating bucket’s concavity must be explained by motion relative to the distant stars, not relative to space.

Mach sharpened this into Mach’s principle: local inertial effects (like the concavity, or the inertia of a body) are determined by the distribution of matter in the universe. Mach’s principle inspired Einstein and is a recurring theme in attempts to formulate gravity from relational first principles.

Classical mechanics takes a pragmatic middle position: it does not need absolute space, only the equivalence class of inertial frames. It cannot say which frames are inertial from first principles — it postulates their existence.

4 Galilean Spacetime

The precise, modern structure behind Newtonian mechanics is Galilean spacetime, a fibre bundle over time:

  • Time is a 1-dimensional affine line \(\mathbb{R}\); durations are absolute.
  • Each instant \(t\) carries a copy of Euclidean 3-space — the simultaneity slice.
  • There is no canonical identification between different time slices: an event’s “same place at a later time” is undefined.

Three consequences follow, and they are exactly the assumptions classical mechanics hides:

  1. Simultaneity is absolute. All observers agree on which events are simultaneous.
  2. Absolute velocity is meaningless. There is no preferred rest frame; only relative velocities are physical.
  3. Acceleration is meaningful and frame-independent. A body’s acceleration is the same in all inertial frames, so “who is really accelerating” is an objective question.

This is the geometric content of the Galilean principle of relativity. Compare it with the Minkowski structure of special relativity, where the simultaneity slices of different observers disagree.

5 Galilean Transformations

Two inertial frames whose origins coincide at \(t=0\), with the primed frame moving at constant velocity \(\mathbf V\) relative to the unprimed one, are related by the Galilean transformation \[ \mathbf r' = \mathbf r - \mathbf V t, \qquad t' = t. \] Differentiating:

  • Velocities add: \(\mathbf v' = \mathbf v - \mathbf V\).
  • Accelerations are invariant: \(\mathbf a' = \mathbf a\) (the \(\mathbf V t\) term is linear in \(t\)).
  • Since the force laws (gravitational, electromagnetic) are unchanged, Newton’s second law is form-invariant: \[m\mathbf a' = \mathbf F' = \mathbf F = m\mathbf a.\]

Therefore no mechanical experiment can distinguish one inertial frame from another — Galilean relativity.

5.1 What Is Not Invariant

Energy and momentum are frame-dependent:

  • Momentum changes by \(-\sum m_a\mathbf V\).
  • Kinetic energy changes by the König term \(T' = T - \mathbf V\cdot\mathbf P + \tfrac12 M V^2\) (with \(M\) the total mass).

Conservation laws, however, hold in every inertial frame: if \(\mathbf P\) is conserved in one, it is conserved in all (see energy and reference frames). This is a good example of an invariant law expressed through frame-dependent quantities.

6 Inertial and Non-Inertial Frames

  • Inertial frame: one in which a free particle moves in a straight line at constant velocity (Newton’s first law), equivalently one in which Newton’s second law holds with no extra terms.
  • Non-inertial frame: accelerating and/or rotating relative to an inertial frame. Newton’s law then acquires fictitious (inertial) forces.

Newton’s first law is not a special case of the second; it is the definition of the frames in which the second holds. That is an assumption, not a theorem.

7 Rotating Frames and Fictitious Forces

Let the primed frame rotate with angular velocity \(\boldsymbol\Omega(t)\) relative to an inertial frame. Applying the transformation twice (for velocity, then for acceleration) gives the classical transport theorem, and Newton’s law in the rotating frame reads \[ m\mathbf a_{\text{rot}} = \mathbf F_{\text{real}} \;\underbrace{-\,2m\,\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf v_{\text{rot}}}_{\text{Coriolis}} \;\underbrace{-\,m\,\boldsymbol\Omega\times(\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf r)}_{\text{centrifugal}} \;\underbrace{-\,m\,\dot{\boldsymbol\Omega}\times\mathbf r}_{\text{Euler}}. \]

The three extra terms are not forces from any interaction. They are what “\(\mathbf F=m\mathbf a\)” becomes when you insist on describing physics in a rotating frame. Their names and roles:

Term Expression What it does
Centrifugal \(-m\,\boldsymbol\Omega\times(\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf r)\) outward push \(\propto m\Omega^2r_\perp\), away from the axis
Coriolis \(-2m\,\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf v_{\text{rot}}\) deflects moving bodies \(\perp\) to both \(\boldsymbol\Omega\) and \(\mathbf v\)
Euler \(-m\,\dot{\boldsymbol\Omega}\times\mathbf r\) appears only when the rotation rate changes

7.1 Physical Consequences

  • Foucault pendulum: the swing plane precesses at rate \(\Omega\sin\phi\) (latitude \(\phi\)), a direct demonstration of Earth’s rotation.
  • Cyclones and trade winds: the Coriolis force bends large-scale flows, giving counterclockwise (Northern) and clockwise (Southern) circulation.
  • Projectile deflection and artillery: long-range shots drift east/deflect laterally.
  • Eötvös effect: a body’s apparent weight depends on its east–west velocity because of the Coriolis term.
  • Equatorial bulge: the centrifugal term, in the co-rotating frame, explains the oblate shape of the Earth.

7.2 Fictitious vs Real Forces

Fictitious forces share features of real forces (they scale with mass, appear in the equations) but differ fundamentally: they vanish in inertial frames, they have no reaction partner (Newton’s third law fails for them alone), and they are frame artifacts. The ambiguity of “which frame is really inertial” is tamed by the equivalence principle — locally, a uniform acceleration is indistinguishable from a gravitational field — which is the seed of general relativity.

8 Where the Assumptions Break

Classical spacetime is a limit, not the truth.

  • Special relativity (1905): the speed of light is the same in all inertial frames. Preserving it forces Lorentz transformations \[ x'=\gamma(x-Vt),\quad t'=\gamma\!\left(t-\frac{Vx}{c^2}\right),\quad \gamma=1/\sqrt{1-V^2/c^2}, \] which mix space and time. Simultaneity becomes relative, time dilation and length contraction appear, and velocities no longer simply add. Galilean transformations are the \(V\ll c\) limit.
  • General relativity (1915): gravity is the curvature of a dynamical spacetime, not a force. Absolute space and time both dissolve, and Mach’s principle is partially realized.

Classical mechanics is thus the \(V/c\to0\) corner of a larger theory, exactly as it is the \(\hbar\to0\) corner of quantum mechanics (see quantum logic and statistical mechanics).

9 Python Laboratory

9.1 Fictitious Forces Reproduce the Physics

Code 
import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

Omega = 1.0                     # angular velocity of the rotating frame
r0    = np.array([1.0, 0.0])    # particle position at t = 0
vi0   = np.array([0.0, 1.0])    # inertial velocity (constant, since force = 0)

# Rotating-frame velocity at t = 0: v_rot = v_inertial - Omega x r.
# For Omega = Omega z-hat, Omega x r = (-y, x).
vr0 = vi0 - Omega * np.array([-r0[1], r0[0]])

# Equations of motion in the rotating frame for a FREE particle:
#   a_rot = -2 Omega x v_rot - Omega x (Omega x r_rot)
# In components (Omega along z):
#   x'' =  2 Omega y' + Omega^2 x      (Coriolis + centrifugal)
#   y'' = -2 Omega x' + Omega^2 y
def rotating_frame(t, s):
    x, y, vx, vy = s
    return [vx, vy, 2*Omega*vy + Omega**2*x, -2*Omega*vx + Omega**2*y]

T  = 2*np.pi/Omega
te = np.linspace(0, T, 2000)
sol = solve_ivp(rotating_frame, [0, T], [r0[0], r0[1], vr0[0], vr0[1]],
                t_eval=te, rtol=1e-11, atol=1e-13)

# For comparison: the inertial straight line, rotated into rotating coordinates
xi = r0[0] + vi0[0]*te
yi = r0[1] + vi0[1]*te
xr =  np.cos(Omega*te)*xi + np.sin(Omega*te)*yi
yr = -np.sin(Omega*te)*xi + np.cos(Omega*te)*yi

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(16, 5))
axes[0].plot(xi, yi, color="#2980b9", lw=2)
axes[0].scatter(*r0, color="#e74c3c", zorder=3)
axes[0].set_title("Inertial frame: straight line"); axes[0].set_aspect("equal")

axes[1].plot(sol.y[0], sol.y[1], color="#e74c3c", lw=2, label="rotating-frame ODE")
axes[1].plot(xr, yr, "k--", lw=1, label="inertial line, rotated")
axes[1].set_title("Rotating frame: curved (fictitious forces)")
axes[1].set_aspect("equal"); axes[1].legend()

axes[2].semilogy(te, np.abs(sol.y[0]-xr) + np.abs(sol.y[1]-yr), color="#27ae60")
axes[2].set_title("Difference (integration error)")
for ax in axes:
    ax.grid(alpha=0.3); ax.set_xlabel("x"); ax.set_ylabel("y")
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

print("=== FICTITIOUS FORCES REPRODUCE THE INERTIAL STRAIGHT LINE ===")
print(f"  max |ODE - coordinate transform| = "
      f"{np.max(np.abs(sol.y[0]-xr) + np.abs(sol.y[1]-yr)):.2e}")
print("  the centrifugal and Coriolis terms are coordinate effects, not interactions.")

  max |ODE - coordinate transform| = 5.32e-11
  the centrifugal and Coriolis terms are coordinate effects, not interactions.

9.2 Galilean Boosts: Conservation Invariant, Energy Frame-Dependent

Code 
# =====================================================================
# GALILEAN RELATIVITY
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------
# A boost r' = r - V t leaves accelerations -- and hence Newton's laws and
# the conservation laws -- invariant, but it changes momentum and kinetic
# energy. This cell shows both facts on a 1D elastic collision.
# =====================================================================
import numpy as np

def elastic_collision(m1, m2, u1, u2):
    """Return the post-collision velocities of a 1D elastic collision."""
    v1 = ((m1 - m2)*u1 + 2*m2*u2) / (m1 + m2)
    v2 = ((m2 - m1)*u2 + 2*m1*u1) / (m1 + m2)
    return v1, v2

m1, m2, u1, u2 = 1.0, 2.0, 3.0, -1.0
v1, v2 = elastic_collision(m1, m2, u1, u2)

def momentum(a, b): return m1*a + m2*b
def kinetic(a, b):  return 0.5*m1*a**2 + 0.5*m2*b**2

print("=== LAB FRAME ===")
print(f"  momentum before/after: {momentum(u1,u2):+.3f} / {momentum(v1,v2):+.3f} (conserved)")
print(f"  kinetic  before/after: {kinetic(u1,u2):+.3f} / {kinetic(v1,v2):+.3f} (conserved)")

V = 5.0     # boost to a frame moving at V
print("=== BOOSTED FRAME (V = 5) ===")
print(f"  momentum before/after: {momentum(u1-V,u2-V):+.3f} / {momentum(v1-V,v2-V):+.3f} (conserved)")
print(f"  kinetic  before/after: {kinetic(u1-V,u2-V):+.3f} / {kinetic(v1-V,v2-V):+.3f} (conserved)")
print("  the values differ from the lab frame, but the conservation laws do not:")
print("  this is the content of Galilean relativity.")

9.3 The Foucault Pendulum Precesses

Code 
import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

Omega_z = 0.5      # vertical component Omega sin(latitude), in rad/s (exaggerated)
w0      = 2.0      # pendulum natural frequency (rad/s)

# Linearized pendulum in a frame rotating about the vertical:
#   x'' = -w0^2 x + 2 Omega_z y'
#   y'' = -w0^2 y - 2 Omega_z x'
def foucault(t, s):
    x, y, vx, vy = s
    return [vx, vy, -w0**2*x + 2*Omega_z*vy, -w0**2*y - 2*Omega_z*vx]

Tf = 40.0
tf = np.linspace(0, Tf, 20000)
sol = solve_ivp(foucault, [0, Tf], [1.0, 0.0, 0.0, 0.0],
                t_eval=tf, rtol=1e-11, atol=1e-13)

# Extract the precession empirically. The complex position z = x + i y
# oscillates at the fast frequency omega = sqrt(w0^2 + Omega^2) AND its
# envelope rotates at the precession rate -Omega_z. Removing omega*t from
# the unwrapped phase leaves a straight line whose slope is the rate.
z = sol.y[0] + 1j*sol.y[1]
omega = np.sqrt(w0**2 + Omega_z**2)
phase = np.unwrap(np.angle(z)) - omega*tf
empirical = np.polyfit(tf, phase - phase[0], 1)[0]    # slope = precession rate

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(13, 5.5))
axes[0].plot(sol.y[0], sol.y[1], lw=0.8, color="#2980b9")
axes[0].set_title("Foucault pendulum in the rotating frame")
axes[0].set_xlabel("x"); axes[0].set_ylabel("y"); axes[0].set_aspect("equal"); axes[0].grid(alpha=0.3)

axes[1].plot(tf, phase - phase[0], color="#2980b9", label="measured envelope angle")
axes[1].plot(tf, -Omega_z*tf, "--", color="#e74c3c", label=r"theory: $-\Omega\sin\phi\,t$")
axes[1].set_title("Precession angle versus time")
axes[1].set_xlabel("time (s)"); axes[1].set_ylabel("precession angle (rad)")
axes[1].legend(); axes[1].grid(alpha=0.3)
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

print("=== FOUCAULT PRECESSION ===")
print(f"  measured precession rate = {empirical:+.4f} rad/s")
print(f"  theory -Omega sin(phi)   = {-Omega_z:+.4f} rad/s")
print("  the swing plane rotates at Omega sin(latitude): a laboratory proof of Earth's rotation.")
=== FOUCAULT PRECESSION ===
  measured precession rate = -0.4994 rad/s
  theory -Omega sin(phi)   = -0.5000 rad/s
  the swing plane rotates at Omega sin(latitude): a laboratory proof of Earth's rotation.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\textbf{Newton: } && \text{absolute space and time; the rotating bucket; real acceleration.} \\[3pt] &\textbf{Leibniz/Mach: } && \text{relationalism; inertia from the distant matter (Mach's principle).} \\[3pt] &\textbf{Galilean spacetime: } && \text{bundle over time; absolute simultaneity; no absolute velocity; invariant acceleration.} \\[3pt] &\textbf{Galilean transform: } && \mathbf r'=\mathbf r-\mathbf V t,\ t'=t;\ \mathbf v'=\mathbf v-\mathbf V;\ \mathbf a'=\mathbf a. \\[3pt] &\textbf{Fictitious forces: } && \text{centrifugal, Coriolis, Euler — coordinate effects of a non-inertial frame.} \\[3pt] &\textbf{Evidence: } && \text{Foucault pendulum, cyclones, projectile deflection, Eötvös, equatorial bulge.} \\[3pt] &\textbf{Breakdown: } && \text{special relativity (relative simultaneity); general relativity (dynamical spacetime).} \end{aligned} \]

The classical picture of space and time is a coherent set of assumptions with a precise geometric meaning, empirical support (Foucault), and a well-defined boundary (relativity). Knowing both makes clear what “inertial frame” and “fictitious force” really are.

## Motion Is Relative; What Is Absolute?

Galileo's ship thought experiment already argued that below decks, no mechanical experiment tells you whether the ship is at rest or moving uniformly. Yet Newton insisted that some motion — **acceleration** — is absolute. The tension between these two claims is the whole subject of spacetime in classical mechanics.

The question has three parts:

1. **Space:** does it exist as a background stage (Newton) or only as relations among bodies (Leibniz)?
2. **Time:** is simultaneity universal and time absolute?
3. **Frames:** which observers are physically equivalent, and how do we convert between them?

---

## Newton's Absolute Space and Time

Newton's *Principia* postulates that:

- **Absolute time** "flows equably" and is the same for all observers.
- **Absolute space** exists independently of matter, and motion *relative to it* is real.

The famous evidence is the **rotating bucket**. A bucket of water, set spinning, first rotates relative to the bucket, then the water surface becomes concave — and the concavity persists even after the water and bucket rotate together, i.e. when there is no relative motion between water and bucket. Newton argued that the concave surface reveals **absolute rotation relative to space itself**.

The same logic underlies the **Foucault pendulum**, whose swing plane rotates relative to the Earth, and the equatorial bulge, whose existence can be attributed to rotation "relative to absolute space."

These are strong, unverifiable-sounding assumptions. They were contested from the beginning.

---

## Relationalism: Leibniz and Mach

**Leibniz** argued that space and time are not substances but **relations among events**: only relative positions and relative motions are meaningful, and a universe shifted through empty space would be indistinguishable from the original. On this view, the rotating bucket's concavity must be explained by **motion relative to the distant stars**, not relative to space.

**Mach** sharpened this into **Mach's principle**: local inertial effects (like the concavity, or the inertia of a body) are determined by the distribution of matter in the universe. Mach's principle inspired Einstein and is a recurring theme in attempts to formulate gravity from relational first principles.

Classical mechanics takes a pragmatic middle position: it does not need absolute space, only the **equivalence class of inertial frames**. It cannot say *which* frames are inertial from first principles — it postulates their existence.

## Galilean Spacetime

The precise, modern structure behind Newtonian mechanics is **Galilean spacetime**, a **fibre bundle over time**:

- **Time** is a 1-dimensional affine line $\mathbb{R}$; durations are absolute.
- Each instant $t$ carries a copy of **Euclidean 3-space** — the simultaneity slice.
- There is **no canonical identification** between different time slices: an event's "same place at a later time" is undefined.

Three consequences follow, and they are exactly the assumptions classical mechanics hides:

1. **Simultaneity is absolute.** All observers agree on which events are simultaneous.
2. **Absolute velocity is meaningless.** There is no preferred rest frame; only relative velocities are physical.
3. **Acceleration is meaningful and frame-independent.** A body's acceleration is the same in all inertial frames, so "who is really accelerating" is an objective question.

This is the geometric content of the Galilean principle of relativity. Compare it with the [Minkowski structure of special relativity](michelson-interferometer-and-ligo.qmd), where the simultaneity slices of different observers disagree.

## Galilean Transformations

Two inertial frames whose origins coincide at $t=0$, with the primed frame moving at constant velocity $\mathbf V$ relative to the unprimed one, are related by the **Galilean transformation**
$$
\mathbf r' = \mathbf r - \mathbf V t, \qquad t' = t.
$$
Differentiating:

- **Velocities add:** $\mathbf v' = \mathbf v - \mathbf V$.
- **Accelerations are invariant:** $\mathbf a' = \mathbf a$ (the $\mathbf V t$ term is linear in $t$).
- Since the force laws (gravitational, electromagnetic) are unchanged, **Newton's second law is form-invariant**:
$$m\mathbf a' = \mathbf F' = \mathbf F = m\mathbf a.$$

Therefore **no mechanical experiment can distinguish one inertial frame from another** — Galilean relativity.

### What Is *Not* Invariant

Energy and momentum are **frame-dependent**:

- Momentum changes by $-\sum m_a\mathbf V$.
- Kinetic energy changes by the **König term** $T' = T - \mathbf V\cdot\mathbf P + \tfrac12 M V^2$ (with $M$ the total mass).

Conservation laws, however, hold in **every** inertial frame: if $\mathbf P$ is conserved in one, it is conserved in all (see [energy and reference frames](energy-and-conservation-laws.qmd)). This is a good example of an **invariant law** expressed through **frame-dependent quantities**.

---

## Inertial and Non-Inertial Frames

- **Inertial frame:** one in which a free particle moves in a straight line at constant velocity (Newton's first law), equivalently one in which Newton's second law holds with no extra terms.
- **Non-inertial frame:** accelerating and/or rotating relative to an inertial frame. Newton's law then acquires **fictitious (inertial) forces**.

Newton's first law is not a special case of the second; it is the **definition of the frames** in which the second holds. That is an assumption, not a theorem.

---

## Rotating Frames and Fictitious Forces

Let the primed frame rotate with angular velocity $\boldsymbol\Omega(t)$ relative to an inertial frame. Applying the transformation twice (for velocity, then for acceleration) gives the classical **transport theorem**, and Newton's law in the rotating frame reads
$$
m\mathbf a_{\text{rot}}
= \mathbf F_{\text{real}}
\;\underbrace{-\,2m\,\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf v_{\text{rot}}}_{\text{Coriolis}}
\;\underbrace{-\,m\,\boldsymbol\Omega\times(\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf r)}_{\text{centrifugal}}
\;\underbrace{-\,m\,\dot{\boldsymbol\Omega}\times\mathbf r}_{\text{Euler}}.
$$

The three extra terms are **not** forces from any interaction. They are what "$\mathbf F=m\mathbf a$" becomes when you insist on describing physics in a rotating frame. Their names and roles:

| Term | Expression | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **Centrifugal** | $-m\,\boldsymbol\Omega\times(\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf r)$ | outward push $\propto m\Omega^2r_\perp$, away from the axis |
| **Coriolis** | $-2m\,\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf v_{\text{rot}}$ | deflects moving bodies $\perp$ to both $\boldsymbol\Omega$ and $\mathbf v$ |
| **Euler** | $-m\,\dot{\boldsymbol\Omega}\times\mathbf r$ | appears only when the rotation rate changes |

### Physical Consequences

- **Foucault pendulum:** the swing plane precesses at rate $\Omega\sin\phi$ (latitude $\phi$), a direct demonstration of Earth's rotation.
- **Cyclones and trade winds:** the Coriolis force bends large-scale flows, giving counterclockwise (Northern) and clockwise (Southern) circulation.
- **Projectile deflection and artillery:** long-range shots drift east/deflect laterally.
- **Eötvös effect:** a body's apparent weight depends on its east–west velocity because of the Coriolis term.
- **Equatorial bulge:** the centrifugal term, in the co-rotating frame, explains the oblate shape of the Earth.

### Fictitious vs Real Forces

Fictitious forces share features of real forces (they scale with mass, appear in the equations) but differ fundamentally: they **vanish in inertial frames**, they have **no reaction partner** (Newton's third law fails for them alone), and they are **frame artifacts**. The ambiguity of "which frame is really inertial" is tamed by the **equivalence principle** — locally, a uniform acceleration is indistinguishable from a gravitational field — which is the seed of general relativity.

---

## Where the Assumptions Break

Classical spacetime is a limit, not the truth.

- **Special relativity (1905):** the speed of light is the same in all inertial frames. Preserving it forces **Lorentz transformations**
$$
x'=\gamma(x-Vt),\quad t'=\gamma\!\left(t-\frac{Vx}{c^2}\right),\quad \gamma=1/\sqrt{1-V^2/c^2},
$$
which **mix space and time**. Simultaneity becomes **relative**, time dilation and length contraction appear, and velocities no longer simply add. Galilean transformations are the $V\ll c$ limit.
- **General relativity (1915):** gravity is the **curvature of a dynamical spacetime**, not a force. Absolute space and time both dissolve, and Mach's principle is partially realized.

Classical mechanics is thus the $V/c\to0$ corner of a larger theory, exactly as it is the $\hbar\to0$ corner of quantum mechanics (see [quantum logic and statistical mechanics](quantum-logic-statistical-mechanics-and-topos.qmd)).

## Python Laboratory

### Fictitious Forces Reproduce the Physics

```{python}
#| label: fig-rotating-frame
#| fig-cap: "A free particle seen from a rotating frame. Left: in the inertial frame the particle moves in a straight line at constant velocity. Middle: integrating the rotating-frame equations of motion -- which contain only the fictitious centrifugal and Coriolis terms -- gives a curved path that matches, to numerical precision, the inertial straight line re-expressed in rotating coordinates. Right: the difference between the two, which is at the level of the integration tolerance. This is what it means that fictitious forces are coordinate effects that reproduce Newtonian physics."

import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

Omega = 1.0                     # angular velocity of the rotating frame
r0    = np.array([1.0, 0.0])    # particle position at t = 0
vi0   = np.array([0.0, 1.0])    # inertial velocity (constant, since force = 0)

# Rotating-frame velocity at t = 0: v_rot = v_inertial - Omega x r.
# For Omega = Omega z-hat, Omega x r = (-y, x).
vr0 = vi0 - Omega * np.array([-r0[1], r0[0]])

# Equations of motion in the rotating frame for a FREE particle:
#   a_rot = -2 Omega x v_rot - Omega x (Omega x r_rot)
# In components (Omega along z):
#   x'' =  2 Omega y' + Omega^2 x      (Coriolis + centrifugal)
#   y'' = -2 Omega x' + Omega^2 y
def rotating_frame(t, s):
    x, y, vx, vy = s
    return [vx, vy, 2*Omega*vy + Omega**2*x, -2*Omega*vx + Omega**2*y]

T  = 2*np.pi/Omega
te = np.linspace(0, T, 2000)
sol = solve_ivp(rotating_frame, [0, T], [r0[0], r0[1], vr0[0], vr0[1]],
                t_eval=te, rtol=1e-11, atol=1e-13)

# For comparison: the inertial straight line, rotated into rotating coordinates
xi = r0[0] + vi0[0]*te
yi = r0[1] + vi0[1]*te
xr =  np.cos(Omega*te)*xi + np.sin(Omega*te)*yi
yr = -np.sin(Omega*te)*xi + np.cos(Omega*te)*yi

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(16, 5))
axes[0].plot(xi, yi, color="#2980b9", lw=2)
axes[0].scatter(*r0, color="#e74c3c", zorder=3)
axes[0].set_title("Inertial frame: straight line"); axes[0].set_aspect("equal")

axes[1].plot(sol.y[0], sol.y[1], color="#e74c3c", lw=2, label="rotating-frame ODE")
axes[1].plot(xr, yr, "k--", lw=1, label="inertial line, rotated")
axes[1].set_title("Rotating frame: curved (fictitious forces)")
axes[1].set_aspect("equal"); axes[1].legend()

axes[2].semilogy(te, np.abs(sol.y[0]-xr) + np.abs(sol.y[1]-yr), color="#27ae60")
axes[2].set_title("Difference (integration error)")
for ax in axes:
    ax.grid(alpha=0.3); ax.set_xlabel("x"); ax.set_ylabel("y")
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

print("=== FICTITIOUS FORCES REPRODUCE THE INERTIAL STRAIGHT LINE ===")
print(f"  max |ODE - coordinate transform| = "
      f"{np.max(np.abs(sol.y[0]-xr) + np.abs(sol.y[1]-yr)):.2e}")
print("  the centrifugal and Coriolis terms are coordinate effects, not interactions.")
```

### Galilean Boosts: Conservation Invariant, Energy Frame-Dependent

```{python}
# =====================================================================
# GALILEAN RELATIVITY
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------
# A boost r' = r - V t leaves accelerations -- and hence Newton's laws and
# the conservation laws -- invariant, but it changes momentum and kinetic
# energy. This cell shows both facts on a 1D elastic collision.
# =====================================================================
import numpy as np

def elastic_collision(m1, m2, u1, u2):
    """Return the post-collision velocities of a 1D elastic collision."""
    v1 = ((m1 - m2)*u1 + 2*m2*u2) / (m1 + m2)
    v2 = ((m2 - m1)*u2 + 2*m1*u1) / (m1 + m2)
    return v1, v2

m1, m2, u1, u2 = 1.0, 2.0, 3.0, -1.0
v1, v2 = elastic_collision(m1, m2, u1, u2)

def momentum(a, b): return m1*a + m2*b
def kinetic(a, b):  return 0.5*m1*a**2 + 0.5*m2*b**2

print("=== LAB FRAME ===")
print(f"  momentum before/after: {momentum(u1,u2):+.3f} / {momentum(v1,v2):+.3f} (conserved)")
print(f"  kinetic  before/after: {kinetic(u1,u2):+.3f} / {kinetic(v1,v2):+.3f} (conserved)")

V = 5.0     # boost to a frame moving at V
print("=== BOOSTED FRAME (V = 5) ===")
print(f"  momentum before/after: {momentum(u1-V,u2-V):+.3f} / {momentum(v1-V,v2-V):+.3f} (conserved)")
print(f"  kinetic  before/after: {kinetic(u1-V,u2-V):+.3f} / {kinetic(v1-V,v2-V):+.3f} (conserved)")
print("  the values differ from the lab frame, but the conservation laws do not:")
print("  this is the content of Galilean relativity.")
```

### The Foucault Pendulum Precesses

```{python}
#| label: fig-foucault
#| fig-cap: "Foucault pendulum. Left: the trajectory in the rotating (Earth-fixed) frame, computed from the linearized equations with the vertical component of Earth's rotation as a Coriolis coupling. The swing plane slowly precesses, tracing a rosette. Right: the precession angle versus time compared with the theoretical rate Omega sin(latitude); the match confirms that the precession is a pure Coriolis effect."

import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

Omega_z = 0.5      # vertical component Omega sin(latitude), in rad/s (exaggerated)
w0      = 2.0      # pendulum natural frequency (rad/s)

# Linearized pendulum in a frame rotating about the vertical:
#   x'' = -w0^2 x + 2 Omega_z y'
#   y'' = -w0^2 y - 2 Omega_z x'
def foucault(t, s):
    x, y, vx, vy = s
    return [vx, vy, -w0**2*x + 2*Omega_z*vy, -w0**2*y - 2*Omega_z*vx]

Tf = 40.0
tf = np.linspace(0, Tf, 20000)
sol = solve_ivp(foucault, [0, Tf], [1.0, 0.0, 0.0, 0.0],
                t_eval=tf, rtol=1e-11, atol=1e-13)

# Extract the precession empirically. The complex position z = x + i y
# oscillates at the fast frequency omega = sqrt(w0^2 + Omega^2) AND its
# envelope rotates at the precession rate -Omega_z. Removing omega*t from
# the unwrapped phase leaves a straight line whose slope is the rate.
z = sol.y[0] + 1j*sol.y[1]
omega = np.sqrt(w0**2 + Omega_z**2)
phase = np.unwrap(np.angle(z)) - omega*tf
empirical = np.polyfit(tf, phase - phase[0], 1)[0]    # slope = precession rate

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(13, 5.5))
axes[0].plot(sol.y[0], sol.y[1], lw=0.8, color="#2980b9")
axes[0].set_title("Foucault pendulum in the rotating frame")
axes[0].set_xlabel("x"); axes[0].set_ylabel("y"); axes[0].set_aspect("equal"); axes[0].grid(alpha=0.3)

axes[1].plot(tf, phase - phase[0], color="#2980b9", label="measured envelope angle")
axes[1].plot(tf, -Omega_z*tf, "--", color="#e74c3c", label=r"theory: $-\Omega\sin\phi\,t$")
axes[1].set_title("Precession angle versus time")
axes[1].set_xlabel("time (s)"); axes[1].set_ylabel("precession angle (rad)")
axes[1].legend(); axes[1].grid(alpha=0.3)
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

print("=== FOUCAULT PRECESSION ===")
print(f"  measured precession rate = {empirical:+.4f} rad/s")
print(f"  theory -Omega sin(phi)   = {-Omega_z:+.4f} rad/s")
print("  the swing plane rotates at Omega sin(latitude): a laboratory proof of Earth's rotation.")
```

## Summary

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\textbf{Newton: } && \text{absolute space and time; the rotating bucket; real acceleration.} \\[3pt]
&\textbf{Leibniz/Mach: } && \text{relationalism; inertia from the distant matter (Mach's principle).} \\[3pt]
&\textbf{Galilean spacetime: } && \text{bundle over time; absolute simultaneity; no absolute velocity; invariant acceleration.} \\[3pt]
&\textbf{Galilean transform: } && \mathbf r'=\mathbf r-\mathbf V t,\ t'=t;\ \mathbf v'=\mathbf v-\mathbf V;\ \mathbf a'=\mathbf a. \\[3pt]
&\textbf{Fictitious forces: } && \text{centrifugal, Coriolis, Euler — coordinate effects of a non-inertial frame.} \\[3pt]
&\textbf{Evidence: } && \text{Foucault pendulum, cyclones, projectile deflection, Eötvös, equatorial bulge.} \\[3pt]
&\textbf{Breakdown: } && \text{special relativity (relative simultaneity); general relativity (dynamical spacetime).}
\end{aligned}
$$

The classical picture of space and time is a coherent set of assumptions with a precise geometric meaning, empirical support (Foucault), and a well-defined boundary (relativity). Knowing both makes clear what "inertial frame" and "fictitious force" really are.
