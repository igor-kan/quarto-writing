Series are sums with infinitely many terms, and their central paradox is that the same expression can be a perfectly ordinary number, a divergent process, or a physically meaningful regularized value depending on how you interpret it. The geometric series is the prototype; the Riemann zeta function is the master key; and identities like \(1+2+3+\cdots=-\tfrac{1}{12}\) are the spectacular edge of the theory.

This article moves from the real line to the complex plane, through several notions of summation, to the constants that fall out — \(\ln 2\), \(e\), \(\pi^2/6\), \(\pi^4/90\) — and the ones that stubbornly do not.

1 The Geometric Series

\[ \sum_{n=0}^\infty z^n=\frac{1}{1-z}\quad\text{for }|z|<1, \] with partial sums \(S_N=\frac{1-z^{N}}{1-z}\) and remainder \(R_N=\frac{z^{N}}{1-z}\). Convergence is absolute for \(|z|<1\) and diverges for \(|z|>1\); on \(|z|=1\) it converges only at \(z=-1\) (to \(1/2\), non-absolutely) and diverges elsewhere.

1.1 Real and Complex Differences

On the real line, \(|r|<1\) is just an interval. In the complex plane, the region of convergence is a disk, and the picture is richer:

  • Geometric decay: \(|z^n|=|z|^n\to0\) iff \(|z|<1\); the phase of \(z\) controls oscillation.
  • Radius of convergence: the series has radius \(R=1\); the boundary is where all the action is.
  • Termwise operations: inside the disk, power series can be differentiated and integrated term by term: \[ \sum_{n\ge0} n z^{n-1}=\frac{1}{(1-z)^2},\qquad \sum_{n\ge1}\frac{z^n}{n}=-\ln(1-z). \]
  • Analytic continuation: the function \(1/(1-z)\) is defined for all \(z\ne1\), even though the series only converges for \(|z|<1\). The series represents the function locally; the function extends globally. This gap between a series and its sum’s continuation is the seed of everything below.

1.2 Power Series and the Geometric Series as Prototype

Every analytic function is locally a power series, and the geometric series is the model: coefficients, radius, boundary behavior, differentiation, integration. The complex case adds uniform convergence on compact subsets of the disk (not on the whole open disk), and boundary phenomena like Abel’s theorem on radial limits.

2 Convergence and Summability

A series \(\sum a_n\) converges (in the ordinary sense) to \(L\) if partial sums \(S_N\to L\). Facts:

  • Convergence requires \(a_n\to0\) (not sufficient: harmonic series).
  • Absolute convergence (\(\sum|a_n|<\infty\)) implies convergence and is reorder-invariant.
  • Conditional convergence (convergent but not absolutely) is reorder-sensitive: Riemann’s rearrangement theorem. Example: the alternating harmonic series converges to \(\ln2\) but can be rearranged to any value.

When a series does not converge, we may still assign it a value by a summability method:

Method Idea Example: \(1-1+1-\cdots\)
Cesàro limit of averages of partial sums \(1/2\)
Abel \(\lim_{z\to1^-}\sum a_n z^n\) \(1/2\)
Borel Laplace-transform weighting \(1/2\)
Zeta regularization \(a_n=n^s \Rightarrow \zeta(-s)\) —

Each method agrees with ordinary convergence when it exists, and extends the notion of “sum.”

3 Ramanujan and the Regularized Sums

3.1 Grandi’s Series: \(1-1+1-1+\cdots = \tfrac12\)

Partial sums oscillate \(1,0,1,0,\dots\). Their Cesàro means are \(\frac{S_0+\cdots+S_N}{N+1}\to\frac12\), and the Abel sum is \[ \lim_{z\to1^-}\sum_{n\ge0}(-z)^n=\lim_{z\to1^-}\frac{1}{1+z}=\frac12. \] So the natural regularized value is \(1/2\) — not a “true” sum but a consistent extension.

3.2 \(1+1+1+\cdots = -\tfrac12\)

By the same Abel/zeta logic, \(\zeta(0)=-\tfrac12\) “sums” the constant series to \(-1/2\) in the regularized sense.

3.3 Ramanujan’s Sum: \(1+2+3+\cdots = -\tfrac{1}{12}\)

This is the most famous identity of the genre. It is not an ordinary sum — the series diverges — but the analytic continuation of \(\zeta(s)=\sum n^{-s}\) to \(s=-1\) gives \[ \zeta(-1)=\sum_{n=1}^\infty n \ \Big|_{\text{regularized}}=-\frac{1}{12}. \] How it is derived (sketch): by Euler–Maclaurin / functional equation, \[ \zeta(s)=2^s\pi^{s-1}\sin\!\left(\frac{\pi s}{2}\right)\Gamma(1-s)\,\zeta(1-s), \] and evaluating at \(s=-1\) (using \(\zeta(2)=\pi^2/6\), \(\Gamma(2)=1\), \(\sin(-\pi/2)=-1\)) yields \(-\frac{1}{12}\).

Why it matters physically: the same value appears in the Casimir effect (force between parallel plates), in bosonic string theory (the critical dimension \(D=26\)), and in renormalization, because the regularized vacuum energy involves \(\sum n\). The mathematics of analytic continuation makes the divergent expression mean something.

4 The Riemann Zeta Function

\[ \zeta(s)=\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{1}{n^s}\quad(\operatorname{Re}s>1), \] with the Euler product \[ \zeta(s)=\prod_{p\ \text{prime}}\frac{1}{1-p^{-s}}, \] encoding unique factorization, and the functional equation that continues it to all \(s\ne1\). Its zeros govern the distribution of primes — the Riemann hypothesis. Special values:

\[ \zeta(0)=-\frac12,\qquad \zeta(-1)=-\frac{1}{12},\qquad \zeta(-2k)=0\ (\text{trivial zeros}),\qquad \zeta(2)=\frac{\pi^2}{6}. \]

4.1 Even Powers: Closed Forms in \(\pi\)

For positive integers \(k\), \[ \zeta(2k)=(-1)^{k+1}\,\frac{B_{2k}\,(2\pi)^{2k}}{2\,(2k)!}, \] where \(B_{2k}\) are the Bernoulli numbers. Examples:

\[ \zeta(2)=\frac{\pi^2}{6},\qquad \zeta(4)=\frac{\pi^4}{90},\qquad \zeta(6)=\frac{\pi^6}{945},\qquad \zeta(8)=\frac{\pi^8}{9450}. \]

Every even zeta value is a rational multiple of \(\pi^{2k}\) — a genuine closed form.

4.2 Odd Powers: No Known Closed Form

For odd arguments \(>1\) there is no known closed form. The history:

  • Apéry (1978) proved \(\zeta(3)\) (Apéry’s constant) is irrational, but it is not known to be a rational multiple of \(\pi^3\) or to have any closed form.
  • \(\zeta(5),\zeta(7),\dots\) are not even known to be irrational in general.
  • There are special identities (§ below) expressing certain odd sums via \(\ln2\), \(\pi\), and \(\zeta(3)\), but no general formula.

This asymmetry between even and odd zeta values is one of the deepest unexplained patterns in number theory.

5 Special Sums and Their Values

A catalogue worth memorizing:

\[ \sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{1}{2^n}=1 \quad(\text{Zeno}),\qquad \sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{1}{n!}=e, \] \[ \sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n}=\ln2,\qquad \sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{1}{n^2}=\frac{\pi^2}{6},\qquad \sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{1}{n^4}=\frac{\pi^4}{90}. \]

  • The geometric sum resolves Zeno’s paradox (see the Cauchy article).
  • \(e=\sum 1/n!\) is the fastest-converging of the constants.
  • \(\ln2\) comes from the Mercator series \(-\ln(1-x)=\sum x^n/n\) at \(x=-1\) (Abel-justified on the boundary).
  • The Basel problem \(\zeta(2)=\pi^2/6\) was Euler’s 1735 triumph; \(\zeta(4)=\pi^4/90\) followed.

Related identities: \(\sum 1/(2n-1)^2=\pi^2/8\); \(\sum (-1)^{n+1}/(2n-1)=\pi/4\) (Gregory–Leibniz); Catalan’s constant \(G=\sum(-1)^n/(2n+1)^2\) has no known closed form (another open problem); and \(\sum_{n\ge1} H_n/n^2=2\zeta(3)\) relates harmonic sums to odd zeta values.

6 What Is a Closed-Form Expression?

A closed form is an expression built from a fixed, finite set of standard operations — arithmetic, powers, roots, exponentials, logarithms, trigonometric functions, and named constants (\(\pi\), \(e\), \(\gamma\), \(\varphi\)) — that gives a value exactly in one evaluation. Non-examples include infinite series, integrals, limits, and recursively defined quantities unless they reduce to the standard functions.

Value Closed form? Notes
\(\zeta(2)\) yes \(\pi^2/6\)
\(\zeta(4)\) yes \(\pi^4/90\)
\(\zeta(3)\) no known irrational (Apéry), no known closed form
\(e\) yes (as a limit/series) \(e=\sum1/n!\); one of the definitions
\(\ln2\) yes as the alternating harmonic sum
Fibonacci \(F_n\) yes Binet: \((\varphi^n-\psi^n)/\sqrt5\)
\(1+2+3+\cdots\) not an ordinary sum regularized value \(-1/12\), not a closed form
\(\sum 1/n!\) not a closed form per se but equals \(e\), which is named

The distinction is partly historical and partly structural: \(\zeta(2k)\) has a closed form because the Bernoulli numbers are “the right constants”; \(\zeta(2k+1)\) resists because no such reduction exists (or at least none is known). Naming a constant (like \(\gamma\) or \(G\)) is not the same as finding a closed form.

7.2 Special Sums: 1, ln 2, e, π²/6, π⁴/90

Code 
import numpy as np
import math
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

N = 20000
ns = np.arange(1, N)
fact_n = np.arange(1, 171)
fact_terms = np.array([1 / math.factorial(k) for k in fact_n])
series = {
    r"$\sum 1/2^n \to 1$": (ns, np.abs(np.cumsum(1 / 2.0 ** ns) - 1)),
    r"$\sum 1/n! \to e$": (fact_n, np.abs(np.cumsum(fact_terms) - np.e)),
    r"$\sum 1/n^2 \to \pi^2/6$": (ns, np.abs(np.cumsum(1.0 / ns ** 2) - np.pi ** 2 / 6)),
    r"$\sum (-1)^{n+1}/n \to \ln 2$": (ns, np.abs(np.cumsum((-1.0) ** (ns + 1) / ns) - np.log(2))),
}
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(10, 5))
for label, (x, err) in series.items():
    ax.loglog(x, np.maximum(err, 1e-18), label=label)
ax.set_title("Convergence of special series to their closed forms")

7.3 Even Zeta Values: Closed Forms via Bernoulli Numbers

Code 
from scipy.special import bernoulli, zeta as zeta_fn
import numpy as np
import math

print("=== ZETA(2k) = (-1)^(k+1) B_2k (2π)^(2k) / (2 (2k)!) ===")
print(f"{'k':>3} {'closed form':>20} {'scipy zeta(2k)':>18} {'match':>8}")
for k in [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]:
    B2k = bernoulli(2 * k)[-1]
    closed = (-1) ** (k + 1) * B2k * (2 * np.pi) ** (2 * k) / (2 * math.factorial(2 * k))
    exact = float(zeta_fn(2 * k, 1))
    print(f"{k:>3} {closed:20.12f} {exact:18.12f} {abs(closed-exact) < 1e-9}")

print("\n  every even zeta value is a rational multiple of pi^(2k).")
print("  odd values (zeta(3), zeta(5), ...) have NO known closed form.")

7.4 Analytic Continuation, Cesàro, and Ramanujan

Code 
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import mpmath as mp

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(13, 4.7))

The laboratory shows the whole arc: geometric convergence in the plane; the special sums converging to their famous constants at different rates; even zeta values reduced to Bernoulli numbers times \(\pi^{2k}\); and the analytic continuation and summability methods that make sense of divergent series — including Ramanujan’s \(-\tfrac{1}{12}\).

8 Summary

\[ \begin{aligned} &\textbf{Geometric: } && \sum z^n=\frac{1}{1-z}\ (|z|<1);\ \text{disk of convergence; analytic continuation.} \\[3pt] &\textbf{Summability: } && \text{Cesàro, Abel, Borel, zeta; } 1-1+1-\cdots=\tfrac12. \\[3pt] &\textbf{Ramanujan: } && 1+2+3+\cdots=-\tfrac{1}{12}=\zeta(-1)\ \text{(regularized).} \\[3pt] &\textbf{Zeta: } && \zeta(s)=\sum n^{-s}=\prod_p(1-p^{-s})^{-1};\ \text{functional equation.} \\[3pt] &\textbf{Even values: } && \zeta(2k)=(-1)^{k+1}\frac{B_{2k}(2\pi)^{2k}}{2(2k)!};\ \zeta(2)=\frac{\pi^2}{6},\ \zeta(4)=\frac{\pi^4}{90}. \\[3pt] &\textbf{Odd values: } && \text{no known closed form; } \zeta(3) \text{ irrational (Apéry).} \\[3pt] &\textbf{Special sums: } && \sum 2^{-n}=1,\ \sum(-1)^{n+1}/n=\ln2,\ \sum 1/n!=e. \\[3pt] &\textbf{Closed form: } && \text{finite expression in standard functions/constants; } \zeta(2k) \text{ yes, } \zeta(2k+1) \text{ no.} \end{aligned} \]

Series are the bridge between the discrete and the continuous — and between the real line and the complex plane. The same sums appear in the harmonic analysis of strings and music, in the Basel problem’s aftermath in number theory, and in the renormalized infinities of quantum field theory.

## The Geometric Series

$$
\sum_{n=0}^\infty z^n=\frac{1}{1-z}\quad\text{for }|z|<1,
$$
with partial sums $S_N=\frac{1-z^{N}}{1-z}$ and remainder $R_N=\frac{z^{N}}{1-z}$. Convergence is **absolute** for $|z|<1$ and **diverges** for $|z|>1$; on $|z|=1$ it converges only at $z=-1$ (to $1/2$, non-absolutely) and diverges elsewhere.

### Real and Complex Differences

On the real line, $|r|<1$ is just an interval. In the complex plane, the region of convergence is a **disk**, and the picture is richer:

- **Geometric decay:** $|z^n|=|z|^n\to0$ iff $|z|<1$; the phase of $z$ controls oscillation.
- **Radius of convergence:** the series has radius $R=1$; the boundary is where all the action is.
- **Termwise operations:** inside the disk, power series can be differentiated and integrated term by term:
$$
\sum_{n\ge0} n z^{n-1}=\frac{1}{(1-z)^2},\qquad
\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{z^n}{n}=-\ln(1-z).
$$
- **Analytic continuation:** the function $1/(1-z)$ is defined for all $z\ne1$, even though the series only converges for $|z|<1$. The series *represents* the function locally; the function extends globally. This gap between a series and its sum's continuation is the seed of everything below.

### Power Series and the Geometric Series as Prototype

Every analytic function is locally a power series, and the geometric series is the model: coefficients, radius, boundary behavior, differentiation, integration. The complex case adds **uniform convergence on compact subsets** of the disk (not on the whole open disk), and boundary phenomena like Abel's theorem on radial limits.

---

## Convergence and Summability

A series $\sum a_n$ **converges** (in the ordinary sense) to $L$ if partial sums $S_N\to L$. Facts:

- Convergence requires $a_n\to0$ (not sufficient: harmonic series).
- **Absolute convergence** ($\sum|a_n|<\infty$) implies convergence and is reorder-invariant.
- **Conditional convergence** (convergent but not absolutely) is reorder-sensitive: Riemann's rearrangement theorem. Example: the [alternating harmonic series](cauchy-completeness-weierstrass-and-series-convergence.qmd) converges to $\ln2$ but can be rearranged to any value.

When a series does not converge, we may still assign it a value by a **summability method**:

| Method | Idea | Example: $1-1+1-\cdots$ |
|---|---|---|
| **Cesàro** | limit of averages of partial sums | $1/2$ |
| **Abel** | $\lim_{z\to1^-}\sum a_n z^n$ | $1/2$ |
| **Borel** | Laplace-transform weighting | $1/2$ |
| **Zeta regularization** | $a_n=n^s \Rightarrow \zeta(-s)$ | — |

Each method agrees with ordinary convergence when it exists, and extends the notion of "sum."

## Ramanujan and the Regularized Sums

### Grandi's Series: $1-1+1-1+\cdots = \tfrac12$

Partial sums oscillate $1,0,1,0,\dots$. Their **Cesàro means** are $\frac{S_0+\cdots+S_N}{N+1}\to\frac12$, and the **Abel sum** is
$$
\lim_{z\to1^-}\sum_{n\ge0}(-z)^n=\lim_{z\to1^-}\frac{1}{1+z}=\frac12.
$$
So the natural regularized value is $1/2$ — not a "true" sum but a consistent extension.

### $1+1+1+\cdots = -\tfrac12$

By the same Abel/zeta logic, $\zeta(0)=-\tfrac12$ "sums" the constant series to $-1/2$ in the regularized sense.

### Ramanujan's Sum: $1+2+3+\cdots = -\tfrac{1}{12}$

This is the most famous identity of the genre. It is **not** an ordinary sum — the series diverges — but the **analytic continuation** of $\zeta(s)=\sum n^{-s}$ to $s=-1$ gives
$$
\zeta(-1)=\sum_{n=1}^\infty n \ \Big|_{\text{regularized}}=-\frac{1}{12}.
$$
How it is derived (sketch): by **Euler–Maclaurin / functional equation**,
$$
\zeta(s)=2^s\pi^{s-1}\sin\!\left(\frac{\pi s}{2}\right)\Gamma(1-s)\,\zeta(1-s),
$$
and evaluating at $s=-1$ (using $\zeta(2)=\pi^2/6$, $\Gamma(2)=1$, $\sin(-\pi/2)=-1$) yields $-\frac{1}{12}$.

**Why it matters physically:** the same value appears in the **Casimir effect** (force between parallel plates), in **bosonic string theory** (the critical dimension $D=26$), and in renormalization, because the regularized vacuum energy involves $\sum n$. The mathematics of analytic continuation makes the divergent expression mean something.

## The Riemann Zeta Function

$$
\zeta(s)=\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{1}{n^s}\quad(\operatorname{Re}s>1),
$$
with the **Euler product**
$$
\zeta(s)=\prod_{p\ \text{prime}}\frac{1}{1-p^{-s}},
$$
encoding unique factorization, and the **functional equation** that continues it to all $s\ne1$. Its zeros govern the distribution of primes — the [Riemann hypothesis](foundations-of-arithmetic-primes-radicals-and-the-riemann-hypothesis.qmd). Special values:

$$
\zeta(0)=-\frac12,\qquad \zeta(-1)=-\frac{1}{12},\qquad \zeta(-2k)=0\ (\text{trivial zeros}),\qquad \zeta(2)=\frac{\pi^2}{6}.
$$

### Even Powers: Closed Forms in $\pi$

For positive integers $k$,
$$
\zeta(2k)=(-1)^{k+1}\,\frac{B_{2k}\,(2\pi)^{2k}}{2\,(2k)!},
$$
where $B_{2k}$ are the **Bernoulli numbers**. Examples:

$$
\zeta(2)=\frac{\pi^2}{6},\qquad
\zeta(4)=\frac{\pi^4}{90},\qquad
\zeta(6)=\frac{\pi^6}{945},\qquad
\zeta(8)=\frac{\pi^8}{9450}.
$$

Every **even** zeta value is a rational multiple of $\pi^{2k}$ — a genuine closed form.

### Odd Powers: No Known Closed Form

For odd arguments $>1$ there is **no known closed form**. The history:

- **Apéry (1978)** proved $\zeta(3)$ (Apéry's constant) is **irrational**, but it is not known to be a rational multiple of $\pi^3$ or to have any closed form.
- $\zeta(5),\zeta(7),\dots$ are not even known to be irrational in general.
- There are special identities (§ below) expressing certain odd sums via $\ln2$, $\pi$, and $\zeta(3)$, but no general formula.

This asymmetry between even and odd zeta values is one of the deepest unexplained patterns in number theory.

---

## Special Sums and Their Values

A catalogue worth memorizing:

$$
\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{1}{2^n}=1 \quad(\text{Zeno}),\qquad
\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{1}{n!}=e,
$$
$$
\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n}=\ln2,\qquad
\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{1}{n^2}=\frac{\pi^2}{6},\qquad
\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{1}{n^4}=\frac{\pi^4}{90}.
$$

- The geometric sum resolves **Zeno's paradox** (see [the Cauchy article](cauchy-completeness-weierstrass-and-series-convergence.qmd)).
- $e=\sum 1/n!$ is the fastest-converging of the constants.
- $\ln2$ comes from the Mercator series $-\ln(1-x)=\sum x^n/n$ at $x=-1$ (Abel-justified on the boundary).
- The **Basel problem** $\zeta(2)=\pi^2/6$ was Euler's 1735 triumph; $\zeta(4)=\pi^4/90$ followed.

Related identities: $\sum 1/(2n-1)^2=\pi^2/8$; $\sum (-1)^{n+1}/(2n-1)=\pi/4$ (Gregory–Leibniz); **Catalan's constant** $G=\sum(-1)^n/(2n+1)^2$ has no known closed form (another open problem); and $\sum_{n\ge1} H_n/n^2=2\zeta(3)$ relates harmonic sums to odd zeta values.

---

## What Is a Closed-Form Expression?

A **closed form** is an expression built from a fixed, finite set of standard operations — arithmetic, powers, roots, exponentials, logarithms, trigonometric functions, and named constants ($\pi$, $e$, $\gamma$, $\varphi$) — that gives a value exactly in one evaluation. Non-examples include infinite series, integrals, limits, and recursively defined quantities unless they reduce to the standard functions.

| Value | Closed form? | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| $\zeta(2)$ | yes | $\pi^2/6$ |
| $\zeta(4)$ | yes | $\pi^4/90$ |
| $\zeta(3)$ | **no known** | irrational (Apéry), no known closed form |
| $e$ | yes (as a limit/series) | $e=\sum1/n!$; one of the definitions |
| $\ln2$ | yes | as the alternating harmonic sum |
| Fibonacci $F_n$ | yes | Binet: $(\varphi^n-\psi^n)/\sqrt5$ |
| $1+2+3+\cdots$ | not an ordinary sum | regularized value $-1/12$, not a closed form |
| $\sum 1/n!$ | not a closed form per se | but equals $e$, which is named |

The distinction is partly historical and partly structural: $\zeta(2k)$ has a closed form because the Bernoulli numbers are "the right constants"; $\zeta(2k+1)$ resists because no such reduction exists (or at least none is known). Naming a constant (like $\gamma$ or $G$) is not the same as finding a closed form.

## Laboratory: Series in Action

### Geometric Series in the Complex Plane

```{python}
#| label: fig-geometric-complex
#| fig-cap: "Partial sums of the complex geometric series. For |z|<1 (left) the partial sums spiral inward to 1/(1−z); for |z|>1 (right) they fly outward and never settle. The disk of convergence is the real difference between real and complex series, and its boundary — the unit circle — is where the most delicate behavior occurs."

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(12.5, 5))
for ax, z, title in [(axes[0], 0.6 + 0.4j, "|z|<1: converges to 1/(1−z)"),
                     (axes[1], 1.05 + 0.2j, "|z|>1: diverges")]:
    S = [0]
    for n in range(30):
        S.append(S[-1] + z ** n)
    S = np.array(S)
    ax.plot(S.real, S.imag, "o-", color="#2980b9", ms=3)
    if abs(z) < 1:
        ax.plot((1/(1-z)).real, (1/(1-z)).imag, "r*", ms=15, label="1/(1−z)")
    ax.set_title(title); ax.set_aspect("equal"); ax.grid(alpha=0.3)
    ax.set_xlabel("Re"); ax.set_ylabel("Im"); ax.legend()
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

for z in [0.6 + 0.4j, 0.9 * np.exp(1j), 1.05 + 0.2j]:
    S = sum(z ** n for n in range(200))
    print(f"  z={z:.3f}: 200-term partial sum = {S:.5f},  1/(1−z) = {1/(1-z):.5f}")
```

### Special Sums: 1, ln 2, e, π²/6, π⁴/90

```{python}
#| label: fig-special-sums
#| fig-cap: "Error (on a log scale) of partial sums against their closed-form values for four famous series. The geometric and factorial series converge geometrically (straight lines on log scale); the Basel series converges like 1/n; the alternating harmonic series, though convergent to ln 2, converges only like 1/n. Convergence rate depends on the series, not just on the value."

import numpy as np
import math
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

N = 20000
ns = np.arange(1, N)
fact_n = np.arange(1, 171)
fact_terms = np.array([1 / math.factorial(k) for k in fact_n])
series = {
    r"$\sum 1/2^n \to 1$": (ns, np.abs(np.cumsum(1 / 2.0 ** ns) - 1)),
    r"$\sum 1/n! \to e$": (fact_n, np.abs(np.cumsum(fact_terms) - np.e)),
    r"$\sum 1/n^2 \to \pi^2/6$": (ns, np.abs(np.cumsum(1.0 / ns ** 2) - np.pi ** 2 / 6)),
    r"$\sum (-1)^{n+1}/n \to \ln 2$": (ns, np.abs(np.cumsum((-1.0) ** (ns + 1) / ns) - np.log(2))),
}
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(10, 5))
for label, (x, err) in series.items():
    ax.loglog(x, np.maximum(err, 1e-18), label=label)
ax.set_title("Convergence of special series to their closed forms")
ax.set_xlabel("terms"); ax.set_ylabel("absolute error"); ax.legend(); ax.grid(alpha=0.3, which="both")
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

print("=== SPECIAL SUMS AT N=1000 ===")
n = np.arange(1, 1001)
fact = np.array([1 / math.factorial(k) for k in range(1001)])
print(f"  sum 1/2^n      = {np.sum(1/2.0**n):.12f}  (1)")
print(f"  sum 1/n!       = {np.sum(fact):.12f}  (e={np.e:.12f})")
print(f"  alternating H  = {np.sum((-1.0)**(n+1)/n):.12f}  (ln2={np.log(2):.12f})")
print(f"  Basel          = {np.sum(1/n**2):.12f}  (pi^2/6={np.pi**2/6:.12f})")
print(f"  zeta(4)        = {np.sum(1/n**4):.12f}  (pi^4/90={np.pi**4/90:.12f})")
```

### Even Zeta Values: Closed Forms via Bernoulli Numbers

```{python}
from scipy.special import bernoulli, zeta as zeta_fn
import numpy as np
import math

print("=== ZETA(2k) = (-1)^(k+1) B_2k (2π)^(2k) / (2 (2k)!) ===")
print(f"{'k':>3} {'closed form':>20} {'scipy zeta(2k)':>18} {'match':>8}")
for k in [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]:
    B2k = bernoulli(2 * k)[-1]
    closed = (-1) ** (k + 1) * B2k * (2 * np.pi) ** (2 * k) / (2 * math.factorial(2 * k))
    exact = float(zeta_fn(2 * k, 1))
    print(f"{k:>3} {closed:20.12f} {exact:18.12f} {abs(closed-exact) < 1e-9}")

print("\n  every even zeta value is a rational multiple of pi^(2k).")
print("  odd values (zeta(3), zeta(5), ...) have NO known closed form.")
```

### Analytic Continuation, Cesàro, and Ramanujan

```{python}
#| label: fig-zeta-continuation
#| fig-cap: "The Riemann zeta function. Left: on the real axis the defining series ζ(s)=Σn^(−s) converges only for s>1, where it is plotted; the analytic continuation extends it to all s≠1 (dashed). Right: the Cesàro means of the divergent series 1−1+1−1⋯ converge to 1/2, illustrating how a summability method assigns a value to a divergent series. Analytic continuation gives 1+2+3+⋯ the regularized value ζ(−1)=−1/12."

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import mpmath as mp

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(13, 4.7))

s_pos = np.linspace(1.02, 6, 200)
zeta_pos = [float(mp.zeta(s)) for s in s_pos]
s_all = np.linspace(-3, 0.98, 200)
zeta_all = [float(mp.zeta(s)) for s in s_all]
axes[0].plot(s_pos, zeta_pos, color="#2980b9", label=r"series (s>1)")
axes[0].plot(s_all, zeta_all, "--", color="#e74c3c", label="analytic continuation")
axes[0].axhline(0, color="k", lw=0.6)
for s0, col in [(0, "#27ae60"), (-1, "#8e44ad"), (-2, "#16a085")]:
    axes[0].plot(s0, float(mp.zeta(s0)), "o", color=col)
    axes[0].annotate(f"ζ({s0})={float(mp.zeta(s0)):.3f}", (s0, float(mp.zeta(s0))),
                     textcoords="offset points", xytext=(4, 6), color=col, fontsize=9)
axes[0].set_title("Riemann zeta and its continuation")
axes[0].set_xlabel("s"); axes[0].set_ylabel("ζ(s)"); axes[0].legend(); axes[0].grid(alpha=0.3)

partial = np.cumsum([(-1.0) ** n for n in range(0, 200)])   # +1, -1, +1, ...
cesaro = np.cumsum(partial) / np.arange(1, 201)
axes[1].plot(range(1, 201), partial, ".", ms=4, color="#95a5a6", label="partial sums")
axes[1].plot(range(1, 201), cesaro, color="#e74c3c", lw=2, label="Cesàro means")
axes[1].axhline(0.5, color="#27ae60", ls="--", label="→ 1/2")
axes[1].set_title(r"Cesàro summation of $1-1+1-1\cdots$")
axes[1].set_xlabel("N"); axes[1].set_ylim(-0.1, 1.1); axes[1].legend(); axes[1].grid(alpha=0.3)
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

print("=== REGULARIZED SUMS VIA ZETA ===")
print(f"  1+1+1+...   -> zeta(0)   = {float(mp.zeta(0)):.6f}  (-1/2)")
print(f"  1+2+3+...   -> zeta(-1)  = {float(mp.zeta(-1)):.6f}  (-1/12)")
print(f"  1^2+2^2+... -> zeta(-2)  = {float(mp.zeta(-2)):.6f}  (trivial zero)")
print(f"  Cesàro sum of 1-1+1-...  = {cesaro[-1]:.6f}")
print("\n  these are analytic-continuation values, NOT ordinary convergence.")
```

The laboratory shows the whole arc: geometric convergence in the plane; the special sums converging to their famous constants at different rates; even zeta values reduced to Bernoulli numbers times $\pi^{2k}$; and the analytic continuation and summability methods that make sense of divergent series — including Ramanujan's $-\tfrac{1}{12}$.

## Summary

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\textbf{Geometric: } && \sum z^n=\frac{1}{1-z}\ (|z|<1);\ \text{disk of convergence; analytic continuation.} \\[3pt]
&\textbf{Summability: } && \text{Cesàro, Abel, Borel, zeta; } 1-1+1-\cdots=\tfrac12. \\[3pt]
&\textbf{Ramanujan: } && 1+2+3+\cdots=-\tfrac{1}{12}=\zeta(-1)\ \text{(regularized).} \\[3pt]
&\textbf{Zeta: } && \zeta(s)=\sum n^{-s}=\prod_p(1-p^{-s})^{-1};\ \text{functional equation.} \\[3pt]
&\textbf{Even values: } && \zeta(2k)=(-1)^{k+1}\frac{B_{2k}(2\pi)^{2k}}{2(2k)!};\ \zeta(2)=\frac{\pi^2}{6},\ \zeta(4)=\frac{\pi^4}{90}. \\[3pt]
&\textbf{Odd values: } && \text{no known closed form; } \zeta(3) \text{ irrational (Apéry).} \\[3pt]
&\textbf{Special sums: } && \sum 2^{-n}=1,\ \sum(-1)^{n+1}/n=\ln2,\ \sum 1/n!=e. \\[3pt]
&\textbf{Closed form: } && \text{finite expression in standard functions/constants; } \zeta(2k) \text{ yes, } \zeta(2k+1) \text{ no.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Series are the bridge between the discrete and the continuous — and between the real line and the complex plane. The same sums appear in the [harmonic analysis of strings and music](../physics/standing-waves-plucked-strings-harmonics-beats-and-doppler.qmd), in the [Basel problem's aftermath in number theory](foundations-of-arithmetic-primes-radicals-and-the-riemann-hypothesis.qmd), and in the renormalized infinities of quantum field theory.
