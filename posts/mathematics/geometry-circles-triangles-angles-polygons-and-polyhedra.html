Geometry is the oldest branch of mathematics and still the most visual. It begins with a point and a line, builds angles, triangles, and circles, then polygons and polyhedra — and at every scale it hides results of startling economy: the angle sum of any triangle is \(180^\circ\), inscribed angles depend only on the arc they subtend, there are exactly five regular polyhedra, and for every convex polyhedron \(V-E+F=2\).

This article collects the basic and advanced properties that make geometry the backbone of trigonometry, physics, astronomy, computer graphics, and architecture.

1 Angles and Their Measurement

An angle is the rotation between two rays from a common vertex. Four units matter:

  • Degrees: a full turn is \(360^\circ\).
  • Radians: a full turn is \(2\pi\); \(1\text{ rad}=180/\pi\approx57.2958^\circ\).
  • Gradians (gons): a full turn is \(400^\text{g}\) (surveying).
  • Degrees–minutes–seconds (DMS): \(1^\circ=60'\) (arcminutes), \(1'=60''\) (arcseconds). Thus \(1'' = 1/3600^\circ\), and \(1\text{ rad}\approx206264.8''\).

Conversions: \[ \theta_{\text{rad}}=\theta_{\text{deg}}\cdot\frac{\pi}{180},\qquad \theta_{\text{deg}}=\theta_{\text{rad}}\cdot\frac{180}{\pi},\qquad D^\circ M' S'' = D+\frac{M}{60}+\frac{S}{3600}. \]

Small-angle approximations (central in physics and astronomy): \[ \sin\theta\approx\tan\theta\approx\theta,\qquad \cos\theta\approx1-\frac{\theta^2}{2}\quad(\theta\ll1\text{ rad}). \]

Angular size. For an object of physical size \(s\) at distance \(d\), its angular size is \(\theta\approx s/d\) radians — the basis of the parsec (\(1\) pc \(=206265\) AU, the distance at which \(1\) AU subtends \(1''\)), of stellar parallax, and of the telescope’s resolution.

Angle classes: acute (\(<90^\circ\)), right (\(=90^\circ\)), obtuse, straight (\(180^\circ\)), reflex (\(>180^\circ\)), complementary (sum \(90^\circ\)), supplementary (sum \(180^\circ\)). With parallel lines cut by a transversal: corresponding angles are equal, alternate interior angles are equal, and co-interior angles are supplementary.

2 Triangles

A triangle has angles summing to \(180^\circ\) (in Euclidean geometry), and exterior angle equals the sum of the two remote interior angles.

2.1 Congruence and Similarity

  • Congruence (\(\cong\)): SSS, SAS, ASA, AAS, and HL (right triangles). Congruent triangles have equal corresponding sides and angles.
  • Similarity (\(\sim\)): AA, SSS, SAS. Corresponding angles equal, corresponding sides proportional with ratio \(k\). Then areas scale by \(k^2\) — the first instance of the square-cube law.

2.2 The Laws of Trigonometry

For a triangle with sides \(a,b,c\) opposite angles \(A,B,C\) and circumradius \(R\):

\[ \frac{a}{\sin A}=\frac{b}{\sin B}=\frac{c}{\sin C}=2R \qquad(\text{law of sines}), \]

\[ c^2=a^2+b^2-2ab\cos C \qquad(\text{law of cosines}), \]

\[ \tan\frac{A-B}{2}=\frac{a-b}{a+b}\cot\frac{C}{2} \qquad(\text{law of tangents}). \]

The law of cosines generalizes the Pythagorean theorem; when \(C=90^\circ\) it reduces to \(c^2=a^2+b^2\).

2.3 Area

\[ \Delta=\frac12\,bh=\frac12\,ab\sin C=\frac{abc}{4R}=rs=\sqrt{s(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)}, \] where \(s=\frac{a+b+c}{2}\) is the semiperimeter, \(r\) the inradius, and the last is Heron’s formula. Brahmagupta’s formula generalizes it to cyclic quadrilaterals: \(\Delta=\sqrt{(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)(s-d)}\).

2.4 The Four Classical Centers

Center Definition Notable property
Centroid \(G\) intersection of medians divides each median \(2:1\); center of mass
Circumcenter \(O\) perpendicular bisectors center of the circumcircle; \(OA=OB=OC=R\)
Incenter \(I\) angle bisectors center of the incircle; equidistant from the sides
Orthocenter \(H\) altitudes the feet form the orthic triangle

Euler line: \(O\), \(G\), and \(H\) are collinear with \(OG:GH=1:2\), so \(H=A+B+C-2O\) in coordinate form. The nine-point circle passes through the three midpoints, the three altitude feet, and the three midpoints from each vertex to the orthocenter; its center is the midpoint of \(OH\) and its radius is \(R/2\).

Inequalities: the triangle inequality \(a+b>c\); the isoperimetric fact that among triangles with a fixed perimeter, the equilateral one has the largest area.

3 Circles

A circle is the set of points at distance \(r\) from a center. Key vocabulary: chord, secant, tangent, arc (minor/major), sector, segment, central angle, inscribed angle, sagitta.

3.1 Angle Theorems

  • Central angle \(\theta\) subtends an arc of length \(L=r\theta\) (radians) and sector area \(\frac12 r^2\theta\).
  • Inscribed angle theorem: an inscribed angle equals half its subtended central angle. Hence all angles in the same segment are equal.
  • Thales’ theorem: an angle subtended by a diameter is a right angle (special case).
  • Cyclic quadrilateral: opposite angles are supplementary (\(A+C=B+D=180^\circ\)); Ptolemy’s theorem for a cyclic quadrilateral: \(AC\cdot BD=AB\cdot CD+BC\cdot AD\).
  • Tangent–chord: the angle between a tangent and a chord equals the inscribed angle in the alternate segment.

3.2 Power of a Point

For a point \(P\) and a circle, the power is \(\operatorname{Pow}(P)=d^2-r^2\) where \(d\) is the distance to the center. It equals the product of directed distances along any line through \(P\):

  • Two chords \(AB\) and \(CD\) meeting at \(P\): \(PA\cdot PB=PC\cdot PD\).
  • Two secants: \(PA\cdot PB=PC\cdot PD\).
  • Tangent: \(PT^2=\operatorname{Pow}(P)\) when \(P\) is outside; \(PT^2=PA\cdot PB\).

3.3 Arcs, Sectors, and Segments

With \(\theta\) in radians: \[ L=r\theta,\qquad A_{\text{sector}}=\frac12 r^2\theta,\qquad A_{\text{segment}}=\frac12 r^2(\theta-\sin\theta),\qquad \text{sagitta }=r(1-\cos(\theta/2)). \]

Inscribed/circumscribed polygons bound the circumference and give Archimedes’ computation of π (see the constants article): \(2n\sin(\pi/n)<\pi<2n\tan(\pi/n)\) for a unit circle.

4 Quadrilaterals and Polygons

4.1 Quadrilaterals

Angles sum to \(360^\circ\). Notable types and properties:

Shape Defining property Area
Parallelogram both pairs of opposite sides parallel \(bh\)
Rectangle parallelogram with a right angle \(bh\)
Rhombus parallelogram with equal sides \(\frac12 d_1 d_2\)
Square rectangle + rhombus \(s^2\)
Trapezoid one pair of parallel sides \(\frac12(b_1+b_2)h\)
Kite two pairs of adjacent equal sides \(\frac12 d_1 d_2\)

For a general quadrilateral with diagonals \(d_1,d_2\) and angle \(\theta\) between them: \(\Delta=\frac12 d_1 d_2\sin\theta\) (Bretschneider’s formula generalizes Heron’s to all quadrilaterals).

4.2 Regular Polygons

For a regular \(n\)-gon with circumradius \(R\):

\[ \text{interior angle}=\frac{(n-2)180^\circ}{n},\qquad \text{exterior angle}=\frac{360^\circ}{n}, \] \[ \text{apothem}=a=R\cos\frac{\pi}{n},\qquad \text{side}=2R\sin\frac{\pi}{n},\qquad A=\frac12 n a s=\frac12 n R^2\sin\frac{2\pi}{n}. \]

The number of diagonals is \(\binom{n}{2}-n=\frac{n(n-3)}{2}\). Only \(n=3,4,6\) tile the plane; only certain \(n\) are constructible with straightedge and compass (Gauss–Wantzel): \(n=2^k\) times a product of distinct Fermat primes (\(3,5,17,257,65537,\dots\)).

Star polygons \(\{n/k\}\) connect every \(k\)-th vertex; the pentagram \(\{5/2\}\) and the golden ratio are intimately related.

5 Polyhedra and Solid Geometry

A polyhedron is a solid bounded by polygonal faces, with edges and vertices.

5.1 Euler’s Formula

For any convex polyhedron, \[ V-E+F=2. \] For a torus-like solid the right-hand side is the Euler characteristic \(\chi=2-2g\), where \(g\) is the number of handles (genus). Euler’s formula is the first theorem of topology.

5.2 The Platonic Solids

Exactly five regular convex polyhedra exist (a fact known to the Greeks):

Solid Faces \(V\) \(E\) \(F\) Face
Tetrahedron 4 triangles 4 6 4 triangle
Cube 6 squares 8 12 6 square
Octahedron 8 triangles 6 12 8 triangle
Dodecahedron 12 pentagons 20 30 12 pentagon
Icosahedron 20 triangles 12 30 20 triangle

Each satisfies \(V-E+F=2\). The five correspond to the five ways to have three or more regular polygons meeting with angle sum \(<360^\circ\); they also give the symmetry groups tied to the exceptional phenomena of mathematics (the quintic, the golden ratio, \(E_8\)).

The Archimedean solids (13) use two or more regular polygon types; the Catalan solids are their duals; the Johnson solids (92) are the remaining strictly convex polyhedra with regular faces.

5.3 Solids of Revolution and Scaling

Solid Surface area Volume
Prism \(Ph+2B\) \(Bh\)
Pyramid \(\frac12 Pl+B\) \(\frac13 Bh\)
Cylinder \(2\pi r h+2\pi r^2\) \(\pi r^2 h\)
Cone \(\pi r\ell+\pi r^2\) \(\frac13\pi r^2 h\)
Sphere \(4\pi r^2\) \(\frac43\pi r^3\)

The sphere is the isoperimetric optimum: it encloses the greatest volume for a given surface area (and the circle does so in the plane). Scaling laws: scaling a solid by \(k\) scales lengths by \(k\), areas by \(k^2\), and volumes by \(k^3\) — the square-cube law that explains why large animals need different skeletons and why bubbles coalesce.

6 Laboratory: Geometry by Computation

6.1 Triangle Centers and the Euler Line

Code 
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

A = np.array([0.0, 0.0]); B = np.array([6.0, 0.0]); C = np.array([2.0, 4.0])

def side(a, b): return np.linalg.norm(b - a)
a, b, c = side(B, C), side(A, C), side(A, B)

G = (A + B + C) / 3

def circumcenter(A, B, C):
    ax, ay = A; bx, by = B; cx, cy = C
    d = 2 * (ax * (by - cy) + bx * (cy - ay) + cx * (ay - by))
    ux = ((ax**2 + ay**2) * (by - cy) + (bx**2 + by**2) * (cy - ay) + (cx**2 + cy**2) * (ay - by)) / d
    uy = ((ax**2 + ay**2) * (cx - bx) + (bx**2 + by**2) * (ax - cx) + (cx**2 + cy**2) * (bx - ax)) / d
    return np.array([ux, uy])

O = circumcenter(A, B, C)
I = (a * A + b * B + c * C) / (a + b + c)
H = A + B + C - 2 * O

R = np.linalg.norm(O - A)
print("=== TRIANGLE CENTERS ===")
print(f"  A={A}, B={B}, C={C}")
print(f"  side lengths a={a:.3f}, b={b:.3f}, c={c:.3f}")
print(f"  centroid     G = {np.round(G, 3)}")
print(f"  circumcenter O = {np.round(O, 3)}  (R = {R:.3f})")
print(f"  incenter     I = {np.round(I, 3)}")
print(f"  orthocenter  H = {np.round(H, 3)}")

# verify collinearity O, G, H and ratio OG:GH = 1:2
v1, v2 = G - O, H - G
print(f"  O,G,H collinear? cross = {np.cross(v1, v2):.2e}")
print(f"  |OG|/|GH| = {np.linalg.norm(v1)/np.linalg.norm(v2):.4f}  (expected 0.5)")

# verify orthocenter via altitude from A: (H-A) ⟂ (B-C)
print(f"  altitude check (H-A)·(B-C) = {np.dot(H - A, B - C):.2e} (expected 0)")

theta = np.linspace(0, 2 * np.pi, 300)
plt.figure(figsize=(7.5, 7))
plt.plot(O[0] + R * np.cos(theta), O[1] + R * np.sin(theta), "k--", lw=1, label="circumcircle")
tri = np.vstack([A, B, C, A])
plt.plot(tri[:, 0], tri[:, 1], "k-", lw=1.5)
plt.plot(*zip(A, B, C, A), "ko")
plt.plot([O[0], H[0]], [O[1], H[1]], color="#e74c3c", lw=1.2, label="Euler line")
for P, name, col in [(G, "G centroid", "#2980b9"), (O, "O circumcenter", "#27ae60"),

plt.title("Triangle centers and the Euler line")
plt.gca().set_aspect("equal"); plt.legend(loc="upper right", fontsize=8); plt.grid(alpha=0.3)
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

# law of cosines check
C_angle = np.arccos(np.dot(A - C, B - C) / (np.linalg.norm(A - C) * np.linalg.norm(B - C)))

6.3 Angles: DMS, Arcseconds, and Angular Size

Code 
def to_dms(deg):
    d = int(deg)
    m = int((deg - d) * 60)
    s = (deg - d - m / 60) * 3600
    return d, m, s

print("=== ANGLE CONVERSIONS ===")
for deg in [57.29577951, 137.5077641, 0.00027778]:
    d, m, s = to_dms(deg)
    print(f"  {deg:>12.7f}° = {d}° {m}' {s:.4f}\"  = {np.radians(deg):.6f} rad")

print(f"\n  1 radian = {np.degrees(1):.6f}° = {np.degrees(1)*3600:.1f}\" (arcseconds)")
print(f"  1 arcsecond = {1/3600:.7f}° = {np.radians(1/3600):.3e} rad")

print("\n=== ANGULAR SIZE AND THE PARSEC ===")
# the Sun (1.39e6 km) at 1 AU (1.496e8 km)
size_km, dist_km = 1.39e6, 1.496e8
theta = size_km / dist_km

7 Summary

\[ \begin{aligned} &\textbf{Angles: } && 360^\circ=2\pi\text{ rad}=400^\text{g};\ 1^\circ=60'=3600'';\ \sin\theta\approx\theta. \\[3pt] &\textbf{Triangles: } && A+B+C=180^\circ;\ \text{laws of sines/cosines; Heron; four centers on the Euler line.} \\[3pt] &\textbf{Circles: } && \text{inscribed} = \tfrac12\text{ central};\ \text{Thales; power of a point; Ptolemy; } L=r\theta. \\[3pt] &\textbf{Polygons: } && \text{interior } \frac{(n-2)180}{n};\ A=\frac12 nR^2\sin\frac{2\pi}{n};\ \frac{n(n-3)}{2} \text{ diagonals.} \\[3pt] &\textbf{Polyhedra: } && \text{five Platonic solids; } V-E+F=2;\ \text{Archimedean/Catalan/Johnson.} \\[3pt] &\textbf{Solids: } && \text{prism/pyramid/cylinder/cone/sphere formulas; isoperimetric; scaling } L,\,L^2,\,L^3. \end{aligned} \]

Geometry supplies the shapes; analysis supplies the limits of those shapes as polygon counts and dimensions grow. The circle’s exhaustion into polygons is exactly how Archimedes computed π — one more place where a static figure becomes an iterative method.

::: {.callout-note appearance="simple"}
*Geometry is the oldest branch of mathematics and still the most visual. It begins with a point and a line, builds angles, triangles, and circles, then polygons and polyhedra — and at every scale it hides results of startling economy: the angle sum of any triangle is $180^\circ$, inscribed angles depend only on the arc they subtend, there are exactly five regular polyhedra, and for **every** convex polyhedron $V-E+F=2$.*

This article collects the basic and advanced properties that make geometry the backbone of trigonometry, physics, astronomy, computer graphics, and architecture.

## Angles and Their Measurement

An **angle** is the rotation between two rays from a common vertex. Four units matter:

- **Degrees:** a full turn is $360^\circ$.
- **Radians:** a full turn is $2\pi$; $1\text{ rad}=180/\pi\approx57.2958^\circ$.
- **Gradians (gons):** a full turn is $400^\text{g}$ (surveying).
- **Degrees–minutes–seconds (DMS):** $1^\circ=60'$ (arcminutes), $1'=60''$ (arcseconds). Thus $1'' = 1/3600^\circ$, and $1\text{ rad}\approx206264.8''$.

**Conversions:**
$$
\theta_{\text{rad}}=\theta_{\text{deg}}\cdot\frac{\pi}{180},\qquad
\theta_{\text{deg}}=\theta_{\text{rad}}\cdot\frac{180}{\pi},\qquad
D^\circ M' S'' = D+\frac{M}{60}+\frac{S}{3600}.
$$

**Small-angle approximations** (central in physics and astronomy):
$$
\sin\theta\approx\tan\theta\approx\theta,\qquad \cos\theta\approx1-\frac{\theta^2}{2}\quad(\theta\ll1\text{ rad}).
$$

**Angular size.** For an object of physical size $s$ at distance $d$, its angular size is $\theta\approx s/d$ radians — the basis of the **parsec** ($1$ pc $=206265$ AU, the distance at which $1$ AU subtends $1''$), of stellar **parallax**, and of the telescope's **resolution**.

Angle classes: acute ($<90^\circ$), right ($=90^\circ$), obtuse, straight ($180^\circ$), reflex ($>180^\circ$), complementary (sum $90^\circ$), supplementary (sum $180^\circ$). With parallel lines cut by a transversal: corresponding angles are equal, alternate interior angles are equal, and co-interior angles are supplementary.

## Triangles

A triangle has angles summing to **$180^\circ$** (in Euclidean geometry), and exterior angle equals the sum of the two remote interior angles.

### Congruence and Similarity

- **Congruence** ($\cong$): SSS, SAS, ASA, AAS, and HL (right triangles). Congruent triangles have equal corresponding sides and angles.
- **Similarity** ($\sim$): AA, SSS, SAS. Corresponding angles equal, corresponding sides proportional with ratio $k$. Then **areas scale by $k^2$** — the first instance of the square-cube law.

### The Laws of Trigonometry

For a triangle with sides $a,b,c$ opposite angles $A,B,C$ and circumradius $R$:

$$
\frac{a}{\sin A}=\frac{b}{\sin B}=\frac{c}{\sin C}=2R
\qquad(\text{law of sines}),
$$

$$
c^2=a^2+b^2-2ab\cos C
\qquad(\text{law of cosines}),
$$

$$
\tan\frac{A-B}{2}=\frac{a-b}{a+b}\cot\frac{C}{2}
\qquad(\text{law of tangents}).
$$

The law of cosines generalizes the Pythagorean theorem; when $C=90^\circ$ it reduces to $c^2=a^2+b^2$.

### Area

$$
\Delta=\frac12\,bh=\frac12\,ab\sin C=\frac{abc}{4R}=rs=\sqrt{s(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)},
$$
where $s=\frac{a+b+c}{2}$ is the semiperimeter, $r$ the inradius, and the last is **Heron's formula**. **Brahmagupta's formula** generalizes it to cyclic quadrilaterals: $\Delta=\sqrt{(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)(s-d)}$.

### The Four Classical Centers

| Center | Definition | Notable property |
|---|---|---|
| **Centroid** $G$ | intersection of medians | divides each median $2:1$; center of mass |
| **Circumcenter** $O$ | perpendicular bisectors | center of the circumcircle; $OA=OB=OC=R$ |
| **Incenter** $I$ | angle bisectors | center of the incircle; equidistant from the sides |
| **Orthocenter** $H$ | altitudes | the feet form the orthic triangle |

**Euler line:** $O$, $G$, and $H$ are collinear with $OG:GH=1:2$, so $H=A+B+C-2O$ in coordinate form. The **nine-point circle** passes through the three midpoints, the three altitude feet, and the three midpoints from each vertex to the orthocenter; its center is the midpoint of $OH$ and its radius is $R/2$.

Inequalities: the **triangle inequality** $a+b>c$; the **isoperimetric** fact that among triangles with a fixed perimeter, the equilateral one has the largest area.

## Circles

A **circle** is the set of points at distance $r$ from a center. Key vocabulary: **chord**, **secant**, **tangent**, **arc** (minor/major), **sector**, **segment**, **central angle**, **inscribed angle**, **sagitta**.

### Angle Theorems

- **Central angle** $\theta$ subtends an arc of length $L=r\theta$ (radians) and sector area $\frac12 r^2\theta$.
- **Inscribed angle theorem:** an inscribed angle equals **half** its subtended central angle. Hence all angles in the same segment are equal.
- **Thales' theorem:** an angle subtended by a diameter is a right angle (special case).
- **Cyclic quadrilateral:** opposite angles are supplementary ($A+C=B+D=180^\circ$); **Ptolemy's theorem** for a cyclic quadrilateral: $AC\cdot BD=AB\cdot CD+BC\cdot AD$.
- **Tangent–chord:** the angle between a tangent and a chord equals the inscribed angle in the alternate segment.

### Power of a Point

For a point $P$ and a circle, the **power** is $\operatorname{Pow}(P)=d^2-r^2$ where $d$ is the distance to the center. It equals the product of directed distances along any line through $P$:

- Two chords $AB$ and $CD$ meeting at $P$: $PA\cdot PB=PC\cdot PD$.
- Two secants: $PA\cdot PB=PC\cdot PD$.
- Tangent: $PT^2=\operatorname{Pow}(P)$ when $P$ is outside; $PT^2=PA\cdot PB$.

### Arcs, Sectors, and Segments

With $\theta$ in radians:
$$
L=r\theta,\qquad
A_{\text{sector}}=\frac12 r^2\theta,\qquad
A_{\text{segment}}=\frac12 r^2(\theta-\sin\theta),\qquad
\text{sagitta }=r(1-\cos(\theta/2)).
$$

Inscribed/circumscribed polygons bound the circumference and give **Archimedes' computation of π** (see [the constants article](iterative-methods-and-computing-constants-pi-and-eulers-constant.qmd)): $2n\sin(\pi/n)<\pi<2n\tan(\pi/n)$ for a unit circle.

## Quadrilaterals and Polygons

### Quadrilaterals

Angles sum to $360^\circ$. Notable types and properties:

| Shape | Defining property | Area |
|---|---|---|
| Parallelogram | both pairs of opposite sides parallel | $bh$ |
| Rectangle | parallelogram with a right angle | $bh$ |
| Rhombus | parallelogram with equal sides | $\frac12 d_1 d_2$ |
| Square | rectangle + rhombus | $s^2$ |
| Trapezoid | one pair of parallel sides | $\frac12(b_1+b_2)h$ |
| Kite | two pairs of adjacent equal sides | $\frac12 d_1 d_2$ |

For a general quadrilateral with diagonals $d_1,d_2$ and angle $\theta$ between them: $\Delta=\frac12 d_1 d_2\sin\theta$ (**Bretschneider's** formula generalizes Heron's to all quadrilaterals).

### Regular Polygons

For a regular $n$-gon with circumradius $R$:

$$
\text{interior angle}=\frac{(n-2)180^\circ}{n},\qquad
\text{exterior angle}=\frac{360^\circ}{n},
$$
$$
\text{apothem}=a=R\cos\frac{\pi}{n},\qquad
\text{side}=2R\sin\frac{\pi}{n},\qquad
A=\frac12 n a s=\frac12 n R^2\sin\frac{2\pi}{n}.
$$

The number of **diagonals** is $\binom{n}{2}-n=\frac{n(n-3)}{2}$. Only $n=3,4,6$ tile the plane; only certain $n$ are constructible with straightedge and compass (Gauss–Wantzel): $n=2^k$ times a product of **distinct Fermat primes** ($3,5,17,257,65537,\dots$).

**Star polygons** $\{n/k\}$ connect every $k$-th vertex; the pentagram $\{5/2\}$ and the golden ratio are intimately related.

## Polyhedra and Solid Geometry

A **polyhedron** is a solid bounded by polygonal **faces**, with **edges** and **vertices**.

### Euler's Formula

For any convex polyhedron,
$$
V-E+F=2.
$$
For a torus-like solid the right-hand side is the **Euler characteristic** $\chi=2-2g$, where $g$ is the number of handles (genus). Euler's formula is the first theorem of topology.

### The Platonic Solids

Exactly **five** regular convex polyhedra exist (a fact known to the Greeks):

| Solid | Faces | $V$ | $E$ | $F$ | Face |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Tetrahedron | 4 triangles | 4 | 6 | 4 | triangle |
| Cube | 6 squares | 8 | 12 | 6 | square |
| Octahedron | 8 triangles | 6 | 12 | 8 | triangle |
| Dodecahedron | 12 pentagons | 20 | 30 | 12 | pentagon |
| Icosahedron | 20 triangles | 12 | 30 | 20 | triangle |

Each satisfies $V-E+F=2$. The five correspond to the five ways to have three or more regular polygons meeting with angle sum $<360^\circ$; they also give the symmetry groups tied to the **exceptional** phenomena of mathematics (the quintic, the golden ratio, $E_8$).

The **Archimedean solids** (13) use two or more regular polygon types; the **Catalan solids** are their duals; the **Johnson solids** (92) are the remaining strictly convex polyhedra with regular faces.

### Solids of Revolution and Scaling

| Solid | Surface area | Volume |
|---|---|---|
| Prism | $Ph+2B$ | $Bh$ |
| Pyramid | $\frac12 Pl+B$ | $\frac13 Bh$ |
| Cylinder | $2\pi r h+2\pi r^2$ | $\pi r^2 h$ |
| Cone | $\pi r\ell+\pi r^2$ | $\frac13\pi r^2 h$ |
| Sphere | $4\pi r^2$ | $\frac43\pi r^3$ |

The sphere is the **isoperimetric optimum**: it encloses the greatest volume for a given surface area (and the circle does so in the plane). **Scaling laws:** scaling a solid by $k$ scales lengths by $k$, areas by $k^2$, and volumes by $k^3$ — the **square-cube law** that explains why large animals need different skeletons and why bubbles coalesce.

## Laboratory: Geometry by Computation

### Triangle Centers and the Euler Line

```{python}
#| label: fig-triangle-centers
#| fig-cap: "A triangle with its four classical centers. The centroid G is the average of the vertices; the circumcenter O is equidistant from them; the incenter I is equidistant from the sides; and the orthocenter H is where the altitudes meet. O, G, and H are collinear on the Euler line, with OG:GH = 1:2. The dashed circle is the circumcircle."

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

A = np.array([0.0, 0.0]); B = np.array([6.0, 0.0]); C = np.array([2.0, 4.0])

def side(a, b): return np.linalg.norm(b - a)
a, b, c = side(B, C), side(A, C), side(A, B)

G = (A + B + C) / 3

def circumcenter(A, B, C):
    ax, ay = A; bx, by = B; cx, cy = C
    d = 2 * (ax * (by - cy) + bx * (cy - ay) + cx * (ay - by))
    ux = ((ax**2 + ay**2) * (by - cy) + (bx**2 + by**2) * (cy - ay) + (cx**2 + cy**2) * (ay - by)) / d
    uy = ((ax**2 + ay**2) * (cx - bx) + (bx**2 + by**2) * (ax - cx) + (cx**2 + cy**2) * (bx - ax)) / d
    return np.array([ux, uy])

O = circumcenter(A, B, C)
I = (a * A + b * B + c * C) / (a + b + c)
H = A + B + C - 2 * O

R = np.linalg.norm(O - A)
print("=== TRIANGLE CENTERS ===")
print(f"  A={A}, B={B}, C={C}")
print(f"  side lengths a={a:.3f}, b={b:.3f}, c={c:.3f}")
print(f"  centroid     G = {np.round(G, 3)}")
print(f"  circumcenter O = {np.round(O, 3)}  (R = {R:.3f})")
print(f"  incenter     I = {np.round(I, 3)}")
print(f"  orthocenter  H = {np.round(H, 3)}")

# verify collinearity O, G, H and ratio OG:GH = 1:2
v1, v2 = G - O, H - G
print(f"  O,G,H collinear? cross = {np.cross(v1, v2):.2e}")
print(f"  |OG|/|GH| = {np.linalg.norm(v1)/np.linalg.norm(v2):.4f}  (expected 0.5)")

# verify orthocenter via altitude from A: (H-A) ⟂ (B-C)
print(f"  altitude check (H-A)·(B-C) = {np.dot(H - A, B - C):.2e} (expected 0)")

theta = np.linspace(0, 2 * np.pi, 300)
plt.figure(figsize=(7.5, 7))
plt.plot(O[0] + R * np.cos(theta), O[1] + R * np.sin(theta), "k--", lw=1, label="circumcircle")
tri = np.vstack([A, B, C, A])
plt.plot(tri[:, 0], tri[:, 1], "k-", lw=1.5)
plt.plot(*zip(A, B, C, A), "ko")
plt.plot([O[0], H[0]], [O[1], H[1]], color="#e74c3c", lw=1.2, label="Euler line")
for P, name, col in [(G, "G centroid", "#2980b9"), (O, "O circumcenter", "#27ae60"),
                     (I, "I incenter", "#e67e22"), (H, "H orthocenter", "#8e44ad")]:
    plt.plot(*P, "o", color=col)
    plt.annotate(name, P, textcoords="offset points", xytext=(6, 4), color=col, fontsize=9)
plt.title("Triangle centers and the Euler line")
plt.gca().set_aspect("equal"); plt.legend(loc="upper right", fontsize=8); plt.grid(alpha=0.3)
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

# law of cosines check
C_angle = np.arccos(np.dot(A - C, B - C) / (np.linalg.norm(A - C) * np.linalg.norm(B - C)))
print(f"\n  law of cosines: c²={c**2:.4f}  a²+b²−2ab·cosC={a**2+b**2-2*a*b*np.cos(C_angle):.4f}")
```

### Regular Polygons and Polyhedra

```{python}
import numpy as np

print("=== REGULAR POLYGONS (unit circumradius) ===")
print(f"{'n':>3} {'interior°':>10} {'exterior°':>10} {'side':>8} {'apothem':>9} {'area':>9} {'diagonals':>10}")
for n in [3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 10, 12, 20]:
    interior = (n - 2) * 180 / n
    exterior = 360 / n
    side = 2 * np.sin(np.pi / n)
    apothem = np.cos(np.pi / n)
    area = 0.5 * n * side * apothem
    diag = n * (n - 3) // 2
    print(f"{n:>3} {interior:10.3f} {exterior:10.3f} {side:8.5f} {apothem:9.5f} {area:9.5f} {diag:>10}")
print(f"\n  as n→∞ the area → π = {np.pi:.5f} (the circle), from polygons.")

print("\n=== EULER'S FORMULA V − E + F = 2 ===")
solids = [("tetrahedron", 4, 6, 4), ("cube", 8, 12, 6), ("octahedron", 6, 12, 8),
          ("dodecahedron", 20, 30, 12), ("icosahedron", 12, 30, 20)]
for name, V, E, F in solids:
    print(f"  {name:14s}: V={V:>2}  E={E:>2}  F={F:>2}  ->  V−E+F = {V-E+F}")
print("  every convex polyhedron satisfies V−E+F=2 (the Euler characteristic).")
```

### Angles: DMS, Arcseconds, and Angular Size

```{python}
def to_dms(deg):
    d = int(deg)
    m = int((deg - d) * 60)
    s = (deg - d - m / 60) * 3600
    return d, m, s

print("=== ANGLE CONVERSIONS ===")
for deg in [57.29577951, 137.5077641, 0.00027778]:
    d, m, s = to_dms(deg)
    print(f"  {deg:>12.7f}° = {d}° {m}' {s:.4f}\"  = {np.radians(deg):.6f} rad")

print(f"\n  1 radian = {np.degrees(1):.6f}° = {np.degrees(1)*3600:.1f}\" (arcseconds)")
print(f"  1 arcsecond = {1/3600:.7f}° = {np.radians(1/3600):.3e} rad")

print("\n=== ANGULAR SIZE AND THE PARSEC ===")
# the Sun (1.39e6 km) at 1 AU (1.496e8 km)
size_km, dist_km = 1.39e6, 1.496e8
theta = size_km / dist_km
print(f"  Sun at 1 AU: angular size = {theta:.6f} rad = {np.degrees(theta):.4f}° = {np.degrees(theta)*60:.2f}'")
print(f"  1 parsec = distance where 1 AU subtends 1 arcsec = {1/np.radians(1/3600):.1f} AU")
print("  small-angle: theta ≈ s/d, so a star at 1 pc with parallax 1\" is 3.26 ly away.")
```

## Summary

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\textbf{Angles: } && 360^\circ=2\pi\text{ rad}=400^\text{g};\ 1^\circ=60'=3600'';\ \sin\theta\approx\theta. \\[3pt]
&\textbf{Triangles: } && A+B+C=180^\circ;\ \text{laws of sines/cosines; Heron; four centers on the Euler line.} \\[3pt]
&\textbf{Circles: } && \text{inscribed} = \tfrac12\text{ central};\ \text{Thales; power of a point; Ptolemy; } L=r\theta. \\[3pt]
&\textbf{Polygons: } && \text{interior } \frac{(n-2)180}{n};\ A=\frac12 nR^2\sin\frac{2\pi}{n};\ \frac{n(n-3)}{2} \text{ diagonals.} \\[3pt]
&\textbf{Polyhedra: } && \text{five Platonic solids; } V-E+F=2;\ \text{Archimedean/Catalan/Johnson.} \\[3pt]
&\textbf{Solids: } && \text{prism/pyramid/cylinder/cone/sphere formulas; isoperimetric; scaling } L,\,L^2,\,L^3.
\end{aligned}
$$

Geometry supplies the shapes; analysis supplies the limits of those shapes as polygon counts and dimensions grow. The circle's exhaustion into polygons is exactly how Archimedes computed π — one more place where a static figure becomes an [iterative method](iterative-methods-and-computing-constants-pi-and-eulers-constant.qmd).
