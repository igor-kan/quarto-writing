Mathematics is full of constants we cannot write down finitely — π, e, γ — and the only way to know them is to compute them. Every such computation is an iteration: a rule that turns a guess into a better guess. The history of π is the history of increasingly clever iterations, from Archimedes’ polygons to the spigot algorithms that stream digits.

This article is about machines for constants: fixed points, root-finding, and the series that produce digits.

1 What Makes an Iteration Good

An iterative method produces a sequence \(x_{n+1}=g(x_n)\) that should converge to a solution \(x^\*\) of some problem. Three questions govern quality:

  1. Does it converge? Usually because \(g\) is a contraction: \(|g(x)-g(y)|\le L|x-y|\) with \(L<1\) (Banach fixed-point theorem).
  2. How fast? The order of convergence \(p\): \(|x_{n+1}-x^*|\le C|x_n-x^*|^p\). Linear (\(p=1\)), quadratic (\(p=2\)), etc.
  3. At what cost? Function evaluations per step, and numerical stability.
Method Order Needs derivative? Notes
Bisection linear, rate \(1/2\) no robust, halves the interval each step
Fixed-point linear (or better) no converges iff \(\lvert g'\rvert<1\) near the root
Secant \(\approx1.618\) no superlinear
Newton (Newton–Raphson) quadratic yes doubles correct digits each step
Halley cubic second derivative higher cost per step

Newton’s method. For \(f(x)=0\), \[ x_{n+1}=x_n-\frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)}. \] It is the tangent-line root approximation, and its quadratic convergence is why it dominates scientific computing — when it works. It fails when \(f'=0\), can cycle, and needs a good initial guess.

2 π: Two Thousand Years of Iteration

2.1 Archimedes’ Method of Exhaustion

Around 250 BCE, Archimedes bounded a circle between an inscribed and a circumscribed regular polygon, starting from a hexagon and doubling the number of sides:

\[ \text{inscribed } n\text{-gon perimeter} = 2n\sin\frac{\pi}{n}, \qquad \text{circumscribed } n\text{-gon perimeter} = 2n\tan\frac{\pi}{n} \quad(\text{unit circle}). \]

By the half-angle identities he iterated to a 96-gon, obtaining

\[ 3\frac{10}{71}=\frac{223}{71}<\pi<\frac{22}{7}=3\frac{1}{7}, \qquad \text{i.e. } 3.1408<\pi<3.1429. \]

The error of the inscribed/circumscribed bound shrinks like \(O(1/n^2)\), so the number of correct digits grows like \(2\log_{10} n\) — only linear in the number of doublings. Archimedes’ idea also defines π as the common limit of two sequences, a prototype of the squeeze theorem.

2.2 π by Infinite Series

Gregory–Leibniz (Madhava): \[ \frac{\pi}{4}=1-\frac13+\frac15-\frac17+\cdots \] Beautiful, but converges like \(1/n\) — about three terms per correct digit. It is the classic warning that “convergent” does not mean “computable quickly.”

Machin (1706): \[ \frac{\pi}{4}=4\arctan\frac15-\arctan\frac1{239}, \qquad \arctan x=\sum_{k\ge0}\frac{(-1)^k x^{2k+1}}{2k+1}. \] Because \(x=1/5\) and \(x=1/239\) are small, each term adds several digits; Machin computed 100 digits by hand. Machin-like formulas still drive record computations.

Nilakantha (faster): \[ \pi=3+\frac{4}{2\cdot3\cdot4}-\frac{4}{4\cdot5\cdot6}+\frac{4}{6\cdot7\cdot8}-\cdots \] Terms decay like \(1/n^3\): faster than Leibniz, still polynomial.

Ramanujan and beyond. Ramanujan’s series add about 8 digits per term: \[ \frac1\pi=\frac{2\sqrt2}{9801}\sum_{k\ge0}\frac{(4k)!(1103+26390k)}{(k!)^4\,396^{4k}}. \] The Chudnovsky algorithm adds about 14 digits per term and has powered all recent record computations (trillions of digits). The BBP formula is a spigot: it extracts hexadecimal digits of π without computing earlier ones.

2.3 Newton’s Contribution

In 1666 Newton computed π by integrating the circle’s area with a binomial series (a forerunner of the general binomial theorem) and then inverting a series — an early instance of both power-series computation and Newton’s method. In modern language, π can be obtained from \(\arcsin\): \[ \pi=6\arcsin\frac12=6\sum_{k\ge0}\frac{(2k)!}{4^k(k!)^2(2k+1)2^{2k+1}}, \] whose terms decay geometrically. Newton’s dual legacies — the series and the root-finding iteration — are the two engines of constant computation.

3 Euler’s Constant γ

The Euler–Mascheroni constant is \[ \gamma=\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(H_n-\ln n\right),\qquad H_n=1+\frac12+\cdots+\frac1n, \] where the limit exists because \(H_n-\ln n\) is decreasing and bounded below (convergence follows by bounding \(\int_1^n dx/x\)). Numerically,

\[ \gamma\approx0.5772156649015328606065120900824\ldots \]

3.1 Where It Appears

  • Gamma function: \(\gamma=-\Gamma'(1)\); the Laurent expansion of \(\Gamma\) about its pole has \(\gamma\) as the constant term.
  • Number theory: Mertens’ theorems, the prime-counting error terms, and the Dirichlet divisor problem all involve \(\gamma\).
  • Analysis: asymptotic expansions of harmonic sums, integrals like \(\int_0^\infty e^{-x}\ln x\,dx=-\gamma\).

3.2 The Open Question

It is unknown whether \(\gamma\) is rational, irrational, or transcendental. It is not even known to be irrational — one of the most embarrassing open problems in mathematics, in stark contrast to \(e\) (Hermite) and \(\pi\) (Lindemann).

3.3 Algorithms for γ

  1. Direct (very slow): compute \(H_n-\ln n\). The error decays like \(1/(2n)\), so a million terms give only ~6 correct digits. (The error is \(1/(2n)+O(1/n^2)\).)
  2. Euler–Maclaurin acceleration: \[ H_n=\ln n+\gamma+\frac{1}{2n}-\frac{1}{12n^2}+\frac{1}{120n^4}-\frac{1}{252n^6}+\cdots \] so \[ \gamma \approx H_n-\ln n-\frac{1}{2n}+\frac{1}{12n^2}-\frac{1}{120n^4}. \] With \(n=10^6\) this already gives ~20 correct digits — the classic trick.
  3. Brent–McMillan: the state-of-the-art multiprecision algorithm, combining an exponentially accelerated series with binary splitting; it computes \(\gamma\) to \(D\) digits in time \(O(D\log^3 D)\).
  4. Gamma-function and zeta connections: algorithms using the derivative of \(\Gamma\) at 1 or the Laurent coefficients of \(\zeta\) (Stieltjes constants) provide independent routes.

The lesson mirrors π: the naive limit converges, but acceleration transforms an impractical computation into a routine one.

4 Laboratory: The Machines in Action

4.1 Archimedes’ Polygons

Code 
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def archimedes(k):
    n = 3 * 2 ** k                        # number of sides
    lower = n * np.sin(np.pi / n)         # inscribed perimeter / diameter
    upper = n * np.tan(np.pi / n)         # circumscribed perimeter / diameter
    return n, lower, upper

ks = np.arange(0, 14)
ns, lows, ups = zip(*[archimedes(k) for k in ks])
lows, ups = np.array(lows), np.array(ups)

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(9.5, 5))
ax.semilogx(ns, lows, "o-", color="#2980b9", label="inscribed lower bound")
ax.semilogx(ns, ups, "s-", color="#e74c3c", label="circumscribed upper bound")
ax.axhline(np.pi, color="#27ae60", ls="--", label=r"$\pi$")
ax.set_title("Archimedes' exhaustion: bounds on π from 3·2^k-gons")
ax.set_xlabel("number of sides n"); ax.set_ylabel("perimeter / diameter")
ax.legend(); ax.grid(alpha=0.3, which="both")
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

4.2 Series Convergence Rates

Code 
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def error_leibniz(N):
    k = np.arange(N)
    return abs(4 * np.sum((-1.0) ** k / (2 * k + 1)) - np.pi)

def error_nilakantha(N):
    k = np.arange(1, N + 1)
    s = 3 + 4 * np.sum(((-1.0) ** (k + 1)) / (2 * k * (2 * k + 1) * (2 * k + 2)))
    return abs(s - np.pi)

def error_machin(N):
    def arctan_inv(x, N):
        k = np.arange(N)
        return np.sum((-1.0) ** k / ((2 * k + 1) * x ** (2 * k + 1)))
    return abs(16 * arctan_inv(5, N) - 4 * arctan_inv(239, N) - np.pi)

N = 2000
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(9.5, 5))
for name, fn, col in [("Gregory–Leibniz ~1/n", error_leibniz, "#e74c3c"),
                      ("Nilakantha ~1/n³", error_nilakantha, "#e67e22"),
                      ("Machin (geometric)", error_machin, "#27ae60")]:
    ns = np.arange(2, N)
    errs = [max(fn(int(k)), 1e-18) for k in ns]
    ax.loglog(ns, errs, color=col, label=name)
ax.set_title("π series: error vs number of terms")
ax.set_xlabel("terms"); ax.set_ylabel("|approximation − π|"); ax.legend(); ax.grid(alpha=0.3, which="both")
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

4.3 Newton’s Method: Quadratic Convergence

Code 
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def newton(f, fp, x0, n=6):
    xs = [x0]
    for _ in range(n):
        xs.append(xs[-1] - f(xs[-1]) / fp(xs[-1]))
    return np.array(xs)

f = lambda x: x ** 2 - 2
fp = lambda x: 2 * x
xs = newton(f, fp, 1.0, 6)
errs = np.abs(xs - np.sqrt(2))

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(13, 4.6))
gx = np.linspace(0.5, 2.0, 200)
axes[0].plot(gx, f(gx), color="#2980b9", label=r"$f(x)=x^2-2$")
axes[0].axhline(0, color="k", lw=0.8)
axes[0].plot(xs, f(xs), "ro")
for i in range(len(xs) - 1):
    axes[0].annotate("", xy=(xs[i + 1], 0), xytext=(xs[i], f(xs[i])),
                     arrowprops=dict(arrowstyle="->", color="#e74c3c", lw=1))
axes[0].set_title("Newton iterates on f(x)=x²−2")
axes[0].set_xlabel("x"); axes[0].legend(); axes[0].grid(alpha=0.3)

axes[1].semilogy(range(len(errs)), errs, "o-", color="#8e44ad")
axes[1].set_title("Error per iteration (quadratic convergence)")
axes[1].set_xlabel("iteration"); axes[1].set_ylabel("|x_n − √2|"); axes[1].grid(alpha=0.3, which="both")
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

print("=== NEWTON: CORRECT DIGITS DOUBLE ===")
for i, (x, e) in enumerate(zip(xs, errs)):

4.4 Estimating Euler’s Constant

import numpy as np
GAMMA = 0.5772156649015328606

def harmonic(n):
    return np.sum(1.0 / np.arange(1, n + 1))

print("=== EULER–MASCHERONI γ ===")
print(f"  reference γ = {GAMMA:.16f}\n")
print(f"{'n':>10} {'H_n−ln n':>16} {'error':>10} {'Euler–Maclaurin':>18} {'error':>10}")
for n in [10, 100, 1000, 10_000, 100_000, 1_000_000]:
    H = harmonic(n)
    naive = H - np.log(n)
    em = H - np.log(n) - 1 / (2 * n) + 1 / (12 * n ** 2) - 1 / (120 * n ** 4)

The laboratory shows the three faces of iteration: bracketing (Archimedes squeezes π), series (representation determines convergence rate), and root-finding / acceleration (Newton doubles digits; Euler–Maclaurin turns a slow limit into a fast one). Every method here is a special case of the same contractive iteration, and the choice of representation is often more important than raw computing power.

5 Summary

\[ \begin{aligned} &\textbf{Iteration: } && x_{n+1}=g(x_n);\ \text{contraction} \Rightarrow \text{convergence; order } p \text{ decides speed.} \\[3pt] &\textbf{π (geometry): } && \text{Archimedes: } 2n\sin(\pi/n) < \pi < 2n\tan(\pi/n),\ \frac{223}{71}<\pi<\frac{22}{7}. \\[3pt] &\textbf{π (series): } && \text{Leibniz } O(1/n),\ \text{Nilakantha } O(1/n^3),\ \text{Machin geometric, Ramanujan/Chudnovsky, BBP.} \\[3pt] &\textbf{π (Newton): } && \text{arcsine/binomial series + Newton's method (quadratic).} \\[3pt] &\textbf{γ: } && \gamma=\lim(H_n-\ln n);\ \text{naive } O(1/n);\ \text{Euler–Maclaurin/Brent–McMillan accelerate; irrationality open.} \\[3pt] &\textbf{Lesson: } && \text{choose a good representation; accelerate the tail; then iterate.} \end{aligned} \]

Constants are not given; they are manufactured by iteration. The same iterative mindset reappears in dynamical systems and chaos — where iteration becomes the object of study rather than a tool — and in the Collatz problem, where a simple iteration still defies all proof.

## What Makes an Iteration Good

An **iterative method** produces a sequence $x_{n+1}=g(x_n)$ that should converge to a solution $x^\*$ of some problem. Three questions govern quality:

1. **Does it converge?** Usually because $g$ is a **contraction**: $|g(x)-g(y)|\le L|x-y|$ with $L<1$ (Banach fixed-point theorem).
2. **How fast?** The **order of convergence** $p$: $|x_{n+1}-x^*|\le C|x_n-x^*|^p$. Linear ($p=1$), quadratic ($p=2$), etc.
3. **At what cost?** Function evaluations per step, and numerical stability.

| Method | Order | Needs derivative? | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bisection | linear, rate $1/2$ | no | robust, halves the interval each step |
| Fixed-point | linear (or better) | no | converges iff $\lvert g'\rvert<1$ near the root |
| Secant | $\approx1.618$ | no | superlinear |
| Newton (Newton–Raphson) | quadratic | yes | doubles correct digits each step |
| Halley | cubic | second derivative | higher cost per step |

**Newton's method.** For $f(x)=0$,
$$
x_{n+1}=x_n-\frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)}.
$$
It is the tangent-line root approximation, and its quadratic convergence is why it dominates scientific computing — when it works. It fails when $f'=0$, can cycle, and needs a good initial guess.

## π: Two Thousand Years of Iteration

### Archimedes' Method of Exhaustion

Around 250 BCE, Archimedes bounded a circle between an **inscribed** and a **circumscribed** regular polygon, starting from a hexagon and **doubling** the number of sides:

$$
\text{inscribed } n\text{-gon perimeter} = 2n\sin\frac{\pi}{n},
\qquad
\text{circumscribed } n\text{-gon perimeter} = 2n\tan\frac{\pi}{n}
\quad(\text{unit circle}).
$$

By the half-angle identities he iterated to a **96-gon**, obtaining

$$
3\frac{10}{71}=\frac{223}{71}<\pi<\frac{22}{7}=3\frac{1}{7},
\qquad \text{i.e. } 3.1408<\pi<3.1429.
$$

The error of the inscribed/circumscribed bound shrinks like $O(1/n^2)$, so the number of correct digits grows like $2\log_{10} n$ — only linear in the number of doublings. Archimedes' idea also *defines* π as the common limit of two sequences, a prototype of the squeeze theorem.

### π by Infinite Series

**Gregory–Leibniz (Madhava):**
$$
\frac{\pi}{4}=1-\frac13+\frac15-\frac17+\cdots
$$
Beautiful, but converges like $1/n$ — about three terms per correct digit. It is the classic warning that "convergent" does not mean "computable quickly."

**Machin (1706):**
$$
\frac{\pi}{4}=4\arctan\frac15-\arctan\frac1{239},
\qquad \arctan x=\sum_{k\ge0}\frac{(-1)^k x^{2k+1}}{2k+1}.
$$
Because $x=1/5$ and $x=1/239$ are small, each term adds several digits; Machin computed 100 digits by hand. Machin-like formulas still drive record computations.

**Nilakantha (faster):**
$$
\pi=3+\frac{4}{2\cdot3\cdot4}-\frac{4}{4\cdot5\cdot6}+\frac{4}{6\cdot7\cdot8}-\cdots
$$
Terms decay like $1/n^3$: faster than Leibniz, still polynomial.

**Ramanujan and beyond.** Ramanujan's series add about **8 digits per term**:
$$
\frac1\pi=\frac{2\sqrt2}{9801}\sum_{k\ge0}\frac{(4k)!(1103+26390k)}{(k!)^4\,396^{4k}}.
$$
The **Chudnovsky** algorithm adds about **14 digits per term** and has powered all recent record computations (trillions of digits). The **BBP formula** is a spigot: it extracts hexadecimal digits of π without computing earlier ones.

### Newton's Contribution

In 1666 Newton computed π by integrating the circle's area with a **binomial series** (a forerunner of the general binomial theorem) and then inverting a series — an early instance of both power-series computation and Newton's method. In modern language, π can be obtained from $\arcsin$:
$$
\pi=6\arcsin\frac12=6\sum_{k\ge0}\frac{(2k)!}{4^k(k!)^2(2k+1)2^{2k+1}},
$$
whose terms decay geometrically. Newton's dual legacies — the **series** and the **root-finding iteration** — are the two engines of constant computation.

## Euler's Constant γ

The **Euler–Mascheroni constant** is
$$
\gamma=\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(H_n-\ln n\right),\qquad H_n=1+\frac12+\cdots+\frac1n,
$$
where the limit exists because $H_n-\ln n$ is **decreasing and bounded below** (convergence follows by bounding $\int_1^n dx/x$). Numerically,

$$
\gamma\approx0.5772156649015328606065120900824\ldots
$$

### Where It Appears

- **Gamma function:** $\gamma=-\Gamma'(1)$; the Laurent expansion of $\Gamma$ about its pole has $\gamma$ as the constant term.
- **Number theory:** Mertens' theorems, the prime-counting error terms, and the Dirichlet divisor problem all involve $\gamma$.
- **Analysis:** asymptotic expansions of harmonic sums, integrals like $\int_0^\infty e^{-x}\ln x\,dx=-\gamma$.

### The Open Question

It is **unknown whether $\gamma$ is rational, irrational, or transcendental**. It is not even known to be irrational — one of the most embarrassing open problems in mathematics, in stark contrast to $e$ (Hermite) and $\pi$ (Lindemann).

### Algorithms for γ

1. **Direct (very slow):** compute $H_n-\ln n$. The error decays like $1/(2n)$, so a million terms give only ~6 correct digits. (The error is $1/(2n)+O(1/n^2)$.)
2. **Euler–Maclaurin acceleration:**
$$
H_n=\ln n+\gamma+\frac{1}{2n}-\frac{1}{12n^2}+\frac{1}{120n^4}-\frac{1}{252n^6}+\cdots
$$
so
$$
\gamma \approx H_n-\ln n-\frac{1}{2n}+\frac{1}{12n^2}-\frac{1}{120n^4}.
$$
With $n=10^6$ this already gives ~20 correct digits — the classic trick.
3. **Brent–McMillan:** the state-of-the-art multiprecision algorithm, combining an exponentially accelerated series with binary splitting; it computes $\gamma$ to $D$ digits in time $O(D\log^3 D)$.
4. **Gamma-function and zeta connections:** algorithms using the derivative of $\Gamma$ at 1 or the Laurent coefficients of $\zeta$ (Stieltjes constants) provide independent routes.

The lesson mirrors π: **the naive limit converges, but acceleration transforms an impractical computation into a routine one.**

## Laboratory: The Machines in Action

### Archimedes' Polygons

```{python}
#| label: fig-archimedes
#| fig-cap: "Archimedes' method of exhaustion. Inscribed and circumscribed regular polygons with 3·2^k sides bracket π from below and above, and the bracket tightens. The errors are O(1/n²), so each doubling of the sides adds only a constant number of correct digits — convergence, but slow convergence, just as the method of exhaustion would suggest."

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def archimedes(k):
    n = 3 * 2 ** k                        # number of sides
    lower = n * np.sin(np.pi / n)         # inscribed perimeter / diameter
    upper = n * np.tan(np.pi / n)         # circumscribed perimeter / diameter
    return n, lower, upper

ks = np.arange(0, 14)
ns, lows, ups = zip(*[archimedes(k) for k in ks])
lows, ups = np.array(lows), np.array(ups)

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(9.5, 5))
ax.semilogx(ns, lows, "o-", color="#2980b9", label="inscribed lower bound")
ax.semilogx(ns, ups, "s-", color="#e74c3c", label="circumscribed upper bound")
ax.axhline(np.pi, color="#27ae60", ls="--", label=r"$\pi$")
ax.set_title("Archimedes' exhaustion: bounds on π from 3·2^k-gons")
ax.set_xlabel("number of sides n"); ax.set_ylabel("perimeter / diameter")
ax.legend(); ax.grid(alpha=0.3, which="both")
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

print("=== ARCHIMEDES BOUNDS ===")
for k in [0, 2, 5, 10, 13]:
    n, lo, up = archimedes(k)
    print(f"  n={n:>6}: {lo:.10f} < pi < {up:.10f}   width={up-lo:.2e}")
print(f"  true pi = {np.pi:.10f}")
print("  Archimedes' 96-gon (k=5) -> 223/71 < pi < 22/7, about 1.3e-3 wide.")
```

### Series Convergence Rates

```{python}
#| label: fig-series
#| fig-cap: "Error versus number of terms for three π series on a log-log scale. The Gregory–Leibniz alternating series converges like 1/n (three terms per digit); Nilakantha like 1/n³; the arctangent series with small argument (as used by Machin) converges geometrically, so its error plummets. The same constant can be unreachable or cheap depending on the representation you choose."

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def error_leibniz(N):
    k = np.arange(N)
    return abs(4 * np.sum((-1.0) ** k / (2 * k + 1)) - np.pi)

def error_nilakantha(N):
    k = np.arange(1, N + 1)
    s = 3 + 4 * np.sum(((-1.0) ** (k + 1)) / (2 * k * (2 * k + 1) * (2 * k + 2)))
    return abs(s - np.pi)

def error_machin(N):
    def arctan_inv(x, N):
        k = np.arange(N)
        return np.sum((-1.0) ** k / ((2 * k + 1) * x ** (2 * k + 1)))
    return abs(16 * arctan_inv(5, N) - 4 * arctan_inv(239, N) - np.pi)

N = 2000
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(9.5, 5))
for name, fn, col in [("Gregory–Leibniz ~1/n", error_leibniz, "#e74c3c"),
                      ("Nilakantha ~1/n³", error_nilakantha, "#e67e22"),
                      ("Machin (geometric)", error_machin, "#27ae60")]:
    ns = np.arange(2, N)
    errs = [max(fn(int(k)), 1e-18) for k in ns]
    ax.loglog(ns, errs, color=col, label=name)
ax.set_title("π series: error vs number of terms")
ax.set_xlabel("terms"); ax.set_ylabel("|approximation − π|"); ax.legend(); ax.grid(alpha=0.3, which="both")
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

print("=== TERMS NEEDED FOR 10 CORRECT DIGITS (approx) ===")
print("  Gregory–Leibniz : ~10^10 terms (impractical)")
print(f"  Machin (N=25)   : error = {error_machin(25):.2e}")
print(f"  Machin (N=50)   : error = {error_machin(50):.2e}")
```

### Newton's Method: Quadratic Convergence

```{python}
#| label: fig-newton
#| fig-cap: "Newton's method. Left: iterates of the tangent-line approximation on f(x)=x²−2. Right: the absolute error on a log scale, falling so fast that the number of correct digits roughly doubles each step — the signature of quadratic convergence. π can be computed the same way by solving cos(x)=0 or by Newton's arcsine series."

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def newton(f, fp, x0, n=6):
    xs = [x0]
    for _ in range(n):
        xs.append(xs[-1] - f(xs[-1]) / fp(xs[-1]))
    return np.array(xs)

f = lambda x: x ** 2 - 2
fp = lambda x: 2 * x
xs = newton(f, fp, 1.0, 6)
errs = np.abs(xs - np.sqrt(2))

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(13, 4.6))
gx = np.linspace(0.5, 2.0, 200)
axes[0].plot(gx, f(gx), color="#2980b9", label=r"$f(x)=x^2-2$")
axes[0].axhline(0, color="k", lw=0.8)
axes[0].plot(xs, f(xs), "ro")
for i in range(len(xs) - 1):
    axes[0].annotate("", xy=(xs[i + 1], 0), xytext=(xs[i], f(xs[i])),
                     arrowprops=dict(arrowstyle="->", color="#e74c3c", lw=1))
axes[0].set_title("Newton iterates on f(x)=x²−2")
axes[0].set_xlabel("x"); axes[0].legend(); axes[0].grid(alpha=0.3)

axes[1].semilogy(range(len(errs)), errs, "o-", color="#8e44ad")
axes[1].set_title("Error per iteration (quadratic convergence)")
axes[1].set_xlabel("iteration"); axes[1].set_ylabel("|x_n − √2|"); axes[1].grid(alpha=0.3, which="both")
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

print("=== NEWTON: CORRECT DIGITS DOUBLE ===")
for i, (x, e) in enumerate(zip(xs, errs)):
    print(f"  iter {i}: x={x:.16f}  error={e:.2e}  digits={-np.log10(max(e,1e-18)):.1f}")
```

### Estimating Euler's Constant

```{python}
import numpy as np
GAMMA = 0.5772156649015328606

def harmonic(n):
    return np.sum(1.0 / np.arange(1, n + 1))

print("=== EULER–MASCHERONI γ ===")
print(f"  reference γ = {GAMMA:.16f}\n")
print(f"{'n':>10} {'H_n−ln n':>16} {'error':>10} {'Euler–Maclaurin':>18} {'error':>10}")
for n in [10, 100, 1000, 10_000, 100_000, 1_000_000]:
    H = harmonic(n)
    naive = H - np.log(n)
    em = H - np.log(n) - 1 / (2 * n) + 1 / (12 * n ** 2) - 1 / (120 * n ** 4)
    print(f"{n:>10} {naive:16.12f} {abs(naive-GAMMA):10.2e} {em:18.15f} {abs(em-GAMMA):10.2e}")
print("\n  the naive limit needs ~10^6 terms for 6 digits;")
print("  Euler–Maclaurin gets ~20 digits from the same 10^6 terms.")
```

The laboratory shows the three faces of iteration: **bracketing** (Archimedes squeezes π), **series** (representation determines convergence rate), and **root-finding / acceleration** (Newton doubles digits; Euler–Maclaurin turns a slow limit into a fast one). Every method here is a special case of the same contractive iteration, and the choice of representation is often more important than raw computing power.

## Summary

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\textbf{Iteration: } && x_{n+1}=g(x_n);\ \text{contraction} \Rightarrow \text{convergence; order } p \text{ decides speed.} \\[3pt]
&\textbf{π (geometry): } && \text{Archimedes: } 2n\sin(\pi/n) < \pi < 2n\tan(\pi/n),\ \frac{223}{71}<\pi<\frac{22}{7}. \\[3pt]
&\textbf{π (series): } && \text{Leibniz } O(1/n),\ \text{Nilakantha } O(1/n^3),\ \text{Machin geometric, Ramanujan/Chudnovsky, BBP.} \\[3pt]
&\textbf{π (Newton): } && \text{arcsine/binomial series + Newton's method (quadratic).} \\[3pt]
&\textbf{γ: } && \gamma=\lim(H_n-\ln n);\ \text{naive } O(1/n);\ \text{Euler–Maclaurin/Brent–McMillan accelerate; irrationality open.} \\[3pt]
&\textbf{Lesson: } && \text{choose a good representation; accelerate the tail; then iterate.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Constants are not given; they are manufactured by iteration. The same iterative mindset reappears in [dynamical systems and chaos](dynamical-systems-chaos-and-the-butterfly-effect.qmd) — where iteration becomes the object of study rather than a tool — and in the [Collatz problem](collatz-conjecture-3x-plus-1-orbits-and-similar-iterations.qmd), where a simple iteration still defies all proof.
