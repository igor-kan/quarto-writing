1 Percentages and Interest

Everything begins with percentages. A percentage change from \(a\) to \(b\) is \((b-a)/a\). Percentage points (an APR rising from 4% to 5%) are not the same as percent (a 25% increase).

1.1 Simple vs Compound Interest

Simple interest grows linearly: \[ A=P(1+rt). \]

Compound interest, with \(m\) compounding periods per year at nominal rate \(r\): \[ A=P\left(1+\frac{r}{m}\right)^{mt}. \]

Compounding is the most important idea in finance: interest earns interest. Over long horizons the difference is enormous. As \(m\to\infty\), compounding becomes continuous: \[ A=Pe^{rt}, \] connecting finance directly to the number \(e\).

1.2 APR vs APY

  • APR (nominal annual rate): the quoted rate, ignoring compounding within the year.
  • APY / EAR (effective annual rate): what you actually pay/earn: \[ \text{APY}=\left(1+\frac{r}{m}\right)^{m}-1 \quad\xrightarrow{m\to\infty}\quad e^{r}-1. \]

APR is a marketing number; APY is the truth. A 20% APR compounded daily is an APY of \(e^{0.20}-1\approx22.1\%\).

1.3 The Rule of 72

To estimate doubling time at rate \(r\) (as a percent), \(t\approx72/r\) years — a linearization of \(2=(1+r)^t\). It is remarkably accurate for rates near 8%.

2 Time Value of Money

A dollar today is worth more than a dollar tomorrow because it can be invested. Present value discounts a future cash flow: \[ PV=\frac{FV}{(1+r)^t},\qquad FV=PV(1+r)^t. \]

Net present value (NPV) of a stream of cash flows \(C_t\) is \[ NPV=\sum_{t=0}^{T}\frac{C_t}{(1+r)^t}, \] and the internal rate of return (IRR) is the \(r\) making \(NPV=0\). These are the tools of capital budgeting and also of comparing a mortgage, a lease, or an investment.

2.1 Annuities

An annuity is a series of equal payments.

  • Future value of an ordinary annuity (payment \(PMT\), \(n\) periods, rate \(i\) per period): \[ FV=PMT\,\frac{(1+i)^n-1}{i}. \]
  • Present value: \(PV=PMT\,\dfrac{1-(1+i)^{-n}}{i}\).

A retirement account is a future-value annuity problem; a mortgage or pension is a present-value annuity problem. Note the geometric series \((1+i)^n-1)/i\) — the same mathematics as series.

3 Loans and Amortization

For a loan of principal \(P\) at periodic rate \(i\) over \(n\) periods, the level payment is derived by equating the present value of payments to the principal: \[ PMT=\frac{P\,i}{1-(1+i)^{-n}}. \]

Each payment splits into interest (\(i\times\) current balance) and principal (the rest). The amortization schedule tracks this. Early payments are mostly interest; late payments are mostly principal.

Key facts:

  • Longer term \(\Rightarrow\) lower payment but more total interest. A 30-year mortgage has smaller payments than a 15-year, but can cost roughly twice the interest overall.
  • Extra principal payments attack the balance directly and shorten the term dramatically because they remove future interest.

4 Mortgages

A mortgage is a secured amortizing loan.

  • Down payment: the fraction paid upfront; below 20% typically triggers PMI (private mortgage insurance).
  • Points: prepaid interest (1 point \(=1\%\) of the loan) that buys a lower rate.
  • Fixed vs adjustable (ARM): fixed locks the rate; an ARM resets, transferring rate risk to the borrower.
  • Closing costs: origination, title, appraisal, and recording fees.
  • Equity \(=\) market value \(-\) outstanding balance.
  • Total cost \(=\) down payment \(+\) sum of payments \(+\) closing costs \(+\) (maintenance, taxes, insurance).

The break-even on paying points or on refinancing is an NPV calculation. The effective cost of borrowing should be compared after tax where mortgage interest is deductible.

5 Credit Cards and Debt

Credit cards charge compound interest at high APR, often compounded daily, with a minimum payment that is a small percentage of the balance. This combination creates the debt trap:

  • A minimum payment of, say, 2% (or $25) mostly covers interest, so the balance shrinks glacially.
  • Because interest compounds daily, the effective rate is higher than the quoted APR suggests.

Debt-payoff strategies:

  • Avalanche: pay highest-APR debt first — minimizes total interest (mathematically optimal).
  • Snowball: pay smallest balance first — maximizes psychological wins.

Both are valid; avalanche is cheaper, snowball is often more sustainable. The mathematics of prepayment is a race between the compounding of the debt and the opportunity cost of the money.

6 Cars: Loans, Leases, and Depreciation

  • Auto loan: amortizing loan, finance charges like any other.
  • Lease: you pay for depreciation plus a money factor (rent charge), not the car. The residual value is crucial: lease payments are roughly \(\frac{(\text{price}-\text{residual})+\text{finance}}{\text{term}}\). Leases hide total cost and often penalize mileage.
  • Depreciation: a new car typically loses 15–25% in the first year and ~60% by five years; this is usually the largest cost of ownership, larger than fuel or maintenance.

Total cost of ownership = depreciation + interest + fuel + insurance + maintenance + taxes − residual value. Comparing a lease versus a buy is an NPV problem using the car’s residual value.

7 Insurance and Expected Value

Insurance is the transfer of risk. Its mathematics is expected value and risk pooling.

  • Premium \(p\), deductible \(d\), coverage — the insurer pays the loss above the deductible.
  • Expected loss to the insurer \(= \Pr(\text{loss})\times\text{typical severity}\).
  • A fair premium equals expected payout plus administrative costs and profit. Buying insurance has negative expected value in isolation — you pay more than the average loss — yet is rational because the utility of avoiding ruin is not linear.

Risk pooling (law of large numbers): if \(n\) independent identical risks each with variance \(\sigma^2\) are pooled, the average loss has variance \(\sigma^2/n\), which vanishes as \(n\to\infty\). This is why insurers can price risk reliably while individuals cannot — a direct application of the law of large numbers.

Concepts: moral hazard (insured behavior changes), adverse selection (high-risk buyers), term vs whole life insurance, and copays/coinsurance in health insurance.

8 Inflation, Taxes, and Planning

  • Inflation erodes purchasing power. The real return is \((1+\text{nominal})/(1+\text{inflation})-1\approx\text{nominal}-\text{inflation}\). A 5% nominal return with 3% inflation is only ~2% real.
  • Rule of 72 applies to inflation too: at 3%, prices double in ~24 years.
  • Taxes: marginal rate applies to the last dollar; effective rate is total tax divided by total income. Tax-advantaged accounts (401(k), IRA, TFSA, RRSP) defer or eliminate taxes on growth.
  • Emergency fund: 3–6 months of expenses in liquid form.
  • Diversification: correlation lowers portfolio variance; the mathematics is quadratic forms and covariance.

9.2 Mortgage Amortization

Code 
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def amortize(P, annual_rate, years, extra=0.0):
    i = annual_rate / 12
    n = years * 12
    pmt = P * i / (1 - (1 + i) ** -n) + extra
    bal = P
    interest_paid, principal_paid, balances = [], [], []
    for _ in range(n):
        interest = bal * i
        principal = min(pmt - interest, bal)
        bal -= principal
        interest_paid.append(interest)
        principal_paid.append(principal)
        balances.append(max(bal, 0))
        if bal <= 0:
            break
    return pmt, np.array(interest_paid), np.array(principal_paid), np.array(balances)

pmt, interest, principal, balances = amortize(300_000, 0.065, 30)
pmt_extra, interest_e, principal_e, balances_e = amortize(300_000, 0.065, 30, extra=200)

print("=== MORTGAGE: $300,000, 6.5%, 30 years ===")
print(f"  monthly payment       = ${pmt:,.2f}")
print(f"  total paid            = ${pmt*len(interest):,.2f}")
print(f"  total interest        = ${interest.sum():,.2f}")
print(f"  with extra $200/month = ${pmt_extra:,.2f}, paid off in {len(interest_e)} months "
      f"(vs {len(interest)})")
print(f"  interest saved         = ${interest.sum()-interest_e.sum():,.2f}")

# compare 15 vs 30 years

9.3 The Credit-Card Minimum-Payment Trap

Code 
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def payoff(balance, apr, min_rate=0.02, min_floor=25.0, fixed=None, cap=3000):
    i = apr / 12
    months, total_interest = 0, 0.0
    bal = balance
    path = [bal]
    for _ in range(cap):
        if bal <= 0:
            break
        interest = bal * i
        pay = fixed if fixed is not None else max(min_floor, min_rate * bal)
        pay = min(pay, bal + interest)
        bal = bal + interest - pay
        total_interest += interest
        months += 1
        path.append(max(bal, 0))
    return months, total_interest, np.array(path)

m_min, int_min, path_min = payoff(5000, 0.22)
m_fix, int_fix, path_fix = payoff(5000, 0.22, fixed=250)
print("=== CREDIT CARD: $5,000 AT 22% APR ===")
print(f"  minimum payments : {m_min} months, interest ${int_min:,.2f}")
print(f"  fixed $250/month : {m_fix} months, interest ${int_fix:,.2f}")
print(f"  savings from paying a fixed amount: ${int_min-int_fix:,.2f}")

plt.figure(figsize=(9.5, 5))
plt.plot(np.arange(len(path_min)) / 12, path_min, color="#e74c3c", label=f"minimum ({m_min} mo)")
plt.plot(np.arange(len(path_fix)) / 12, path_fix, color="#27ae60", label=f"$250/mo ({m_fix} mo)")
plt.title("Credit-card balance over time")
plt.xlabel("years"); plt.ylabel("balance ($)"); plt.legend(); plt.grid(alpha=0.3)
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

import numpy as np

print("=== AUTO: BUY (5-yr loan) vs LEASE (3-yr) ===")
price, resid3 = 35_000, 21_000        # residual after 3 years
down = 3_000
loan_rate, lease_rate = 0.07, 0.06

# buy: finance price-down over 5 years
i = loan_rate / 12
P = price - down
pmt_buy = P * i / (1 - (1 + i) ** -60)
bal_after_3 = sum(pmt_buy / (1 + i) ** k for k in range(1, 25))  # remaining balance after 36 months
equity3 = resid3 - bal_after_3
# lease: pay depreciation + finance (money factor)
dep = (price - resid3) / 36
money_factor = lease_rate / 24
lease_pmt = dep + (price + resid3) * money_factor
print(f"  buy   : payment ${pmt_buy:,.2f}/mo x 60; after 3 yrs you own a car worth ~${resid3:,} "
      f"with balance ~${bal_after_3:,.0f} -> equity ~${equity3:,.0f}")
print(f"  lease : payment ${lease_pmt:,.2f}/mo x 36; after 3 yrs you own nothing")
print(f"  the lease is essentially paying ${dep*36:,.0f} of depreciation + ${(lease_pmt-dep)*36:,.0f} finance")
print("  verdict: leasing is convenient and lower-commitment; buying builds equity.")

print("\n=== INSURANCE: EXPECTED VALUE AND RISK POOLING ===")
p_loss, loss = 0.01, 50_000
fair = p_loss * loss
print(f"  P(loss)={p_loss:.0%}, loss if it happens=${loss:,}")
print(f"  fair premium (expected payout) = ${fair:,.2f}")
print(f"  an insurer charging $700 keeps ${700-fair:,.2f} expected margin per policy")

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
for n in [1, 10, 1000, 100_000]:
    claims = rng.random(n) < p_loss
    payouts = np.where(claims, loss, 0)
    print(f"  pool of {n:>6}: mean loss/person = ${payouts.mean():8,.2f}, "
          f"std = ${payouts.std():9,.2f}")
print("  as the pool grows, the average loss per person converges to $500 (LLN),")
print("  so the insurer can price risk precisely while any individual cannot.")

## Percentages and Interest

Everything begins with percentages. A **percentage change** from $a$ to $b$ is $(b-a)/a$. **Percentage points** (an APR rising from 4% to 5%) are not the same as percent (a 25% increase).

### Simple vs Compound Interest

**Simple interest** grows linearly:
$$
A=P(1+rt).
$$

**Compound interest**, with $m$ compounding periods per year at nominal rate $r$:
$$
A=P\left(1+\frac{r}{m}\right)^{mt}.
$$

Compounding is the most important idea in finance: interest earns interest. Over long horizons the difference is enormous. As $m\to\infty$, compounding becomes **continuous**:
$$
A=Pe^{rt},
$$
connecting finance directly to [the number $e$](the-great-constants-golden-ratio-e-pi-tau-and-the-transcendental-fabric.qmd).

### APR vs APY

- **APR (nominal annual rate):** the quoted rate, ignoring compounding within the year.
- **APY / EAR (effective annual rate):** what you actually pay/earn:
$$
\text{APY}=\left(1+\frac{r}{m}\right)^{m}-1 \quad\xrightarrow{m\to\infty}\quad e^{r}-1.
$$

APR is a marketing number; APY is the truth. A 20% APR compounded daily is an APY of $e^{0.20}-1\approx22.1\%$.

### The Rule of 72

To estimate doubling time at rate $r$ (as a percent), $t\approx72/r$ years — a linearization of $2=(1+r)^t$. It is remarkably accurate for rates near 8%.

## Time Value of Money

A dollar today is worth more than a dollar tomorrow because it can be invested. **Present value** discounts a future cash flow:
$$
PV=\frac{FV}{(1+r)^t},\qquad FV=PV(1+r)^t.
$$

**Net present value (NPV)** of a stream of cash flows $C_t$ is
$$
NPV=\sum_{t=0}^{T}\frac{C_t}{(1+r)^t},
$$
and the **internal rate of return (IRR)** is the $r$ making $NPV=0$. These are the tools of capital budgeting and also of comparing a mortgage, a lease, or an investment.

### Annuities

An **annuity** is a series of equal payments.

- **Future value** of an ordinary annuity (payment $PMT$, $n$ periods, rate $i$ per period):
$$
FV=PMT\,\frac{(1+i)^n-1}{i}.
$$
- **Present value:** $PV=PMT\,\dfrac{1-(1+i)^{-n}}{i}$.

A retirement account is a future-value annuity problem; a mortgage or pension is a present-value annuity problem. Note the geometric series $(1+i)^n-1)/i$ — the same mathematics as [series](sequences-series-and-convergence-tests.qmd).

---

## Loans and Amortization

For a loan of principal $P$ at periodic rate $i$ over $n$ periods, the **level payment** is derived by equating the present value of payments to the principal:
$$
PMT=\frac{P\,i}{1-(1+i)^{-n}}.
$$

Each payment splits into **interest** ($i\times$ current balance) and **principal** (the rest). The **amortization schedule** tracks this. Early payments are mostly interest; late payments are mostly principal.

Key facts:

- **Longer term $\Rightarrow$ lower payment but more total interest.** A 30-year mortgage has smaller payments than a 15-year, but can cost roughly twice the interest overall.
- **Extra principal payments** attack the balance directly and shorten the term dramatically because they remove future interest.

---

## Mortgages

A **mortgage** is a secured amortizing loan.

- **Down payment:** the fraction paid upfront; below 20% typically triggers **PMI** (private mortgage insurance).
- **Points:** prepaid interest (1 point $=1\%$ of the loan) that buys a lower rate.
- **Fixed vs adjustable (ARM):** fixed locks the rate; an ARM resets, transferring rate risk to the borrower.
- **Closing costs:** origination, title, appraisal, and recording fees.
- **Equity** $=$ market value $-$ outstanding balance.
- **Total cost** $=$ down payment $+$ sum of payments $+$ closing costs $+$ (maintenance, taxes, insurance).

The break-even on paying points or on refinancing is an NPV calculation. The **effective cost of borrowing** should be compared **after tax** where mortgage interest is deductible.

---

## Credit Cards and Debt

Credit cards charge **compound interest at high APR**, often compounded daily, with a **minimum payment** that is a small percentage of the balance. This combination creates the debt trap:

- A minimum payment of, say, 2% (or \$25) mostly covers interest, so the balance shrinks glacially.
- Because interest compounds daily, the effective rate is higher than the quoted APR suggests.

| Variable | Typical value | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| APR | 18–29% | high compounding cost |
| Minimum payment | 1–3% of balance | near-perpetual debt |
| Grace period | ~21–25 days | interest-free if paid in full |
| Credit utilization | <30% recommended | affects credit score |

**Debt-payoff strategies:**

- **Avalanche:** pay highest-APR debt first — minimizes total interest (mathematically optimal).
- **Snowball:** pay smallest balance first — maximizes psychological wins.

Both are valid; avalanche is cheaper, snowball is often more sustainable. The mathematics of prepayment is a race between the compounding of the debt and the opportunity cost of the money.

---

## Cars: Loans, Leases, and Depreciation

- **Auto loan:** amortizing loan, finance charges like any other.
- **Lease:** you pay for **depreciation plus a money factor (rent charge)**, not the car. The **residual value** is crucial: lease payments are roughly $\frac{(\text{price}-\text{residual})+\text{finance}}{\text{term}}$. Leases hide total cost and often penalize mileage.
- **Depreciation:** a new car typically loses 15–25% in the first year and ~60% by five years; this is usually the largest cost of ownership, larger than fuel or maintenance.

**Total cost of ownership** = depreciation + interest + fuel + insurance + maintenance + taxes − residual value. Comparing a lease versus a buy is an NPV problem using the car's residual value.

---

## Insurance and Expected Value

Insurance is the transfer of risk. Its mathematics is **expected value** and **risk pooling**.

- **Premium** $p$, **deductible** $d$, **coverage** — the insurer pays the loss above the deductible.
- **Expected loss** to the insurer $= \Pr(\text{loss})\times\text{typical severity}$.
- A **fair premium** equals expected payout plus administrative costs and profit. Buying insurance has negative expected value in isolation — you pay more than the average loss — yet is rational because the **utility** of avoiding ruin is not linear.

**Risk pooling (law of large numbers):** if $n$ independent identical risks each with variance $\sigma^2$ are pooled, the average loss has variance $\sigma^2/n$, which vanishes as $n\to\infty$. This is why insurers can price risk reliably while individuals cannot — a direct application of the [law of large numbers](probability-theory-and-mathematical-probability.qmd).

Concepts: **moral hazard** (insured behavior changes), **adverse selection** (high-risk buyers), **term vs whole life** insurance, and **copays/coinsurance** in health insurance.

---

## Inflation, Taxes, and Planning

- **Inflation** erodes purchasing power. The **real return** is $(1+\text{nominal})/(1+\text{inflation})-1\approx\text{nominal}-\text{inflation}$. A 5% nominal return with 3% inflation is only ~2% real.
- **Rule of 72** applies to inflation too: at 3%, prices double in ~24 years.
- **Taxes:** **marginal** rate applies to the last dollar; **effective** rate is total tax divided by total income. Tax-advantaged accounts (401(k), IRA, TFSA, RRSP) defer or eliminate taxes on growth.
- **Emergency fund:** 3–6 months of expenses in liquid form.
- **Diversification:** correlation lowers portfolio variance; the mathematics is quadratic forms and covariance.

### Compound Growth and the Rule of 72

```{python}
import numpy as np

print("=== COMPOUNDING: $10,000 AT 7% ===")
P, r = 10_000, 0.07
print(f"{'years':>6} {'annual':>12} {'monthly':>12} {'daily':>12} {'continuous':>12}")
for t in [1, 5, 10, 20, 30]:
    annual = P * (1 + r) ** t
    monthly = P * (1 + r / 12) ** (12 * t)
    daily = P * (1 + r / 365) ** (365 * t)
    cont = P * np.exp(r * t)
    print(f"{t:>6} {annual:12,.0f} {monthly:12,.0f} {daily:12,.0f} {cont:12,.0f}")

print("\n=== APR vs APY ===")
for apr, m in [(0.20, 365), (0.06, 12), (0.05, 1), (0.05, 4)]:
    apy = (1 + apr / m) ** m - 1
    print(f"  APR {apr:.2%} compounded {m:>3}x -> APY {apy:.4%}")

print("\n=== RULE OF 72 ===")
for r_pct in [3, 5, 7, 10]:
    exact = np.log(2) / np.log(1 + r_pct / 100)
    print(f"  {r_pct:>2}%: rule-of-72 -> {72/r_pct:5.1f} yrs, exact -> {exact:5.1f} yrs")
```

### Mortgage Amortization

```{python}
#| label: fig-mortgage
#| fig-cap: "A 30-year fixed mortgage of $300,000 at 6.5%. Left: the monthly payment splits into interest (which dominates early) and principal (which dominates late); the crossover is around year 20. Right: how the balance falls, and how much faster it falls if an extra $200/month is applied. The total interest saved by the extra payment is often tens of thousands of dollars."

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def amortize(P, annual_rate, years, extra=0.0):
    i = annual_rate / 12
    n = years * 12
    pmt = P * i / (1 - (1 + i) ** -n) + extra
    bal = P
    interest_paid, principal_paid, balances = [], [], []
    for _ in range(n):
        interest = bal * i
        principal = min(pmt - interest, bal)
        bal -= principal
        interest_paid.append(interest)
        principal_paid.append(principal)
        balances.append(max(bal, 0))
        if bal <= 0:
            break
    return pmt, np.array(interest_paid), np.array(principal_paid), np.array(balances)

pmt, interest, principal, balances = amortize(300_000, 0.065, 30)
pmt_extra, interest_e, principal_e, balances_e = amortize(300_000, 0.065, 30, extra=200)

print("=== MORTGAGE: $300,000, 6.5%, 30 years ===")
print(f"  monthly payment       = ${pmt:,.2f}")
print(f"  total paid            = ${pmt*len(interest):,.2f}")
print(f"  total interest        = ${interest.sum():,.2f}")
print(f"  with extra $200/month = ${pmt_extra:,.2f}, paid off in {len(interest_e)} months "
      f"(vs {len(interest)})")
print(f"  interest saved         = ${interest.sum()-interest_e.sum():,.2f}")

# compare 15 vs 30 years
pmt15, int15, _, _ = amortize(300_000, 0.065, 15)
print(f"\n  15-year: payment ${pmt15:,.2f}, total interest ${int15.sum():,.2f}")
print(f"  30-year: payment ${pmt:,.2f}, total interest ${interest.sum():,.2f}")
print(f"  shorter term costs ${pmt15-pmt:,.2f}/month more but saves ${interest.sum()-int15.sum():,.2f}")

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(13.5, 5))
years = np.arange(len(interest)) / 12
axes[0].stackplot(years, principal, interest, labels=["principal", "interest"],
                  colors=["#2980b9", "#e74c3c"], alpha=0.85)
axes[0].set_title("Monthly payment split (30-year, baseline)")
axes[0].set_xlabel("year"); axes[0].set_ylabel("$ per month"); axes[0].legend(); axes[0].grid(alpha=0.3)
axes[1].plot(np.arange(len(balances)) / 12, balances, color="#2980b9", label="baseline")
axes[1].plot(np.arange(len(balances_e)) / 12, balances_e, color="#27ae60", label="+$200/month")
axes[1].set_title("Remaining balance")
axes[1].set_xlabel("year"); axes[1].set_ylabel("balance ($)"); axes[1].legend(); axes[1].grid(alpha=0.3)
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()
```

### The Credit-Card Minimum-Payment Trap

```{python}
#| label: fig-creditcard
#| fig-cap: "Credit-card payoff. A $5,000 balance at 22% APR paid with the minimum (2% of balance, floor $25) takes many years and costs thousands in interest, whereas a fixed $250/month clears it in under two years. The curves show why minimum payments are designed to keep you in debt: most of each payment is interest, so the principal shrinks extremely slowly."

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def payoff(balance, apr, min_rate=0.02, min_floor=25.0, fixed=None, cap=3000):
    i = apr / 12
    months, total_interest = 0, 0.0
    bal = balance
    path = [bal]
    for _ in range(cap):
        if bal <= 0:
            break
        interest = bal * i
        pay = fixed if fixed is not None else max(min_floor, min_rate * bal)
        pay = min(pay, bal + interest)
        bal = bal + interest - pay
        total_interest += interest
        months += 1
        path.append(max(bal, 0))
    return months, total_interest, np.array(path)

m_min, int_min, path_min = payoff(5000, 0.22)
m_fix, int_fix, path_fix = payoff(5000, 0.22, fixed=250)
print("=== CREDIT CARD: $5,000 AT 22% APR ===")
print(f"  minimum payments : {m_min} months, interest ${int_min:,.2f}")
print(f"  fixed $250/month : {m_fix} months, interest ${int_fix:,.2f}")
print(f"  savings from paying a fixed amount: ${int_min-int_fix:,.2f}")

plt.figure(figsize=(9.5, 5))
plt.plot(np.arange(len(path_min)) / 12, path_min, color="#e74c3c", label=f"minimum ({m_min} mo)")
plt.plot(np.arange(len(path_fix)) / 12, path_fix, color="#27ae60", label=f"$250/mo ({m_fix} mo)")
plt.title("Credit-card balance over time")
plt.xlabel("years"); plt.ylabel("balance ($)"); plt.legend(); plt.grid(alpha=0.3)
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()
```

### Loans vs Leases and Risk Pooling

```{python}
import numpy as np

print("=== AUTO: BUY (5-yr loan) vs LEASE (3-yr) ===")
price, resid3 = 35_000, 21_000        # residual after 3 years
down = 3_000
loan_rate, lease_rate = 0.07, 0.06

# buy: finance price-down over 5 years
i = loan_rate / 12
P = price - down
pmt_buy = P * i / (1 - (1 + i) ** -60)
bal_after_3 = sum(pmt_buy / (1 + i) ** k for k in range(1, 25))  # remaining balance after 36 months
equity3 = resid3 - bal_after_3
# lease: pay depreciation + finance (money factor)
dep = (price - resid3) / 36
money_factor = lease_rate / 24
lease_pmt = dep + (price + resid3) * money_factor
print(f"  buy   : payment ${pmt_buy:,.2f}/mo x 60; after 3 yrs you own a car worth ~${resid3:,} "
      f"with balance ~${bal_after_3:,.0f} -> equity ~${equity3:,.0f}")
print(f"  lease : payment ${lease_pmt:,.2f}/mo x 36; after 3 yrs you own nothing")
print(f"  the lease is essentially paying ${dep*36:,.0f} of depreciation + ${(lease_pmt-dep)*36:,.0f} finance")
print("  verdict: leasing is convenient and lower-commitment; buying builds equity.")

print("\n=== INSURANCE: EXPECTED VALUE AND RISK POOLING ===")
p_loss, loss = 0.01, 50_000
fair = p_loss * loss
print(f"  P(loss)={p_loss:.0%}, loss if it happens=${loss:,}")
print(f"  fair premium (expected payout) = ${fair:,.2f}")
print(f"  an insurer charging $700 keeps ${700-fair:,.2f} expected margin per policy")

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
for n in [1, 10, 1000, 100_000]:
    claims = rng.random(n) < p_loss
    payouts = np.where(claims, loss, 0)
    print(f"  pool of {n:>6}: mean loss/person = ${payouts.mean():8,.2f}, "
          f"std = ${payouts.std():9,.2f}")
print("  as the pool grows, the average loss per person converges to $500 (LLN),")
print("  so the insurer can price risk precisely while any individual cannot.")
```

## Summary

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\textbf{Interest: } && A=P(1+r/m)^{mt};\ \text{continuous } A=Pe^{rt};\ \text{APY}=(1+r/m)^m-1;\ \text{rule of 72.} \\[3pt]
&\textbf{Time value: } && PV=FV/(1+r)^t;\ NPV=\sum C_t/(1+r)^t;\ \text{annuities.} \\[3pt]
&\textbf{Loans: } && PMT=\frac{Pi}{1-(1+i)^{-n}};\ \text{amortization: interest early, principal late.} \\[3pt]
&\textbf{Mortgage: } && \text{term, down payment, points, equity; extra principal saves large interest.} \\[3pt]
&\textbf{Credit cards: } && \text{high compounding APR + minimum payments = debt trap; avalanche vs snowball.} \\[3pt]
&\textbf{Cars: } && \text{loans build equity; leases pay depreciation; depreciation dominates TCO.} \\[3pt]
&\textbf{Insurance: } && \text{expected value + risk pooling (variance } \to0 \text{ as } n\to\infty). \\[3pt]
&\textbf{Planning: } && \text{real return} \approx \text{nominal}-\text{inflation};\ \text{marginal vs effective tax; emergency fund; diversification.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Financial literacy is not a body of tricks but a handful of formulas — compounding, discounting, amortization, and expected value — applied consistently across a lifetime.
