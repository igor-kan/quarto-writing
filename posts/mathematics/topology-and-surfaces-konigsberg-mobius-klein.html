In 1736 Euler solved the Seven Bridges of Königsberg not by measuring distances but by ignoring them: only the connections mattered. That decision founded graph theory and, ultimately, topology — the geometry of shape up to continuous deformation, where a coffee cup and a donut are the same object.

This article follows that thread from a puzzle about bridges to one-sided surfaces and the classification of all closed surfaces — with algorithms you can run.

1 The Seven Bridges of Königsberg

The city of Königsberg (now Kaliningrad) sat on both banks of the Pregel River and two islands, connected by seven bridges. The puzzle: walk through the city crossing each bridge exactly once and return to the start.

Euler’s insight (1736): replace landmasses by vertices and bridges by edges — the first graph. The question becomes whether the graph has a closed trail using every edge exactly once (an Eulerian circuit).

Euler’s theorem. A connected graph has an Eulerian circuit iff every vertex has even degree. It has an Eulerian path (open trail) iff exactly zero or two vertices have odd degree (the odd-degree vertices are the endpoints).

Königsberg had four vertices of odd degree (3, 3, 3, 5), so no such walk exists. The distances and shapes were irrelevant; only the parity of degrees mattered. This is the birth of both graph theory and topological thinking: an invariant (degree parity) decides a question that geometry alone could not.

1.1 Degrees, Connectivity, and Euler’s Formula for Planar Graphs

  • Degree of a vertex: number of incident edges (loops count twice).
  • Connected: any two vertices joined by a path.
  • Handshake lemma: the sum of degrees is twice the number of edges, so the number of odd-degree vertices is even.
  • Planar graph: drawable with no edge crossings; Euler’s formula for a connected planar graph with \(V\) vertices, \(E\) edges, and \(F\) faces (including the outer face): \[ V-E+F=2. \] This is the same Euler characteristic that governs polyhedra — and it generalizes to surfaces.

2 Readily Applicable: Eulerian Algorithms

The parity criterion turns into fast algorithms with immediate uses:

  • Hierholzer’s algorithm builds an Eulerian circuit in \(O(E)\) time by following unused edges until stuck and splicing cycles.
  • Route inspection (the Chinese Postman problem): a postal worker or snowplow must traverse every street; if the graph has odd-degree vertices, the optimal route pairs them with shortest paths and reuses those edges. Euler’s parity theorem is the starting point.
  • Genome assembly: reads are \(k\)-mers; build a de Bruijn graph whose edges are \((k{+}1)\)-mers; a genome corresponds to an Eulerian path through the graph. This is the algorithm behind many real assemblers (Euler, Velvet, SPAdes).
  • Circuit design and robotics: traversing every wire or every configuration edge exactly once.

The laboratory implements Hierholzer and a working mini-assembler.

3 Topology: Shape Without Distance

Topology studies properties preserved under homeomorphism: a continuous bijection with continuous inverse (“continuous deformations,” no cutting or gluing). A coffee cup and a donut are homeomorphic (both have one hole).

Invariants distinguish spaces:

  • Connectivity (connected components).
  • Compactness and closedness.
  • Genus (number of holes/handles).
  • Orientability (two-sided vs one-sided).
  • Euler characteristic \(\chi\).
  • Fundamental group \(\pi_1\) and homology (algebraic invariants).

A donut (torus) and a sphere differ in genus and Euler characteristic; no amount of bending changes that.

4 Surfaces and Their Classification

A surface is a space locally like the plane. Closed surfaces are compact and without boundary. The classification theorem says every closed surface is determined by two invariants:

  1. Orientability (two-sided or one-sided).
  2. Genus \(g\) (number of handles).

For a connected closed orientable surface of genus \(g\), the Euler characteristic is \[ \chi = V-E+F = 2-2g. \]

  • Sphere: \(g=0\), \(\chi=2\).
  • Torus: \(g=1\), \(\chi=0\).
  • Double torus: \(g=2\), \(\chi=-2\), and so on.

Non-orientable closed surfaces are built from projective planes or one Klein bottle; a sphere with \(k\) Möbius bands has \(\chi=2-k\).

4.1 The Möbius Strip

Take a strip of paper, give it a half twist, and glue the ends. The result is the Möbius strip:

  • It has one side and one edge (a single closed curve).
  • It is non-orientable: there is no consistent choice of “up.”
  • Cutting it down the middle does not separate it — it produces one longer, thinner two-sided band.

Applications: conveyor belts and drive belts that wear evenly (each “side” becomes both sides), Möbius resistor designs, continuous-loop tapes, and mathematical art (Escher’s Möbius Strip II). Bach’s Musical Offering crab canon has a Möbius-like structure.

4.2 The Klein Bottle

Glue two Möbius strips along their boundary and you get the Klein bottle, a closed non-orientable surface with \(\chi=0\). It has:

  • No inside or outside (non-orientable).
  • No boundary (closed).
  • Cannot be embedded in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) without self-intersection; an immersion with a passage through itself is the usual glass-model picture. It embeds in \(\mathbb{R}^4\).

The projective plane (\(\chi=1\)) is the other basic non-orientable surface; the Klein bottle is the connected sum of two projective planes.

4.3 Connected Sums

Gluing two surfaces by removing a disk from each and joining the boundaries yields the connected sum. The sphere is the identity; the torus is the connected sum of two; the Klein bottle is the connected sum of two projective planes. This operation generates the whole classification table.

5 Applications of Topology

Topology is not merely abstract; its invariants and ideas are used widely:

  • Topological data analysis (TDA): persistent homology tracks holes across scales in point-cloud data, used in biology, materials, and finance.
  • Robotics and motion planning: configuration spaces of a robot are manifolds or graphs; connectivity and homotopy decide reachability.
  • Computer graphics and 3D printing: genus and Euler characteristic detect and repair mesh defects (holes, non-manifold edges); a watertight mesh has a well-defined genus.
  • Knot theory: DNA topology and polymer physics; the trefoil is a chiral knot; knot invariants (Jones polynomial) classify entanglement.
  • Network routing: graph connectivity, planarity, and Eulerian/Hamiltonian traversal drive GPS routing, internet topology, and circuit layout.
  • Materials and physics: topological insulators, anyons, and topological quantum computing rely on global invariants robust to local perturbation.
  • Differential geometry’s global half: Gauss–Bonnet links curvature to \(\chi\) — local geometry determines global topology.

The recurring lesson: the invariants that do not change under deformation are the ones that carry robust information.

6.1 Hierholzer’s Eulerian Circuit

Code 
from collections import defaultdict

def eulerian_circuit(edges):
    graph = defaultdict(list)
    degree = defaultdict(int)
    for u, v in edges:
        graph[u].append(v); graph[v].append(u)
        degree[u] += 1; degree[v] += 1
    odd = [v for v, d in degree.items() if d % 2]
    if odd:
        return None, odd
    # Hierholzer
    stack, circuit = [next(iter(graph))], []
    local = {u: list(vs) for u, vs in graph.items()}
    while stack:
        u = stack[-1]
        if local.get(u):
            v = local[u].pop(); local[v].remove(u)
            stack.append(v)
        else:
            circuit.append(stack.pop())
    return circuit[::-1], []

konigsberg = [("A","B"),("A","C"),("A","D"),("B","D"),("B","D"),("C","D"),("C","D")]
eulerian = [("A","B"),("B","C"),("C","A"),("C","D"),("D","E"),("E","C")]

print("=== KÖNIGSBERG (Seven Bridges) ===")
circ, odd = eulerian_circuit(konigsberg)
print(f"  Eulerian circuit exists? {circ is not None}; odd-degree vertices: {sorted(odd)}")
print("  -> no walk crosses every bridge exactly once (Euler, 1736).")

print("\n=== A SOLVABLE GRAPH ===")
circ, odd = eulerian_circuit(eulerian)
print(f"  circuit: {' -> '.join(circ)}")

print("\n=== ROUTE INSPECTION (Chinese Postman) ===")
def odd_vertices(edges):
    deg = defaultdict(int)
    for u, v in edges: deg[u] += 1; deg[v] += 1
    return [v for v, d in deg.items() if d % 2]
print(f"  odd-degree vertices needing pairing: {odd_vertices(konigsberg)}")
print("  the optimal route pairs them by shortest paths and reuses those edges.")

6.2 Genome Assembly via de Bruijn Graph

Code 
def assemble(reads, k):
    # de Bruijn: nodes are (k-1)-mers, edges are k-mers
    graph = defaultdict(list)
    indeg = defaultdict(int); outdeg = defaultdict(int)
    for r in reads:
        for i in range(len(r) - k + 1):
            kmer = r[i:i+k]
            a, b = kmer[:-1], kmer[1:]
            graph[a].append(b); indeg[b] += 1; outdeg[a] += 1
    nodes = set(list(graph) + list(indeg))
    starts = [n for n in nodes if outdeg[n] - indeg[n] == 1]
    start = starts[0] if starts else next(iter(nodes))
    # Hierholzer on directed graph
    stack, path = [start], []
    local = {u: list(vs) for u, vs in graph.items()}
    while stack:
        u = stack[-1]
        if local.get(u):
            stack.append(local[u].pop())
        else:
            path.append(stack.pop())
    path = path[::-1]
    return path[0] + "".join(p[-1] for p in path[1:])

reads = ["ATGGC", "TGGCA", "GGCAT", "GCATG"]
print("=== DE BRUIJN GENOME ASSEMBLY (k=5) ===")
print(f"  reads: {reads}")
print(f"  assembled contig: {assemble(reads, 5)}")
print("  a genome is an Eulerian path in the de Bruijn graph of its k-mers.")

6.3 Euler Characteristic and Genus of Meshes

Code 
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def torus_mesh(nu=24, nv=12):
    verts, faces = [], []
    for i in range(nu):
        for j in range(nv):
            u = 2*np.pi*i/nu; v = 2*np.pi*j/nv
            R, r = 2.0, 0.7
            verts.append([(R + r*np.cos(v))*np.cos(u), (R + r*np.cos(v))*np.sin(u), r*np.sin(v)])
    idx = lambda i, j: (i % nu)*nv + (j % nv)
    for i in range(nu):
        for j in range(nv):
            a, b, c, d = idx(i,j), idx(i+1,j), idx(i+1,j+1), idx(i,j+1)
            faces += [(a,b,c), (a,c,d)]
    return np.array(verts), faces

V, F = torus_mesh()
E = set()
for a, b, c in F:
    for x, y in [(a,b),(b,c),(c,a)]:
        E.add((min(x,y), max(x,y)))
chi = len(V) - len(E) + len(F)
g = (2 - chi) / 2
print("=== MESH EULER CHARACTERISTIC AND GENUS ===")
print(f"  torus mesh: V={len(V)}, E={len(E)}, F={len(F)} -> chi={chi}, genus={g:.1f}")
print("  sphere: chi=2, genus=0; each handle lowers chi by 2.")
print("  a watertight mesh obeys V - E + F = 2 - 2g; a defect breaks it.")

- **No inside or outside** (non-orientable).
- **No boundary** (closed).
- **Cannot be embedded in $\mathbb{R}^3$** without self-intersection; an immersion with a passage through itself is the usual glass-model picture. It embeds in $\mathbb{R}^4$.

The **projective plane** ($\chi=1$) is the other basic non-orientable surface; the Klein bottle is the connected sum of two projective planes.

### Connected Sums

Gluing two surfaces by removing a disk from each and joining the boundaries yields the **connected sum**. The sphere is the identity; the torus is the connected sum of two; the Klein bottle is the connected sum of two projective planes. This operation generates the whole classification table.

---

## Applications of Topology

Topology is not merely abstract; its invariants and ideas are used widely:

- **Topological data analysis (TDA):** **persistent homology** tracks holes across scales in point-cloud data, used in biology, materials, and finance.
- **Robotics and motion planning:** **configuration spaces** of a robot are manifolds or graphs; connectivity and homotopy decide reachability.
- **Computer graphics and 3D printing:** **genus** and Euler characteristic detect and repair mesh defects (holes, non-manifold edges); a watertight mesh has a well-defined genus.
- **Knot theory:** DNA topology and polymer physics; the trefoil is a chiral knot; knot invariants (Jones polynomial) classify entanglement.
- **Network routing:** graph connectivity, planarity, and Eulerian/Hamiltonian traversal drive GPS routing, internet topology, and circuit layout.
- **Materials and physics:** topological insulators, anyons, and topological quantum computing rely on global invariants robust to local perturbation.
- **Differential geometry's global half:** Gauss–Bonnet links curvature to $\chi$ — local geometry determines global topology.

**The recurring lesson:** the invariants that do not change under deformation are the ones that carry robust information.

### Hierholzer's Eulerian Circuit

```{python}
from collections import defaultdict

def eulerian_circuit(edges):
    graph = defaultdict(list)
    degree = defaultdict(int)
    for u, v in edges:
        graph[u].append(v); graph[v].append(u)
        degree[u] += 1; degree[v] += 1
    odd = [v for v, d in degree.items() if d % 2]
    if odd:
        return None, odd
    # Hierholzer
    stack, circuit = [next(iter(graph))], []
    local = {u: list(vs) for u, vs in graph.items()}
    while stack:
        u = stack[-1]
        if local.get(u):
            v = local[u].pop(); local[v].remove(u)
            stack.append(v)
        else:
            circuit.append(stack.pop())
    return circuit[::-1], []

konigsberg = [("A","B"),("A","C"),("A","D"),("B","D"),("B","D"),("C","D"),("C","D")]
eulerian = [("A","B"),("B","C"),("C","A"),("C","D"),("D","E"),("E","C")]

print("=== KÖNIGSBERG (Seven Bridges) ===")
circ, odd = eulerian_circuit(konigsberg)
print(f"  Eulerian circuit exists? {circ is not None}; odd-degree vertices: {sorted(odd)}")
print("  -> no walk crosses every bridge exactly once (Euler, 1736).")

print("\n=== A SOLVABLE GRAPH ===")
circ, odd = eulerian_circuit(eulerian)
print(f"  circuit: {' -> '.join(circ)}")

print("\n=== ROUTE INSPECTION (Chinese Postman) ===")
def odd_vertices(edges):
    deg = defaultdict(int)
    for u, v in edges: deg[u] += 1; deg[v] += 1
    return [v for v, d in deg.items() if d % 2]
print(f"  odd-degree vertices needing pairing: {odd_vertices(konigsberg)}")
print("  the optimal route pairs them by shortest paths and reuses those edges.")
```

### Genome Assembly via de Bruijn Graph

```{python}
def assemble(reads, k):
    # de Bruijn: nodes are (k-1)-mers, edges are k-mers
    graph = defaultdict(list)
    indeg = defaultdict(int); outdeg = defaultdict(int)
    for r in reads:
        for i in range(len(r) - k + 1):
            kmer = r[i:i+k]
            a, b = kmer[:-1], kmer[1:]
            graph[a].append(b); indeg[b] += 1; outdeg[a] += 1
    nodes = set(list(graph) + list(indeg))
    starts = [n for n in nodes if outdeg[n] - indeg[n] == 1]
    start = starts[0] if starts else next(iter(nodes))
    # Hierholzer on directed graph
    stack, path = [start], []
    local = {u: list(vs) for u, vs in graph.items()}
    while stack:
        u = stack[-1]
        if local.get(u):
            stack.append(local[u].pop())
        else:
            path.append(stack.pop())
    path = path[::-1]
    return path[0] + "".join(p[-1] for p in path[1:])

reads = ["ATGGC", "TGGCA", "GGCAT", "GCATG"]
print("=== DE BRUIJN GENOME ASSEMBLY (k=5) ===")
print(f"  reads: {reads}")
print(f"  assembled contig: {assemble(reads, 5)}")
print("  a genome is an Eulerian path in the de Bruijn graph of its k-mers.")
```

### Euler Characteristic and Genus of Meshes

```{python}
#| label: fig-surfaces
#| fig-cap: "Euler characteristic and genus of surfaces. Left: a sphere (genus 0, χ = 2) and a torus (genus 1, χ = 0) shown as the two ends of the classification. Right: the genus computed from a triangle mesh by χ = V − E + F and g = (2 − χ)/2, which is exactly how 3D modeling software detects holes and repairs meshes. A watertight torus mesh has χ = 0."

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def torus_mesh(nu=24, nv=12):
    verts, faces = [], []
    for i in range(nu):
        for j in range(nv):
            u = 2*np.pi*i/nu; v = 2*np.pi*j/nv
            R, r = 2.0, 0.7
            verts.append([(R + r*np.cos(v))*np.cos(u), (R + r*np.cos(v))*np.sin(u), r*np.sin(v)])
    idx = lambda i, j: (i % nu)*nv + (j % nv)
    for i in range(nu):
        for j in range(nv):
            a, b, c, d = idx(i,j), idx(i+1,j), idx(i+1,j+1), idx(i,j+1)
            faces += [(a,b,c), (a,c,d)]
    return np.array(verts), faces

V, F = torus_mesh()
E = set()
for a, b, c in F:
    for x, y in [(a,b),(b,c),(c,a)]:
        E.add((min(x,y), max(x,y)))
chi = len(V) - len(E) + len(F)
g = (2 - chi) / 2
print("=== MESH EULER CHARACTERISTIC AND GENUS ===")
print(f"  torus mesh: V={len(V)}, E={len(E)}, F={len(F)} -> chi={chi}, genus={g:.1f}")
print("  sphere: chi=2, genus=0; each handle lowers chi by 2.")
print("  a watertight mesh obeys V - E + F = 2 - 2g; a defect breaks it.")

fig = plt.figure(figsize=(12.5, 5))
ax1 = fig.add_subplot(1, 2, 1, projection="3d")
ax1.plot_trisurf(V[:,0], V[:,1], V[:,2], triangles=F, color="#2980b9", alpha=0.9, linewidth=0)
ax1.set_title(f"Torus mesh: chi={chi}, genus={g:.0f}"); ax1.set_axis_off()

ax2 = fig.add_subplot(1, 2, 2)
labels = ["sphere", "torus", "double torus", "Klein bottle"]
genus = [0, 1, 2, None]
chis = [2, 0, -2, 0]
colors = ["#27ae60", "#2980b9", "#8e44ad", "#e74c3c"]
ax2.bar(labels, chis, color=colors)
ax2.axhline(0, color="k", lw=0.8)
ax2.set_title(r"Euler characteristic $\chi = 2 - 2g$ (Klein bottle: non-orientable, $\chi=0$)")
ax2.set_ylabel(r"$\chi$"); ax2.grid(axis="y", alpha=0.3)
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()
```

## Summary

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\textbf{Königsberg: } && \text{graph model; Eulerian circuit iff all degrees even (path iff 0 or 2 odd).} \\[3pt]
&\textbf{Planar graphs: } && V-E+F=2;\ \text{handshake lemma; parity is the key invariant.} \\[3pt]
&\textbf{Applications: } && \text{route inspection, de Bruijn genome assembly, circuit design.} \\[3pt]
&\textbf{Topology: } && \text{homeomorphism; invariants: connectivity, genus, orientability, } \chi,\ \pi_1. \\[3pt]
&\textbf{Surfaces: } && \text{classified by orientability + genus;} \chi=2-2g. \\[3pt]
&\textbf{Möbius / Klein: } && \text{one-sided, non-orientable; Klein bottle is closed but not embeddable in } \mathbb{R}^3. \\[3pt]
&\textbf{Real uses: } && \text{TDA, robotics, mesh repair, knot theory, materials, routing.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Topology is the geometry that survives bending. Its sibling, **measure theory**, provides the other great abstraction of analysis — the rigorous theory of length, area, and integration — and is the subject of [the next article](measure-theory-and-lebesgue-measure.qmd).
