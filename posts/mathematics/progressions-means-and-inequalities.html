Three ideas organize an astonishing amount of elementary mathematics: progressions (sequences with a linear or multiplicative rule), means (averages that summarize a list), and inequalities (the bounds that constrain them). Together they underpin compound interest, signal processing, optimization, statistics, and the deepest results in additive number theory.

This article collects them, with formulas you can use and proofs you can follow.

1 Progressions

A progression is a sequence generated by a simple recursive rule.

1.1 Arithmetic Progression (AP)

$ a, a+d, a+2d, $, with common difference \(d\):

\[ a_n=a+(n-1)d,\qquad S_n=\frac{n}{2}\bigl(a_1+a_n\bigr)=na+\frac{n(n-1)}{2}d. \]

The arithmetic mean of \(a_1,\dots,a_n\) is \(\bar a=S_n/n\). An AP is linear in \(n\), so its second differences vanish.

Gauss’s trick proves the sum: pair the first and last terms, each pair summing to \(a_1+a_n\).

1.2 Geometric Progression (GP)

$ a, ar, ar^2, $, with common ratio \(r\):

\[ a_n=ar^{n-1},\qquad S_n=a\,\frac{1-r^n}{1-r}\ (r\ne1),\qquad S_\infty=\frac{a}{1-r}\ (|r|<1). \]

The geometric mean of positive \(a_1,\dots,a_n\) is \((a_1a_2\cdots a_n)^{1/n}\). A GP is exponential, so its ratios of consecutive terms are constant.

1.3 Harmonic Progression (HP)

A sequence whose reciprocals form an AP: \(1/a_n\) is arithmetic. Example: \(1,\tfrac12,\tfrac13,\dots\). There is no simple closed form for the sum of an HP (that is the harmonic series, which diverges). The harmonic mean is defined below.

1.4 Relationships and Hybrid Progressions

  • AP ↔︎ GP: exponential transforms AP to GP and vice versa. If \(a,b,c\) are in AP, then \(e^a,e^b,e^c\) are in GP.
  • Arithmetico-geometric (AGP): \(a_n=(a+(n-1)d)r^{n-1}\); sums use the identity \[ \sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(a+kd)r^k=\frac{a-(a+(n-1)d)r^n}{1-r}+\frac{dr(1-r^{n-1})}{(1-r)^2}\quad(r\ne1). \]
  • Difference sequences: repeated differences turning constant reveal a polynomial sequence; the \(k\)-th differences of a degree-\(k\) polynomial are constant.
  • Telescoping: if \(a_k=b_{k+1}-b_k\), then \(\sum_{k=1}^n a_k=b_{n+1}-b_1\). Example: \(\sum_{k=1}^n\frac{1}{k(k+1)}=1-\frac1{n+1}\).
  • Summation by parts (Abel): \(\sum a_k\Delta b_k=a_nb_{n+1}-a_1b_1-\sum b_{k+1}\Delta a_k\) — the discrete analogue of integration by parts and the tool behind the Abel and Dirichlet convergence tests.

1.5 Sums of Powers (Faulhaber)

\[ \sum_{k=1}^n k=\frac{n(n+1)}{2},\qquad \sum_{k=1}^n k^2=\frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{6},\qquad \sum_{k=1}^n k^3=\left(\frac{n(n+1)}{2}\right)^2. \]

In general \(P_p(n)=\sum_{k=1}^n k^p\) is a degree-\((p+1)\) polynomial in \(n\); its coefficients involve the Bernoulli numbers, exactly the constants that also govern the special sums \(\zeta(2k)\).

2 Means

Given positive numbers \(x_1,\dots,x_n\), the classical means are:

Mean Definition Interpretation
Arithmetic \(A\) \(\frac1n\sum x_i\) additive average
Geometric \(G\) \((\prod x_i)^{1/n}\) multiplicative average
Harmonic \(H\) \(\dfrac{n}{\sum 1/x_i}\) average of rates
Quadratic (RMS) \(\sqrt{\frac1n\sum x_i^2}\) energy/amplitude
Contraharmonic \(C\) \(\dfrac{\sum x_i^2}{\sum x_i}\) —
Logarithmic \(L\) \(\dfrac{a-b}{\ln a-\ln b}\) used in heat transfer
Power mean \(M_p\) \(\left(\frac1n\sum x_i^p\right)^{1/p}\) unifies all of the above

The power mean is the master object: \[ M_{-\infty}=\min x_i,\quad M_{-1}=H,\quad M_0=G,\quad M_1=A,\quad M_2=\mathrm{RMS},\quad M_{\infty}=\max x_i. \]

Key ordering (power-mean inequality): \(M_p\) is increasing in \(p\), hence \[ \min\le H\le G\le A\le \mathrm{RMS}\le\max, \] with equality throughout iff all \(x_i\) are equal.

Weighted means replace average by weighted average; the same inequalities hold. The AM–GM–HM relation is especially clean for two numbers: \(G^2=AH\) — the three means form a geometric progression.

Why it matters: averaging rates, ratios, and multiplicative quantities requires the geometric or harmonic mean, not the arithmetic mean. Averaging speeds, growth rates, and per-unit costs are the classic traps: the wrong mean gives the wrong answer.

3 Inequalities

3.1 AM–GM

For positive \(x_i\), \[ \frac{x_1+\cdots+x_n}{n}\ \ge\ (x_1\cdots x_n)^{1/n}, \] with equality iff all \(x_i\) equal. Proofs: Cauchy’s induction (backward/forward induction) or Jensen applied to \(-\ln x\) (concave). The two-variable case follows from \((\sqrt a-\sqrt b)^2\ge0\).

3.2 Cauchy–Schwarz

\[ \left(\sum_{i=1}^n a_i b_i\right)^2 \le \left(\sum a_i^2\right)\left(\sum b_i^2\right), \] with equality iff the vectors are linearly dependent. It is the \(p=q=2\) case of Hölder, and the triangle inequality for the Euclidean norm follows from it.

3.3 Triangle, Minkowski, and Hölder

  • Triangle: \(\lVert x+y\rVert\le\lVert x\rVert+\lVert y\rVert\).
  • Minkowski (triangle for \(L^p\)): \(\left(\sum|x_i+y_i|^p\right)^{1/p}\le\left(\sum|x_i|^p\right)^{1/p}+\left(\sum|y_i|^p\right)^{1/p}\).
  • Hölder: \(\sum|a_ib_i|\le\left(\sum|a_i|^p\right)^{1/p}\left(\sum|b_i|^q\right)^{1/q}\) for \(1/p+1/q=1\).

3.4 Jensen and Convexity

For a convex function \(\varphi\), \[ \varphi\!\left(\frac1n\sum x_i\right)\le \frac1n\sum \varphi(x_i), \] and the inequality reverses for concave \(\varphi\). Jensen is the general engine behind AM–GM, the power-mean ordering, and countless optimization bounds.

3.5 Chebyshev, Rearrangement, and Others

  • Chebyshev sum inequality: for similarly ordered sequences, \(\frac1n\sum a_ib_i\ge\left(\frac1n\sum a_i\right)\left(\frac1n\sum b_i\right)\).
  • Rearrangement: a sum of products is maximized by pairing the sequences in the same order and minimized by opposite order.
  • Bernoulli: \((1+x)^r\ge1+rx\) for \(x\ge-1\), \(r\ge1\) (or \(r\le0\)); the workhorse for growth and compounding bounds.
  • Young: \(ab\le\frac{a^p}{p}+\frac{b^q}{q}\).
  • Nesbitt: \(\frac{a}{b+c}+\frac{b}{c+a}+\frac{c}{a+b}\ge\frac32\) for positive \(a,b,c\).
  • Weitzenböck: \(a^2+b^2+c^2\ge4\sqrt3\,\Delta\) for a triangle of area \(\Delta\).
  • Von Neumann / trace inequalities, Ky Fan, and many more generalize these to operators and matrices in infinite-dimensional settings.

3.6 Green–Tao: Arithmetic Progressions of Primes

A spectacular 2004 result connects progressions and number theory:

Green–Tao theorem: the prime numbers contain arithmetically long progressions of every finite length — for every \(k\), there exist primes \(p_1,\dots,p_k\) forming an AP.

It does not say the primes contain an infinite AP (they do not; Dirichlet handles progressions with a fixed common difference). It sits atop Szemerédi’s theorem (any set of positive upper density contains arbitrarily long APs) and required a relative Szemerédi theorem for the sparse set of primes, using the Green–Tao–Ziegler structure theorem. See the primes article for the surrounding theory.

4 Laboratory: Verifying the Bounds

4.1 Means Ordering and Power Means

Code 
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(2)
x = rng.uniform(1, 10, size=8)

A = x.mean()
G = np.exp(np.log(x).mean())
H = len(x) / np.sum(1 / x)
RMS = np.sqrt(np.mean(x**2))
print("=== CLASSICAL MEANS OF A RANDOM SAMPLE ===")
print(f"  min={x.min():.4f}  HM={H:.4f}  GM={G:.4f}  AM={A:.4f}  "
      f"RMS={RMS:.4f}  max={x.max():.4f}")
print(f"  chain holds? {x.min() <= H <= G <= A <= RMS <= x.max()}")
print(f"  AM-GM check: AM-GM = {A-G:.4f} >= 0")

def power_mean(x, p):
    if abs(p) < 1e-9:
        return np.exp(np.log(x).mean())
    return np.mean(x**p) ** (1 / p)

ps = np.linspace(-6, 6, 400)
mp = [power_mean(x, p) for p in ps]

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(13, 4.7))
labels = ["min", "HM", "GM", "AM", "RMS", "max"]
vals = [x.min(), H, G, A, RMS, x.max()]
axes[0].bar(labels, vals, color=["#95a5a6", "#e74c3c", "#e67e22", "#27ae60", "#2980b9", "#95a5a6"])
for i, v in enumerate(vals):
    axes[0].text(i, v + 0.1, f"{v:.2f}", ha="center", fontsize=9)
axes[0].set_title("Means ordering"); axes[0].set_ylabel("value"); axes[0].grid(axis="y", alpha=0.3)

axes[1].plot(ps, mp, color="#2980b9", lw=2)
for p, name in [(-1, "HM"), (0, "GM"), (1, "AM"), (2, "RMS")]:
    axes[1].plot(p, power_mean(x, p), "o", color="#e74c3c")
    axes[1].annotate(name, (p, power_mean(x, p)), textcoords="offset points", xytext=(4, 4))
axes[1].axhline(x.min(), color="#95a5a6", ls=":"); axes[1].axhline(x.max(), color="#95a5a6", ls=":")
axes[1].set_title(r"Power mean $M_p$ increases with p")
axes[1].set_xlabel("p"); axes[1].set_ylabel(r"$M_p$"); axes[1].grid(alpha=0.3)
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

rhs = np.dot(a, a) * np.dot(b, b)

print("\n=== TRIANGLE INEQUALITY ===")
print(f"  |a+b| = {np.linalg.norm(a+b):.4f}  <=  |a|+|b| = {np.linalg.norm(a)+np.linalg.norm(b):.4f}")

print("\n=== CHEBYSHEV (similarly ordered) ===")
u = np.sort(rng.uniform(0, 1, 50)); v = np.sort(rng.uniform(0, 1, 50))
print(f"  mean(uv) = {np.mean(u*v):.4f}  >=  mean(u)mean(v) = {np.mean(u)*np.mean(v):.4f}")

4.4 Arithmetic Progressions of Primes (Small Green–Tao Examples)

Code 
import numpy as np

LIM = 100_000
sieve = np.ones(LIM + 1, bool); sieve[:2] = False
for i in range(2, int(LIM ** 0.5) + 1):
    if sieve[i]:
        sieve[i * i::i] = False
primes = set(np.nonzero(sieve)[0].tolist())

def find_ap(k, amax=5000, dmax=1000):
    for a in sorted(p for p in primes if p < amax):
        for d in range(2, dmax, 2):
            if all((a + i * d) in primes for i in range(k)):
                return [a + i * d for i in range(k)], d
    return None, None

print("=== ARITHMETIC PROGRESSIONS OF PRIMES (Green–Tao, small cases) ===")
for k in [3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8]:
    terms, d = find_ap(k)
    print(f"  length {k}: {terms}  (common difference {d})")
print("\n  the primes contain APs of every finite length, though the first")
print("  example grows extremely fast; length 8 already needs a large search.")

## Progressions

A **progression** is a sequence generated by a simple recursive rule.

### Arithmetic Progression (AP)

$ a,\ a+d,\ a+2d,\ \dots$, with common difference $d$:

$$
a_n=a+(n-1)d,\qquad
S_n=\frac{n}{2}\bigl(a_1+a_n\bigr)=na+\frac{n(n-1)}{2}d.
$$

The **arithmetic mean** of $a_1,\dots,a_n$ is $\bar a=S_n/n$. An AP is linear in $n$, so its **second differences vanish**.

**Gauss's trick** proves the sum: pair the first and last terms, each pair summing to $a_1+a_n$.

### Geometric Progression (GP)

$ a,\ ar,\ ar^2,\ \dots$, with common ratio $r$:

$$
a_n=ar^{n-1},\qquad
S_n=a\,\frac{1-r^n}{1-r}\ (r\ne1),\qquad
S_\infty=\frac{a}{1-r}\ (|r|<1).
$$

The **geometric mean** of positive $a_1,\dots,a_n$ is $(a_1a_2\cdots a_n)^{1/n}$. A GP is exponential, so its **ratios of consecutive terms are constant**.

### Harmonic Progression (HP)

A sequence whose **reciprocals form an AP**: $1/a_n$ is arithmetic. Example: $1,\tfrac12,\tfrac13,\dots$. There is no simple closed form for the sum of an HP (that is the harmonic series, which diverges). The **harmonic mean** is defined below.

### Relationships and Hybrid Progressions

- **AP ↔ GP:** exponential transforms AP to GP and vice versa. If $a,b,c$ are in AP, then $e^a,e^b,e^c$ are in GP.
- **Arithmetico-geometric (AGP):** $a_n=(a+(n-1)d)r^{n-1}$; sums use the identity
$$
\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(a+kd)r^k=\frac{a-(a+(n-1)d)r^n}{1-r}+\frac{dr(1-r^{n-1})}{(1-r)^2}\quad(r\ne1).
$$
- **Difference sequences:** repeated differences turning constant reveal a **polynomial sequence**; the $k$-th differences of a degree-$k$ polynomial are constant.
- **Telescoping:** if $a_k=b_{k+1}-b_k$, then $\sum_{k=1}^n a_k=b_{n+1}-b_1$. Example: $\sum_{k=1}^n\frac{1}{k(k+1)}=1-\frac1{n+1}$.
- **Summation by parts** (Abel): $\sum a_k\Delta b_k=a_nb_{n+1}-a_1b_1-\sum b_{k+1}\Delta a_k$ — the discrete analogue of integration by parts and the tool behind the [Abel and Dirichlet convergence tests](sequences-series-and-convergence-tests.qmd).

### Sums of Powers (Faulhaber)

$$
\sum_{k=1}^n k=\frac{n(n+1)}{2},\qquad
\sum_{k=1}^n k^2=\frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{6},\qquad
\sum_{k=1}^n k^3=\left(\frac{n(n+1)}{2}\right)^2.
$$

In general $P_p(n)=\sum_{k=1}^n k^p$ is a degree-$(p+1)$ polynomial in $n$; its coefficients involve the **Bernoulli numbers**, exactly the constants that also govern the [special sums $\zeta(2k)$](series-real-complex-analysis-special-sums-and-closed-forms.qmd).

## Means

Given positive numbers $x_1,\dots,x_n$, the classical means are:

| Mean | Definition | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Arithmetic $A$ | $\frac1n\sum x_i$ | additive average |
| Geometric $G$ | $(\prod x_i)^{1/n}$ | multiplicative average |
| Harmonic $H$ | $\dfrac{n}{\sum 1/x_i}$ | average of rates |
| Quadratic (RMS) | $\sqrt{\frac1n\sum x_i^2}$ | energy/amplitude |
| Contraharmonic $C$ | $\dfrac{\sum x_i^2}{\sum x_i}$ | — |
| Logarithmic $L$ | $\dfrac{a-b}{\ln a-\ln b}$ | used in heat transfer |
| Power mean $M_p$ | $\left(\frac1n\sum x_i^p\right)^{1/p}$ | unifies all of the above |

The **power mean** is the master object:
$$
M_{-\infty}=\min x_i,\quad M_{-1}=H,\quad M_0=G,\quad M_1=A,\quad M_2=\mathrm{RMS},\quad M_{\infty}=\max x_i.
$$

**Key ordering (power-mean inequality):** $M_p$ is **increasing in $p$**, hence
$$
\min\le H\le G\le A\le \mathrm{RMS}\le\max,
$$
with equality throughout iff all $x_i$ are equal.

**Weighted means** replace average by weighted average; the same inequalities hold. The **AM–GM–HM relation** is especially clean for two numbers: $G^2=AH$ — the three means form a **geometric progression**.

**Why it matters:** averaging rates, ratios, and multiplicative quantities requires the **geometric** or **harmonic** mean, not the arithmetic mean. Averaging speeds, growth rates, and per-unit costs are the classic traps: the wrong mean gives the wrong answer.

## Inequalities

### AM–GM

For positive $x_i$,
$$
\frac{x_1+\cdots+x_n}{n}\ \ge\ (x_1\cdots x_n)^{1/n},
$$
with equality iff all $x_i$ equal. Proofs: **Cauchy's induction** (backward/forward induction) or **Jensen** applied to $-\ln x$ (concave). The two-variable case follows from $(\sqrt a-\sqrt b)^2\ge0$.

### Cauchy–Schwarz

$$
\left(\sum_{i=1}^n a_i b_i\right)^2 \le \left(\sum a_i^2\right)\left(\sum b_i^2\right),
$$
with equality iff the vectors are linearly dependent. It is the $p=q=2$ case of Hölder, and the **triangle inequality** for the Euclidean norm follows from it.

### Triangle, Minkowski, and Hölder

- **Triangle:** $\lVert x+y\rVert\le\lVert x\rVert+\lVert y\rVert$.
- **Minkowski (triangle for $L^p$):** $\left(\sum|x_i+y_i|^p\right)^{1/p}\le\left(\sum|x_i|^p\right)^{1/p}+\left(\sum|y_i|^p\right)^{1/p}$.
- **Hölder:** $\sum|a_ib_i|\le\left(\sum|a_i|^p\right)^{1/p}\left(\sum|b_i|^q\right)^{1/q}$ for $1/p+1/q=1$.

### Jensen and Convexity

For a convex function $\varphi$,
$$
\varphi\!\left(\frac1n\sum x_i\right)\le \frac1n\sum \varphi(x_i),
$$
and the inequality reverses for concave $\varphi$. Jensen is the general engine behind AM–GM, the power-mean ordering, and countless optimization bounds.

### Chebyshev, Rearrangement, and Others

- **Chebyshev sum inequality:** for similarly ordered sequences, $\frac1n\sum a_ib_i\ge\left(\frac1n\sum a_i\right)\left(\frac1n\sum b_i\right)$.
- **Rearrangement:** a sum of products is maximized by pairing the sequences in the same order and minimized by opposite order.
- **Bernoulli:** $(1+x)^r\ge1+rx$ for $x\ge-1$, $r\ge1$ (or $r\le0$); the workhorse for growth and compounding bounds.
- **Young:** $ab\le\frac{a^p}{p}+\frac{b^q}{q}$.
- **Nesbitt:** $\frac{a}{b+c}+\frac{b}{c+a}+\frac{c}{a+b}\ge\frac32$ for positive $a,b,c$.
- **Weitzenböck:** $a^2+b^2+c^2\ge4\sqrt3\,\Delta$ for a triangle of area $\Delta$.
- **Von Neumann / trace inequalities, Ky Fan, and many more** generalize these to operators and matrices in [infinite-dimensional settings](infinite-dimensional-algebra-sequence-and-function-spaces.qmd).

### Green–Tao: Arithmetic Progressions of Primes

A spectacular 2004 result connects progressions and number theory:

> **Green–Tao theorem:** the prime numbers contain **arithmetically long progressions of every finite length** — for every $k$, there exist primes $p_1,\dots,p_k$ forming an AP.

It does not say the primes contain an *infinite* AP (they do not; Dirichlet handles progressions with a fixed common difference). It sits atop **Szemerédi's theorem** (any set of positive upper density contains arbitrarily long APs) and required a relative Szemerédi theorem for the sparse set of primes, using the **Green–Tao–Ziegler** structure theorem. See [the primes article](foundations-of-arithmetic-primes-radicals-and-the-riemann-hypothesis.qmd) for the surrounding theory.

## Laboratory: Verifying the Bounds

### Means Ordering and Power Means

```{python}
#| label: fig-means
#| fig-cap: "Left: the five classical means of a sample of positive numbers, showing the universal chain min ≤ HM ≤ GM ≤ AM ≤ RMS ≤ max. Right: the power mean M_p as a function of p, strictly increasing from the minimum (p→−∞) to the maximum (p→+∞) and passing through the harmonic (p=−1), geometric (p→0), arithmetic (p=1), and quadratic (p=2) means."

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(2)
x = rng.uniform(1, 10, size=8)

A = x.mean()
G = np.exp(np.log(x).mean())
H = len(x) / np.sum(1 / x)
RMS = np.sqrt(np.mean(x**2))
print("=== CLASSICAL MEANS OF A RANDOM SAMPLE ===")
print(f"  min={x.min():.4f}  HM={H:.4f}  GM={G:.4f}  AM={A:.4f}  "
      f"RMS={RMS:.4f}  max={x.max():.4f}")
print(f"  chain holds? {x.min() <= H <= G <= A <= RMS <= x.max()}")
print(f"  AM-GM check: AM-GM = {A-G:.4f} >= 0")

def power_mean(x, p):
    if abs(p) < 1e-9:
        return np.exp(np.log(x).mean())
    return np.mean(x**p) ** (1 / p)

ps = np.linspace(-6, 6, 400)
mp = [power_mean(x, p) for p in ps]

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(13, 4.7))
labels = ["min", "HM", "GM", "AM", "RMS", "max"]
vals = [x.min(), H, G, A, RMS, x.max()]
axes[0].bar(labels, vals, color=["#95a5a6", "#e74c3c", "#e67e22", "#27ae60", "#2980b9", "#95a5a6"])
for i, v in enumerate(vals):
    axes[0].text(i, v + 0.1, f"{v:.2f}", ha="center", fontsize=9)
axes[0].set_title("Means ordering"); axes[0].set_ylabel("value"); axes[0].grid(axis="y", alpha=0.3)

axes[1].plot(ps, mp, color="#2980b9", lw=2)
for p, name in [(-1, "HM"), (0, "GM"), (1, "AM"), (2, "RMS")]:
    axes[1].plot(p, power_mean(x, p), "o", color="#e74c3c")
    axes[1].annotate(name, (p, power_mean(x, p)), textcoords="offset points", xytext=(4, 4))
axes[1].axhline(x.min(), color="#95a5a6", ls=":"); axes[1].axhline(x.max(), color="#95a5a6", ls=":")
axes[1].set_title(r"Power mean $M_p$ increases with p")
axes[1].set_xlabel("p"); axes[1].set_ylabel(r"$M_p$"); axes[1].grid(alpha=0.3)
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()
```

### Progressions and Sums

```{python}
import numpy as np

print("=== AP AND GP PARTIAL SUMS ===")
a1, d, n = 3, 4, 20
ap = a1 + d * np.arange(n)
print(f"  AP {a1},{a1+d},...: S_n formula={n*(2*a1+(n-1)*d)//2}, numpy sum={ap.sum()}")

g1, r, n = 2, 0.5, 30
gp = g1 * r ** np.arange(n)
print(f"  GP {g1},{g1*r},...: S_n formula={g1*(1-r**n)/(1-r):.10f}, infinite={g1/(1-r):.10f}")

print("\n=== TELESCOPING: sum 1/(k(k+1)) = 1 - 1/(n+1) ===")
for N in [10, 100, 1000]:
    s = np.sum(1 / (np.arange(1, N + 1) * np.arange(2, N + 2)))
    print(f"  N={N:>4}: sum={s:.8f}, closed form={1 - 1/(N+1):.8f}")

print("\n=== FAULHABER: sum of powers ===")
N = 100
for p, formula in [(1, N*(N+1)//2), (2, N*(N+1)*(2*N+1)//6), (3, (N*(N+1)//2)**2)]:
    actual = np.sum(np.arange(1, N+1) ** p)
    print(f"  sum k^{p} = {actual:,} (formula {formula:,}, match={actual==formula})")

print("\n=== AM >= GM >= HM ===")
xs = np.array([1.0, 4.0, 9.0, 16.0])
AM, GM, HM = xs.mean(), np.exp(np.log(xs).mean()), len(xs)/np.sum(1/xs)
print(f"  HM={HM:.4f} <= GM={GM:.4f} <= AM={AM:.4f};  G^2 = {GM**2:.4f} = A*H = {AM*HM:.4f}")
```

### Arithmetic Progressions of Primes (Small Green–Tao Examples)

```{python}
import numpy as np

LIM = 100_000
sieve = np.ones(LIM + 1, bool); sieve[:2] = False
for i in range(2, int(LIM ** 0.5) + 1):
    if sieve[i]:
        sieve[i * i::i] = False
primes = set(np.nonzero(sieve)[0].tolist())

def find_ap(k, amax=5000, dmax=1000):
    for a in sorted(p for p in primes if p < amax):
        for d in range(2, dmax, 2):
            if all((a + i * d) in primes for i in range(k)):
                return [a + i * d for i in range(k)], d
    return None, None

print("=== ARITHMETIC PROGRESSIONS OF PRIMES (Green–Tao, small cases) ===")
for k in [3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8]:
    terms, d = find_ap(k)
    print(f"  length {k}: {terms}  (common difference {d})")
print("\n  the primes contain APs of every finite length, though the first")
print("  example grows extremely fast; length 8 already needs a large search.")
```

## Summary

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\textbf{AP: } && a_n=a+(n-1)d,\quad S_n=\tfrac{n}{2}(a_1+a_n). \\[3pt]
&\textbf{GP: } && a_n=ar^{n-1},\quad S_n=a\tfrac{1-r^n}{1-r},\quad S_\infty=\tfrac{a}{1-r}\ (|r|<1). \\[3pt]
&\textbf{HP: } && \text{reciprocals in AP; no simple partial sum.} \\[3pt]
&\textbf{Means: } && \min\le H\le G\le A\le \mathrm{RMS}\le\max;\ M_p \text{ increasing in } p;\ G^2=AH. \\[3pt]
&\textbf{Inequalities: } && \text{AM–GM, Cauchy–Schwarz, Hölder, Minkowski, Jensen, Chebyshev, rearrangement, Bernoulli, Young, Nesbitt.} \\[3pt]
&\textbf{Green–Tao: } && \text{primes contain arbitrarily long arithmetic progressions.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Progressions give the patterns; means summarize them; inequalities bound them. Their continuous counterparts — [geometric and power series in real and complex analysis](series-real-complex-analysis-special-sums-and-closed-forms.qmd) — are the subject of the next article.
