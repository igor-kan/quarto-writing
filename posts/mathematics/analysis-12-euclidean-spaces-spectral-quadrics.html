1. The Conceptual Shift: Coordinate Grids vs. Coordinate-Free Spaces

In elementary introductory calculus, vectors are introduced as arrows in 2D or 3D space, or as column tuples of numbers \((x_1, x_2, x_3)^T\). Quadric surfaces—such as ellipsoids, paraboloids, and hyperboloids—are presented as a memorized catalog of standard Cartesian equations: \(\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} \pm \frac{z^2}{c^2} = 1\). When cross-terms like \(2xy\) or \(-4xz\) appear, students lack the theoretical apparatus to understand how coordinates relate to intrinsic geometric shapes.

In modern geometric linear algebra and functional analysis, this coordinate-bound perspective is dismantled: 1. Coordinate-Free Vector Spaces: A Euclidean space is an abstract real vector space \(V\) equipped with an intrinsic positive-definite symmetric bilinear form \(\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle\). Bases are merely arbitrary frames of reference. 2. Dual Spaces and Covectors (\(V^*\)): Linear functionals \(\phi: V \to \mathbb{R}\) constitute the dual space \(V^*\). Gradients and differentials (\(df\)) are not vectors; they are covectors (1-forms) that ingest tangent vectors to produce directional rates of change. 3. The Riesz Representation Isomorphism: The inner product establishes a canonical isomorphism between vectors and covectors: the “musical isomorphisms” \(\flat: V \to V^*\) and \(\sharp: V^* \to V\). 4. Spectral Decomposition of Quadrics: Quadric surfaces \(Q = \{x \in V : \langle x, A x \rangle + \langle b, x \rangle + c = 0\}\) are classified intrinsically by the Spectral Theorem for Self-Adjoint Operators. Diagonalizing \(A\) via an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors aligns the coordinate axes with the principal axes of symmetry.

2. Foundational Definitions & Elementary Properties

Real Inner Product Spaces

Let \(V\) be a real vector space. An inner product is a map \(\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle: V \times V \to \mathbb{R}\) satisfying for all \(u, v, w \in V\) and \(\alpha \in \mathbb{R}\): 1. Symmetry: \(\langle u, v \rangle = \langle v, u \rangle\). 2. Linearity: \(\langle \alpha u + v, w \rangle = \alpha \langle u, w \rangle + \langle v, w \rangle\). 3. Positive-Definiteness: \(\langle v, v \rangle \ge 0\), and \(\langle v, v \rangle = 0 \iff v = \mathbf{0}\). The norm induced by the inner product is \(\|v\| = \sqrt{\langle v, v \rangle}\).

Dual Spaces and Dual Bases

  • The dual space of \(V\) is \(V^* = \mathcal{L}(V, \mathbb{R})\), the vector space of all continuous linear functionals on \(V\).
  • If \(\{e_1, \dots, e_n\}\) is a basis for \(V\), the unique dual basis \(\{e^1, \dots, e^n\}\) of \(V^*\) satisfies: \[e^i(e_j) = \delta^i_j = \begin{cases} 1, & i = j \\ 0, & i \neq j \end{cases}\]
  • Any vector \(v = \sum v^i e_i\) has coordinates given by evaluating the dual functionals: \(v^i = e^i(v)\).

Self-Adjoint Operators

A linear operator \(A: V \to V\) on an inner product space is self-adjoint (or symmetric) if: \[\langle A u, v \rangle = \langle u, A v \rangle, \quad \forall u, v \in V\] In any orthonormal basis, the matrix representation of a self-adjoint operator satisfies \(A^T = A\).

3. Intermediate Lemmas & Rigorous Proofs

Lemma 12.1 (The Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality): For any vectors \(u, v\) in a real inner product space \(V\): \[|\langle u, v \rangle| \le \|u\| \|v\|\] with equality if and only if \(u\) and \(v\) are linearly dependent.

Proof: If \(v = \mathbf{0}\), both sides equal zero, so the inequality holds trivially. Assume \(v \neq \mathbf{0}\). For any real scalar \(t \in \mathbb{R}\), consider the norm square: \[p(t) = \|u + t v\|^2 = \langle u + t v, u + t v \rangle = \langle u, u \rangle + 2 t \langle u, v \rangle + t^2 \langle v, v \rangle = \|u\|^2 + 2 t \langle u, v \rangle + t^2 \|v\|^2\] By positive-definiteness, \(p(t) \ge 0\) for all \(t \in \mathbb{R}\). This is a quadratic polynomial \(a t^2 + b t + c \ge 0\) in \(t\), where \(a = \|v\|^2 > 0\), \(b = 2\langle u, v \rangle\), and \(c = \|u\|^2\). Because a quadratic polynomial that is non-negative everywhere cannot have two distinct real roots, its discriminant must be non-positive: \[\Delta = b^2 - 4ac = 4 \langle u, v \rangle^2 - 4 \|v\|^2 \|u\|^2 \le 0\] Dividing by 4 gives: \[\langle u, v \rangle^2 \le \|u\|^2 \|v\|^2\] Taking the square root of both sides yields \(|\langle u, v \rangle| \le \|u\| \|v\|\). Equality holds if and only if \(\Delta = 0\), meaning there is a unique real root \(t_0\) such that \(\|u + t_0 v\|^2 = 0 \iff u = -t_0 v\), meaning \(u\) and \(v\) are collinear. \(\blacksquare\)

Lemma 12.2 (Riesz Representation Theorem — Finite Dimensions): Let \(V\) be a finite-dimensional real inner product space. For every linear functional \(\phi \in V^*\), there exists a unique vector \(v_\phi \in V\) such that: \[\phi(u) = \langle u, v_\phi \rangle, \quad \forall u \in V\]

Proof: 1. Existence: Choose an orthonormal basis \(\{e_1, \dots, e_n\}\) of \(V\) (satisfying \(\langle e_i, e_j \rangle = \delta_{ij}\)). Define the candidate vector: \[v_\phi = \sum_{i=1}^n \phi(e_i) e_i\] For any arbitrary vector \(u = \sum_{j=1}^n u^j e_j \in V\), linearity of \(\phi\) gives \(\phi(u) = \sum_{j=1}^n u^j \phi(e_j)\). Evaluating the inner product: \[\langle u, v_\phi \rangle = \left\langle \sum_{j=1}^n u^j e_j, \sum_{i=1}^n \phi(e_i) e_i \right\rangle = \sum_{i,j} u^j \phi(e_i) \langle e_j, e_i \rangle = \sum_{i=1}^n u^i \phi(e_i) = \phi(u)\] 2. Uniqueness: Suppose there exists another vector \(w \in V\) such that \(\langle u, w \rangle = \phi(u)\) for all \(u\). Then \(\langle u, v_\phi - w \rangle = 0\) for all \(u \in V\). Setting \(u = v_\phi - w\) yields \(\|v_\phi - w\|^2 = 0 \implies w = v_\phi\). \(\blacksquare\)

Lemma 12.3 (Orthogonality of Eigenspaces of Self-Adjoint Operators): Let \(A: V \to V\) be self-adjoint. 1. All eigenvalues of \(A\) are real. 2. Eigenvectors corresponding to distinct eigenvalues are mutually orthogonal.

Proof: 1. Let \(\lambda\) be an eigenvalue with eigenvector \(v \neq \mathbf{0}\), so \(A v = \lambda v\). In the complexification \(V_\mathbb{C}\), \(\lambda \langle v, v \rangle = \langle \lambda v, v \rangle = \langle A v, v \rangle = \langle v, A v \rangle = \langle v, \lambda v \rangle = \bar{\lambda} \langle v, v \rangle\). Since \(\|v\|^2 > 0\), we have \(\lambda = \bar{\lambda} \implies \lambda \in \mathbb{R}\). 2. Let \(A v_1 = \lambda_1 v_1\) and \(A v_2 = \lambda_2 v_2\) with \(\lambda_1 \neq \lambda_2\). Compute \(\langle A v_1, v_2 \rangle\): \[\lambda_1 \langle v_1, v_2 \rangle = \langle A v_1, v_2 \rangle = \langle v_1, A v_2 \rangle = \langle v_1, \lambda_2 v_2 \rangle = \lambda_2 \langle v_1, v_2 \rangle\] Regrouping: \((\lambda_1 - \lambda_2) \langle v_1, v_2 \rangle = 0\). Because \(\lambda_1 \neq \lambda_2\), we must have \(\langle v_1, v_2 \rangle = 0\). \(\blacksquare\)

4. Rigorous Construction & Advanced Theorems

The Spectral Theorem for Real Symmetric Operators

Theorem 12.1 (The Spectral Theorem): Let \(V\) be an \(n\)-dimensional real inner product space and \(A: V \to V\) a self-adjoint operator. Then there exists an orthonormal basis \(\{u_1, \dots, u_n\}\) of \(V\) consisting entirely of eigenvectors of \(A\). Equivalently, any real symmetric \(n \times n\) matrix \(A\) can be orthogonally diagonalized: \[A = Q \Lambda Q^T, \quad Q \in O(n), \quad \Lambda = \text{diag}(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n)\]

Proof (Variational Method via Rayleigh Quotient): 1. Consider the unit sphere \(S = \{x \in V : \|x\| = 1\}\). \(S\) is closed and bounded in \(\mathbb{R}^n\), hence compact by the Heine-Borel theorem. 2. Define the quadratic Rayleigh functional \(R(x) = \langle x, A x \rangle\). Because \(R\) is continuous and \(S\) is compact, by the Extreme Value Theorem (Theorem 4.1), \(R\) attains its maximum on \(S\) at some point \(u_1 \in S\). Let \(\lambda_1 = R(u_1) = \langle u_1, A u_1 \rangle\). 3. By Lagrange multipliers (or directional derivatives on \(S\)), for any vector \(w\) orthogonal to \(u_1\) (\(\langle w, u_1 \rangle = 0\)), the curve \(\gamma(t) = \frac{u_1 + t w}{\|u_1 + t w\|}\) lies on \(S\) with \(\gamma(0) = u_1\). The derivative of \(R(\gamma(t))\) at \(t = 0\) must vanish because \(t = 0\) is a maximum: \[\left. \frac{d}{dt} R(\gamma(t)) \right|_{t=0} = 2 \langle A u_1 - \lambda_1 u_1, w \rangle = 0\] Since this holds for all \(w \in \{u_1\}^\perp\), the vector \(A u_1 - \lambda_1 u_1\) must be proportional to \(u_1\). Taking the inner product with \(u_1\) confirms \(A u_1 = \lambda_1 u_1\). Thus \(u_1\) is an eigenvector with eigenvalue \(\lambda_1\). 4. Consider the orthogonal complement \(W = \{u_1\}^\perp\). If \(w \in W\), then \(\langle A w, u_1 \rangle = \langle w, A u_1 \rangle = \langle w, \lambda_1 u_1 \rangle = \lambda_1 \langle w, u_1 \rangle = 0\). Hence \(A(W) \subseteq W\) (\(W\) is an invariant subspace under \(A\)). The restriction \(A|_W: W \to W\) is a self-adjoint operator on the \((n-1)\)-dimensional inner product space \(W\). By induction on dimension, \(W\) possesses an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors \(\{u_2, \dots, u_n\}\). The combined set \(\{u_1, u_2, \dots, u_n\}\) forms an orthonormal eigenbasis of \(V\). \(\blacksquare\)

Spectral Classification of Quadric Surfaces

Theorem 12.2 (Canonical Quadric Classification in \(\mathbb{R}^3\)): Every general second-degree algebraic surface: \[x^T A x + 2 b^T x + c = 0, \quad A = A^T \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}, \ b \in \mathbb{R}^3, \ c \in \mathbb{R}\] is equivalent, under a rigid Euclidean motion \(x = Q y + x_0\) (\(Q \in SO(3)\)), to one of the canonical forms: 1. Ellipsoid: \(\frac{y_1^2}{a^2} + \frac{y_2^2}{b^2} + \frac{y_3^2}{c^2} = 1\) (\(\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \lambda_3 > 0\)). 2. Hyperboloid of One Sheet: \(\frac{y_1^2}{a^2} + \frac{y_2^2}{b^2} - \frac{y_3^2}{c^2} = 1\) (two positive eigenvalues, one negative). 3. Hyperboloid of Two Sheets: \(\frac{y_1^2}{a^2} - \frac{y_2^2}{b^2} - \frac{y_3^2}{c^2} = 1\) (one positive eigenvalue, two negative). 4. Elliptic Paraboloid: \(\frac{y_1^2}{a^2} + \frac{y_2^2}{b^2} = 2 p y_3\) (\(\lambda_1, \lambda_2 > 0, \lambda_3 = 0\)). 5. Hyperbolic Paraboloid (Saddle): \(\frac{y_1^2}{a^2} - \frac{y_2^2}{b^2} = 2 p y_3\) (\(\lambda_1 > 0 > \lambda_2, \lambda_3 = 0\)).

(For the planar conic treatment, the rotation angle formula \(\cot(2\theta) = \frac{A-C}{B}\), discriminant invariant \(\Delta = B^2 - 4AC\), and optical reflection properties, see the Stewart Appendices Masterclass: Appendix C.)

5. Concrete Worked Examples & Step-by-Step Calculations

Worked Example 12.1: Orthogonal Diagonalization and Principal Axes of an Ellipse

Classify and rotate into canonical form the rotated ellipse in \(\mathbb{R}^2\): \[5 x^2 - 4 x y + 8 y^2 = 36\]

  1. Matrix Representation: Rewrite the quadratic form as \(x^T A x = 36\), where \(x = \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix}\): \[A = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & -2 \\ -2 & 8 \end{pmatrix}\]

  2. Eigenvalue Computation: \[\det(A - \lambda I) = \det \begin{pmatrix} 5 - \lambda & -2 \\ -2 & 8 - \lambda \end{pmatrix} = (5 - \lambda)(8 - \lambda) - (-2)^2 = \lambda^2 - 13\lambda + 40 - 4 = \lambda^2 - 13\lambda + 36 = 0\] Factoring the quadratic equation: \[(\lambda - 9)(\lambda - 4) = 0 \implies \lambda_1 = 9, \quad \lambda_2 = 4\]

  3. Eigenvector Computation & Orthonormalization:

    • For \(\lambda_1 = 9\): \[(A - 9I) v = \begin{pmatrix} -4 & -2 \\ -2 & -1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} \implies -4x - 2y = 0 \implies y = -2x\] Normalized eigenvector: \[u_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1^2 + (-2)^2}} \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}} \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \end{pmatrix}\]
    • For \(\lambda_2 = 4\): \[(A - 4I) v = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -2 \\ -2 & 4 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} \implies x - 2y = 0 \implies x = 2y\] Normalized eigenvector: \[u_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2^2 + 1^2}} \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}} \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}\] Notice that \(\langle u_1, u_2 \rangle = \frac{1}{5}(1 \cdot 2 + (-2) \cdot 1) = 0\) (rigorous confirmation of Lemma 12.3).

  4. Orthogonal Coordinate Transformation: Form the rotation matrix \(Q = [u_1 \mid u_2] = \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ -2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\). In rotated coordinates \(\begin{pmatrix} \xi \\ \eta \end{pmatrix} = Q^T \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix}\), the equation becomes: \[\lambda_1 \xi^2 + \lambda_2 \eta^2 = 36 \implies 9 \xi^2 + 4 \eta^2 = 36\] Dividing by 36: \[\frac{\xi^2}{4} + \frac{\eta^2}{9} = 1 \implies \frac{\xi^2}{2^2} + \frac{\eta^2}{3^2} = 1\]

  5. Geometric Interpretation: The curve is an ellipse with semi-minor axis \(a = 2\) along the direction \(u_1\) and semi-major axis \(b = 3\) along the direction \(u_2\). The rotation angle is \(\theta = \arctan(-2) \approx -63.43^\circ\).

Worked Example 12.2: The Gradient as a Covector under Non-Euclidean Metrics

Let \(f(x, y) = 3x^2 + 2xy - y^2\). Compute the differential 1-form \(df\) and contrast it with the gradient vector \(\nabla f = (df)^\sharp\) under a non-standard Riemannian metric tensor: \[g = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\]

  1. The Differential (Covector): The differential is independent of any metric: \[df = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} dx + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} dy = (6x + 2y) dx + (2x - 2y) dy\] At the point \((x_0, y_0) = (1, 1)\): \[df|_{(1, 1)} = 8 dx + 0 dy = 8 dx \in V^*\]

  2. Musical Isomorphism \(\sharp\) (Vector Gradient): By the Riesz Representation Theorem, the gradient \(\nabla_g f\) is the unique vector satisfying: \[g(\nabla_g f, v) = df(v), \quad \forall v \in V \implies \nabla_g f = g^{-1} (df)^T\] Invert the metric tensor: \(\det g = 4 - 1 = 3\). \[g^{-1} = \frac{1}{3} \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\] Applying \(g^{-1}\) to the covector components \(\begin{pmatrix} 8 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}\): \[\nabla_g f = \frac{1}{3} \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 8 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 16/3 \\ -8/3 \end{pmatrix}\] Notice that under the standard Euclidean metric \(g = I\), the gradient would be \(\begin{pmatrix} 8 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}\). The metric rotates and stretches the gradient vector!

6. Multi-Disciplinary Applications

  • Machine Learning & Data Science (PCA): Principal Component Analysis is the spectral decomposition of the empirical covariance matrix \(\Sigma = \frac{1}{N} X^T X = Q \Lambda Q^T\). The principal axes of highest variance correspond to the largest eigenvalues \(\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \dots\), enabling dimensionality reduction.
  • Quantum Mechanics: Physical observables (energy, momentum, angular momentum) are represented by self-adjoint operators \(\hat{A}\) on infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces. By the Spectral Theorem, the possible measurement outcomes are precisely the eigenvalues \(\lambda \in \sigma(\hat{A})\), and states collapse into corresponding eigenstates.
  • Continuum Mechanics: The Cauchy stress tensor \(\sigma_{ij}\) is a symmetric \(3 \times 3\) tensor. Its eigenvalues are the principal stresses (\(\sigma_1, \sigma_2, \sigma_3\)), and its eigenvectors give the orientations of planes experiencing zero shear stress.

import numpy as np

def diagonalize_quadric_2d(A: np.ndarray):
    # eigh is optimized for symmetric / Hermitian matrices
    eigenvalues, eigenvectors = np.linalg.eigh(A)
    return eigenvalues, eigenvectors

# Quadratic form: 5x^2 - 4xy + 8y^2 = 36
A = np.array([[5.0, -2.0], [-2.0, 8.0]])
vals, vecs = diagonalize_quadric_2d(A)

print(f"Python 3: Eigenvalues = {vals}")
print(f"  Axis 1 (lambda = {vals[0]:.1f}): {vecs[:, 0]}")
print(f"  Axis 2 (lambda = {vals[1]:.1f}): {vecs[:, 1]}")
#include <iostream>
#include <cmath>
#include <iomanip>

int main() {
    // Diagonalize symmetric [[5, -2], [-2, 8]]
    double a = 5.0, b = -2.0, c = 8.0;
    double tr = a + c;
    double det = a * c - b * b;
    double disc = std::sqrt(tr * tr - 4.0 * det);
    double l1 = (tr - disc) / 2.0; // lambda = 4
    double l2 = (tr + disc) / 2.0; // lambda = 9

    // Eigenvectors
    double u1_x = 2.0 / std::sqrt(5.0), u1_y = 1.0 / std::sqrt(5.0);
    double u2_x = 1.0 / std::sqrt(5.0), u2_y = -2.0 / std::sqrt(5.0);

    std::cout << std::fixed << std::setprecision(4);
    std::cout << "C++20:    Eigenvalues: (" << l1 << ", " << l2 << ")\n"
              << "          Axis 1: (" << u1_x << ", " << u1_y << ")\n"
              << "          Axis 2: (" << u2_x << ", " << u2_y << ")\n";
    return 0;
}

program spectral_quadric
  implicit none
  real(8) :: a, b, c, tr, det, disc, l1, l2

  a = 5.0d0; b = -2.0d0; c = 8.0d0
  tr = a + c
  det = a * c - b * b
  disc = sqrt(tr**2 - 4.0d0 * det)
  l1 = (tr - disc) / 2.0d0
  l2 = (tr + disc) / 2.0d0

  print '(A,F8.4,A,F8.4)', "Fortran:  lambda_1 = ", l1, ", lambda_2 = ", l2
