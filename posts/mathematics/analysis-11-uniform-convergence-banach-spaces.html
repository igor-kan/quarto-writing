Banach spaces of continuous functions, failure of pointwise limits, the 3-epsilon lemma, and equicontinuity.

1. The Conceptual Shift: Pointwise Limits vs. Function Space Geometry

In introductory calculus, sequences and series of functions are treated as families of static formulas evaluated at individual points \(x\). Convergence is conceptualized purely pointwise: for each fixed \(x\), does the number sequence \(f_n(x)\) approach a limit \(L(x)\)?

In rigorous modern analysis and functional analysis, pointwise convergence is revealed to be fatally flawed: 1. Destruction of Continuity: Pointwise limits of continuous functions can fail to be continuous! For example, \(f_n(x) = x^n\) on \([0, 1]\) consists entirely of smooth polynomials, yet its pointwise limit is a discontinuous step function (\(0\) for \(x \in [0, 1)\) and \(1\) at \(x = 1\)). 2. Failure of Operator Commutativity: Pointwise convergence fails to commute with integration and differentiation: \[\lim_{n \to \infty} \int_0^1 f_n(x) dx \neq \int_0^1 \left(\lim_{n \to \infty} f_n(x)\right) dx\] 3. The Topology of Uniform Convergence: The space of continuous functions \(C(K)\) on a compact space is an infinite-dimensional Banach space under the supremum norm \(\|f\|_\infty = \sup_{x \in K} |f(x)|\). Uniform convergence (\(d_\infty(f_n, f) \to 0\)) is the topological condition that preserves continuity and allows limit swapping. 4. Infinite-Dimensional Compactness: The Heine-Borel theorem fails in infinite-dimensional spaces: closed and bounded sets in \(C(K)\) are generally not compact. Compactness requires the additional constraint of equicontinuity, codified by the Arzelà-Ascoli Theorem.

2. Foundational Definitions & Elementary Properties

Pointwise vs. Uniform Convergence

Let \(E \subseteq \mathbb{R}\) and \(f_n, f: E \to \mathbb{R}\). * \(f_n \to f\) pointwise on \(E\) if: \[\forall x \in E, \ \forall \epsilon > 0, \ \exists N = N(x, \epsilon) \in \mathbb{N} \text{ such that } n > N \implies |f_n(x) - f(x)| < \epsilon\] * \(f_n \to f\) uniformly on \(E\) if: \[\forall \epsilon > 0, \ \exists N = N(\epsilon) \in \mathbb{N} \text{ such that } n > N \implies \forall x \in E, \ |f_n(x) - f(x)| < \epsilon\] Equivalently: \(\|f_n - f\|_\infty = \sup_{x \in E} |f_n(x) - f(x)| \to 0\) as \(n \to \infty\).

Equicontinuity

A family of functions \(\mathcal{F} \subset C(X)\) on a metric space \((X, d)\) is equicontinuous if: \[\forall \epsilon > 0, \ \exists \delta > 0 \text{ such that } d(x, y) < \delta \implies |f(x) - f(y)| < \epsilon, \quad \forall f \in \mathcal{F}, \ \forall x, y \in X\] The critical distinction is that \(\delta\) depends only on \(\epsilon\), uniformly across the entire infinite family \(\mathcal{F}\).

3. Intermediate Lemmas & Rigorous Proofs

Lemma 11.1 (Uniform Cauchy Criterion): A sequence of functions \(f_n: E \to \mathbb{R}\) converges uniformly on \(E\) if and only if for every \(\epsilon > 0\), there exists an integer \(N\) such that: \[m, n > N \implies \sup_{x \in E} |f_n(x) - f_m(x)| < \epsilon\]

Proof: \((\implies)\) If \(f_n \to f\) uniformly, choose \(N\) such that \(n > N \implies \|f_n - f\|_\infty < \epsilon / 2\). Then for \(m, n > N\): \[|f_n(x) - f_m(x)| \le |f_n(x) - f(x)| + |f(x) - f_m(x)| < \frac{\epsilon}{2} + \frac{\epsilon}{2} = \epsilon\] \((\impliedby)\) For each fixed \(x \in E\), the number sequence \((f_n(x))\) is a Cauchy sequence in \(\mathbb{R}\). By completeness of \(\mathbb{R}\), \(f(x) = \lim_{n \to \infty} f_n(x)\) exists. In the Cauchy inequality \(|f_n(x) - f_m(x)| < \epsilon\), take the limit as \(m \to \infty\): \[|f_n(x) - f(x)| \le \epsilon, \quad \forall n > N, \ \forall x \in E\] Hence \(\|f_n - f\|_\infty \le \epsilon\), proving uniform convergence. \(\blacksquare\)

Lemma 11.2 (Uniform Limit Theorem — Preservation of Continuity): If \(f_n: E \to \mathbb{R}\) are continuous and \(f_n \to f\) uniformly on \(E\), then \(f\) is continuous on \(E\).

Proof (The \(3\epsilon\) Argument): Let \(x_0 \in E\) and \(\epsilon > 0\). 1. By uniform convergence, choose \(N\) such that \(\|f_N - f\|_\infty < \frac{\epsilon}{3}\). 2. Since \(f_N\) is continuous at \(x_0\), choose \(\delta > 0\) such that \(|x - x_0| < \delta \implies |f_N(x) - f_N(x_0)| < \frac{\epsilon}{3}\). 3. Now for any \(x \in E\) with \(|x - x_0| < \delta\), apply the triangle inequality: \[|f(x) - f(x_0)| \le |f(x) - f_N(x)| + |f_N(x) - f_N(x_0)| + |f_N(x_0) - f(x_0)| < \frac{\epsilon}{3} + \frac{\epsilon}{3} + \frac{\epsilon}{3} = \epsilon\] Hence \(f\) is continuous at \(x_0\). \(\blacksquare\)

Lemma 11.3 (Interchange of Limit and Integral): If \(f_n \to f\) uniformly on a compact interval \([a, b]\), and each \(f_n\) is Riemann integrable, then \(f\) is Riemann integrable and: \[\lim_{n \to \infty} \int_a^b f_n(x) dx = \int_a^b f(x) dx\]

Proof: Given \(\epsilon > 0\), choose \(N\) such that \(n > N \implies \|f_n - f\|_\infty < \frac{\epsilon}{b - a}\). Then for all \(n > N\): \[\left| \int_a^b f_n(x) dx - \int_a^b f(x) dx \right| = \left| \int_a^b (f_n(x) - f(x)) dx \right| \le \int_a^b |f_n(x) - f(x)| dx \le \int_a^b \|f_n - f\|_\infty dx < \left( \frac{\epsilon}{b - a} \right) (b - a) = \epsilon\] This proves the limit swaps with the integral. \(\blacksquare\)

4. Rigorous Construction & Advanced Theorems

The Weierstrass M-Test

Theorem 11.1 (Weierstrass M-Test): Let \(\sum_{n=1}^\infty u_n(x)\) be a series of functions on \(E\). Suppose there exists a sequence of positive real constants \(M_n \ge 0\) such that: 1. \(|u_n(x)| \le M_n\) for all \(x \in E\) and all \(n \in \mathbb{N}\). 2. \(\sum_{n=1}^\infty M_n < \infty\) converges. Then \(\sum_{n=1}^\infty u_n(x)\) converges uniformly and absolutely on \(E\).

Proof: Let \(S_k(x) = \sum_{n=1}^k u_n(x)\) be the partial sums. For \(k > j\): \[|S_k(x) - S_j(x)| = \left| \sum_{n=j+1}^k u_n(x) \right| \le \sum_{n=j+1}^k |u_n(x)| \le \sum_{n=j+1}^k M_n\] Since \(\sum M_n\) converges, its sequence of partial sums is Cauchy in \(\mathbb{R}\). Given \(\epsilon > 0\), there exists \(N\) such that for all \(k > j > N\), \(\sum_{n=j+1}^k M_n < \epsilon\). Thus \(\|S_k - S_j\|_\infty < \epsilon\) for all \(k > j > N\). By Lemma 11.1 (Uniform Cauchy Criterion), the partial sums converge uniformly on \(E\). \(\blacksquare\)

The Arzelà-Ascoli Theorem

Theorem 11.2 (Arzelà-Ascoli Theorem): Let \(K\) be a compact metric space. A subset \(\mathcal{F} \subset C(K)\) is relatively compact (its closure is compact in the supremum metric) if and only if \(\mathcal{F}\) is: 1. Pointwise bounded: for every \(x \in K\), \(\sup_{f \in \mathcal{F}} |f(x)| < \infty\). 2. Equicontinuous: \(\forall \epsilon > 0, \exists \delta > 0 \text{ s.t. } d(x, y) < \delta \implies |f(x) - f(y)| < \epsilon, \ \forall f \in \mathcal{F}\).

Proof Sketch: Compact metric spaces are separable; let \(\{x_1, x_2, \dots\}\) be a dense countable subset of \(K\). Given any sequence \((f_n) \subset \mathcal{F}\): 1. Since \((f_n(x_1))\) is bounded in \(\mathbb{R}\), by Bolzano-Weierstrass there exists a subsequence \(f_{1,n}\) converging at \(x_1\). 2. From \(f_{1,n}\), extract a sub-subsequence \(f_{2,n}\) converging at \(x_2\). 3. By Cantor’s diagonal argument, the diagonal sequence \(g_n = f_{n,n}\) converges at every point of the dense set \(\{x_k\}\). 4. By equicontinuity, choose a finite \(\delta\)-net covering \(K\). The uniform control on neighborhoods extends convergence from the dense set to all of \(K\), proving \(g_n\) converges uniformly on \(K\). \(\blacksquare\)

5. Concrete Worked Examples & Step-by-Step Calculations

Worked Example 11.1: Pointwise vs. Uniform Convergence Analysis

Investigate the convergence of the sequence: \[f_n(x) = \frac{n x}{1 + n^2 x^2}, \quad x \in [0, 1]\]

  1. Pointwise Limit:

    • At \(x = 0\): \(f_n(0) = \frac{0}{1} = 0\).
    • For \(x > 0\): \(f_n(x) = \frac{n x}{1 + n^2 x^2} = \frac{x / n}{1/n^2 + x^2} \to \frac{0}{x^2} = 0\) as \(n \to \infty\). Thus, the pointwise limit is the identically zero function: \(f(x) \equiv 0\).

  2. Supremum Norm Calculation: To test for uniform convergence, compute \(\|f_n - f\|_\infty = \sup_{x \in [0, 1]} f_n(x)\). Differentiating to find the maximum: \[f_n'(x) = \frac{n(1 + n^2 x^2) - n x(2 n^2 x)}{(1 + n^2 x^2)^2} = \frac{n - n^3 x^2}{(1 + n^2 x^2)^2} = \frac{n(1 - n^2 x^2)}{(1 + n^2 x^2)^2}\] Setting \(f_n'(x) = 0\) gives critical point \(x^* = \frac{1}{n} \in [0, 1]\). Evaluating at the critical point: \[f_n\left(\frac{1}{n}\right) = \frac{n(1/n)}{1 + n^2(1/n^2)} = \frac{1}{1 + 1} = \frac{1}{2}\]

  3. Conclusion on Uniform Convergence: \[\|f_n - f\|_\infty = \sup_{x \in [0, 1]} |f_n(x) - 0| = \frac{1}{2} \not\to 0 \quad \text{as } n \to \infty\] Therefore, \(f_n\) does not converge uniformly to \(0\) on \([0, 1]\)! However, on any restricted interval \([\delta, 1]\) with \(\delta > 0\): for \(n > 1/\delta\), the peak \(1/n\) lies outside the interval, and \(\sup_{x \in [\delta, 1]} f_n(x) = f_n(\delta) \approx \frac{1}{n \delta} \to 0\). Hence convergence IS uniform on \([\delta, 1]\).

Worked Example 11.2: The Weierstrass Nowhere-Differentiable Monster

Karl Weierstrass (1872) shattered the 19th-century consensus that continuous functions must be differentiable almost everywhere by constructing: \[W(x) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty a^n \cos(b^n \pi x), \quad 0 < a < 1, \ ab > 1 + \frac{3}{2}\pi, \ b \text{ odd integer}\]

  1. Proof of Continuity via Weierstrass M-Test: For each term: \(|a^n \cos(b^n \pi x)| \le a^n\). Since \(0 < a < 1\), the geometric series \(\sum_{n=0}^\infty a^n = \frac{1}{1 - a} < \infty\) converges. By Theorem 11.1 (Weierstrass M-Test), the series converges uniformly on all of \(\mathbb{R}\). Since each partial sum is a finite sum of continuous cosine functions, by Lemma 11.2, \(W(x)\) is everywhere continuous on \(\mathbb{R}\).

  2. Divergence of Difference Quotients: Taking the formal derivative term-by-term yields: \[W'(x) \stackrel{?}{=} -\pi \sum_{n=0}^\infty (ab)^n \sin(b^n \pi x)\] Since \(ab > 1\), \((ab)^n \to \infty\). The difference quotients \(\frac{W(x + h) - W(x)}{h}\) oscillate with unbounded amplitude as \(h \to 0\), rigorously proving that \(W'(x)\) exists at no point in \(\mathbb{R}\)!

6. Multi-Disciplinary Applications

  • Machine Learning & Neural Networks: The Universal Approximation Theorem (Hornik, Cybenko) states that single-hidden-layer feedforward networks are dense in the Banach space \((C(K), \|\cdot\|_\infty)\). Proving that neural networks can approximate any continuous function to arbitrary \(\epsilon\)-precision relies on the Stone-Weierstrass theorem.
  • Partial Differential Equations (PDEs): The existence of weak solutions to elliptic and parabolic PDEs (e.g., Navier-Stokes, reaction-diffusion) uses the Galerkin method, where approximate finite-dimensional solutions are proved to have a convergent subsequence via the Arzelà-Ascoli theorem and Rellich-Kondrachov compactness theorems.
  • Fourier Analysis & Signal Processing: Gibbs phenomenon—the 9% overshoot at jump discontinuities in Fourier square waves—occurs precisely because Fourier series fail to converge uniformly near jump discontinuities.

import numpy as np

def weierstrass_func(x: float, a: float = 0.5, b: int = 13, n_terms: int = 100) -> float:
    n = np.arange(n_terms)
    terms = (a ** n) * np.cos((b ** n) * np.pi * x)
    return float(np.sum(terms))

def difference_quotient(f, x: float, h: float) -> float:
    return (f(x + h) - f(x)) / h

use std::f64::consts::PI;

fn weierstrass(x: f64, a: f64, b: usize, terms: usize) -> f64 {
    let mut sum = 0.0;
    let mut a_n = 1.0;
    let mut b_n = 1.0;
    for _ in 0..terms {
        sum += a_n * (b_n * PI * x).cos();
        a_n *= a;
        b_n *= b as f64;
    }
    sum
}
