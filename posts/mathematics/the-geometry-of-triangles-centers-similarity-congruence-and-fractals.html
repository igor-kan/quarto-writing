The triangle is the fundamental rigid shape. Its four classical centers — centroid, circumcenter, incenter, orthocenter — are governed by concurrency theorems, and understanding them unlocks similarity, congruence, and the geometry of physical objects. From there, the same ideas of scaling and symmetry extend to polygons, curves, fractals, and the angular language of navigation and astronomy.

This article proves the key facts rather than merely stating them, and is careful about the subtle conditions that do and do not guarantee similarity and congruence.

1 The Four Classical Centers

1.1 Centroid: The Center of Mass

The medians (from each vertex to the midpoint of the opposite side) are concurrent, meeting at the centroid \(G\), which divides each median in the ratio \(2:1\) from the vertex.

Why the centroid is the center of mass of a uniform triangle. Slice the triangle into infinitely thin strips parallel to a side. Reflection symmetry of each strip about the median to that side shows the center of mass of every strip lies on that median. Hence the center of mass of the whole lamina lies on that median; repeating for a second median, it lies on both, i.e. at their intersection — the centroid. Equivalently, a median divides a triangle into two triangles of equal area, so the first moment about the median vanishes. In coordinates, \[ G=\frac{A+B+C}{3}. \]

1.2 Circumcenter: Equidistant from the Vertices

The perpendicular bisectors of the sides are concurrent at the circumcenter \(O\), the unique point equidistant from the three vertices, hence the center of the circumcircle of radius \(R\).

Proof of concurrency. Let \(P\) be the intersection of the perpendicular bisectors of \(AB\) and \(BC\) (they are not parallel, so they meet). Then \(PA=PB\) (from the first bisector) and \(PB=PC\) (from the second), so \(PA=PB=PC\). Therefore \(P\) also lies on the perpendicular bisector of \(AC\), and all three are concurrent. The equidistance is now immediate.

The circumradius satisfies the law of sines: \(a/\sin A=b/\sin B=c/\sin C=2R\).

1.3 Incenter: Equidistant from the Sides

The angle bisectors are concurrent at the incenter \(I\), the center of the incircle of radius \(r\).

Proof of concurrency. The locus of points equidistant from the two sides of an angle is its angle bisector. Let \(P\) be the intersection of the bisectors of angles \(A\) and \(B\). Then \(P\) is equidistant from sides \(AB\) and \(AC\) (bisector of \(A\)), and from \(AB\) and \(BC\) (bisector of \(B\)); hence equidistant from all three sides, so it lies on the bisector of \(C\) too. Since the distance from \(I\) to each side is \(r\), the area is \(\Delta=rs\) with \(s\) the semiperimeter.

1.4 Orthocenter: Intersection of Altitudes

The altitudes (from a vertex perpendicular to the opposite side) are concurrent at the orthocenter \(H\). A clean proof: through each vertex draw the line parallel to the opposite side; these form an anticomplementary triangle for which the original altitudes become perpendicular bisectors, whose concurrency is the circumcenter fact. In coordinates, \[ H=A+B+C-2O. \]

1.5 Excenters, the Euler Line, and the Nine-Point Circle

  • Excenters: each external angle bisector meets two internal ones; there are three excenters and three excircles, each tangent to one side externally and the others’ extensions.
  • Euler line: \(O\), \(G\), \(H\) are collinear with \(OG:GH=1:2\).
  • Nine-point circle: passes through the three side midpoints, the three altitude feet, and the three midpoints from each vertex to \(H\); center at the midpoint of \(OH\); radius \(R/2\).
  • Barycentric and trilinear coordinates express every center as a weighted combination, unifying hundreds of named centers (Kimberling’s Encyclopedia of Triangle Centers lists tens of thousands).

1.6 Centers of Tetrahedra and Pyramids

For a tetrahedron, the four medians (from a vertex to the centroid of the opposite face) are concurrent at the centroid, which is the average of the four vertices and the center of mass of the uniform solid. More generally, for a uniform simplex the centroid is the average of its vertices, and for a right or regular pyramid the center of mass lies on the axis through the apex and the base centroid, at a height determined by the ratio of volumes. For a cone, the center of mass is one quarter of the height from the base; for a uniform solid hemisphere, three-eighths of the radius from the flat face.

Center Defined by Key property
Centroid \(G\) medians center of mass; \(G=(A+B+C)/3\)
Circumcenter \(O\) perpendicular bisectors equidistant from vertices; radius \(R\)
Incenter \(I\) angle bisectors equidistant from sides; radius \(r\); \(\Delta=rs\)
Orthocenter \(H\) altitudes \(H=A+B+C-2O\); on the Euler line

2 Special Triangles

  • Equilateral: all sides equal, all angles \(60^\circ\); all four centers coincide; symmetry group \(D_3\) (order 6); area \(\frac{\sqrt3}{4}a^2\).
  • Isosceles: two equal sides; the axis of symmetry is the perpendicular bisector of the base, which is also the median, altitude, and angle bisector from the apex; base angles are equal (and conversely). The centroid, incenter, circumcenter, and orthocenter all lie on the axis, and for the equilateral case coincide.
  • Right triangle: Pythagorean theorem; the circumcenter is the midpoint of the hypotenuse (Thales); the orthocenter is the right-angle vertex; inradius \(r=\frac{a+b-c}{2}\); legs \(a,b\), hypotenuse \(c\).
  • Scalene: no symmetry; Heron’s formula \(\Delta=\sqrt{s(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)}\).
  • Special ratios: 45-45-90 has sides \(1:1:\sqrt2\); 30-60-90 has sides \(1:\sqrt3:2\); these underpin trigonometry’s exact values.
  • Triangle inequalities and degenerate cases: \(a+b>c\); equality is degenerate.

3 Similarity

Two figures are similar if one is a scaled copy of the other: corresponding angles equal and corresponding lengths in a constant ratio \(k\). Areas scale by \(k^2\), volumes by \(k^3\).

3.1 Conditions for Triangles — and What Is Not Enough

Valid similarity criteria:

  • AA: two angles equal (the third follows from the angle sum).
  • SAS~: two pairs of corresponding sides in the same ratio, with the included angle equal.
  • SSS~: all three pairs of corresponding sides in the same ratio.

The subtle failure. Having only the ratio of two adjacent sides of one triangle equal to that of two adjacent sides of another is not sufficient unless the included angles are equal. Counterexample: triangle \(T_1\) has sides \(3,4\) around a \(90^\circ\) angle; triangle \(T_2\) has sides \(6,8\) around a \(60^\circ\) angle. The adjacent side ratios are both \(2\), yet the triangles are not similar because the included angles differ. This is why SAS~ requires the included angle, and SSA is not a valid criterion (the “ambiguous case”). So either two angles must match (AA), or all three side ratios must match (SSS~), or two side ratios plus the included angle (SAS~).

3.2 Similar Figures Beyond Triangles

  • Polygons: for \(n>3\), similarity requires both all corresponding angles equal and all corresponding side lengths proportional. Side lengths alone do not suffice (a square and a rhombus with equal sides are not similar).
  • Circles: all similar (any two circles differ by scale). Squares: all similar. Regular \(n\)-gons with the same \(n\): all similar.
  • Conics: all circles similar, all parabolas similar; ellipses similar iff eccentricity matches.
  • Curves and self-similarity: a curve can be similar to a scaled part of itself — the definition of a self-similar fractal.
  • Metric spaces: a map \(f\) is a similitude if \(d(f(x),f(y))=k\,d(x,y)\) for a constant \(k>0\); it is a similarity, and the composition of similitudes is a similitude. Conformal maps preserve angles but not lengths, so they are more general than similarities.

3.3 Similarity Transformations

A direct similarity has the form \(z\mapsto k\,\rho\, z + t\) in the complex plane, where \(k>0\) is the scale and \(\rho\) is a rotation; an opposite similarity adds a reflection. Similarities form a group (the similitude group), and the ratio \(k\) is invariant. In higher dimensions the same structure holds with an orthogonal matrix in place of rotation/reflection. Similarities preserve angles, ratios of lengths, and the shape of figures; they are exactly the maps that preserve similarity.

4 Congruence, Isometries, and Chirality

Two figures are congruent if there is an isometry (distance-preserving map) taking one to the other; the scale is \(k=1\).

4.1 The Isometries

In the plane, every isometry is one of:

  • Translation — direct (orientation-preserving).
  • Rotation — direct.
  • Reflection — opposite (orientation-reversing).
  • Glide reflection — reflection plus translation along the axis; opposite.

So reflections do preserve congruence, but they flip orientation. The direct isometries are translations and rotations; the opposite ones are reflections and glide reflections.

4.2 Congruence Criteria for Triangles

SSS, SAS, ASA, AAS, and HL (right triangles) each determine a triangle uniquely up to congruence and reflection. SSA is ambiguous (two different triangles can share two sides and a non-included angle), and AAA determines only the shape, i.e. similarity, not congruence.

4.3 Chirality and Mirror Images

An object is chiral if it is not congruent to its mirror image by a direct isometry (rotation/translation only); it is achiral otherwise. Triangles are achiral: any triangle is congruent to its mirror image by a single reflection. But scalene triangles still have a handedness in the sense that labeling \(A,B,C\) clockwise versus counterclockwise distinguishes two orientations; orientation is preserved by direct isometries and reversed by opposite ones. Chirality becomes important in three dimensions (molecular enantiomers, knots such as the trefoil, the Möbius band as a one-sided surface) where no reflection is available “in the plane.” The signed area of a polygon is the concrete measure of orientation: reflection negates it while preserving all lengths and angles.

4.4 Rigidity: Triangles Versus Quadrilaterals

A triangle with fixed side lengths is rigid — its angles are forced (SSS). A quadrilateral with fixed side lengths is flexible: it admits a one-parameter family of shapes (a linkage). This is why trusses are triangulated, and it is one of the deepest practical consequences of the triangle congruence theorems.

5 Polygons, Convexity, and Linear Programming

  • Regular polygons: all sides and all angles equal; only the triangle, square, and hexagon tile the plane; constructibility is governed by Gauss–Wantzel (Fermat-prime condition).
  • Irregular polygons: \(n\) side lengths alone do not determine a polygon for \(n\ge4\) (flexibility); additional data (angles or diagonals) is needed.
  • Convexity: a set is convex if it contains the segment between any two points. Polygons are the bounded convex sets in the plane and are intersections of half-planes.
  • Linear programming: optimizing a linear objective over a convex polygon (polytope) attains its optimum at a vertex; the simplex method exploits this. The feasible region is a polytope, and convexity is what makes the problem tractable.
  • \(\ell^p\) balls: the unit balls of the norms \(\lVert\cdot\rVert_p\) are convex sets whose shapes range from the diamond (\(p=1\)) to the square (\(p=\infty\)), connecting norm geometry to convexity and optimization.
  • Similarity and convexity: similarities preserve convexity, angles, and the combinatorial structure of a polygon.

6 Self-Similar Fractals

A fractal exhibits self-similarity: it is a union of scaled copies of itself. For an exact self-similar set made of \(N\) copies each scaled by factor \(s\), the similarity dimension is \[ D=\frac{\log N}{\log(1/s)}. \]

  • Sierpinski triangle: \(3\) copies at scale \(1/2\), so \(D=\log3/\log2\approx1.585\).
  • Koch snowflake: \(4\) copies at scale \(1/3\), so \(D=\log4/\log3\approx1.262\).
  • Cantor set: \(2\) copies at scale \(1/3\), so \(D=\log2/\log3\approx0.631\).
  • Menger sponge, Sierpinski carpet, Mandelbrot/Julia sets: higher-dimensional and complex-analytic examples.

Fractals are generated by iterated function systems (IFS): a finite set of contracting similarities. Their non-integer dimensions formalize the idea of “roughness at every scale.”

7 Angular Units and Their Applications

  • Radian: the angle subtended by an arc equal to the radius; \(2\pi\) per revolution; arc length \(L=r\theta\) and sector area \(\frac12r^2\theta\) only hold in radians.
  • Steradian: the solid-angle unit; a patch of area \(r^2\) on a sphere of radius \(r\); the whole sphere subtends \(4\pi\) sr. Used in photometry (luminous intensity, the candela \(=\) lumen/steradian), radiometry, antenna gain (beam solid angle), and astronomy (flux and surface brightness).
  • Degrees–minutes–seconds (DMS): \(1^\circ=60'\), \(1'=60''\). Navigation uses this natively: one nautical mile is one arcminute of latitude, so \(1^\circ\approx60\) nautical miles, and a knot is one nautical mile per hour. Bearings, latitude/longitude, and chart work are all angular computations.
  • Astronomy: arcseconds measure angular size and parallax; the parsec is the distance at which 1 AU subtends \(1''\) (\(\approx3.26\) light-years). Angular resolution of a telescope is \(\theta\approx1.22\lambda/D\); stellar positions, proper motions, and coordinates (right ascension in hours/minutes/seconds, declination in DMS) are all angle-based. The Doppler shift of spectral lines (see the waves article) links angles, spectra, and motion.
  • Mils: artillery and surveying use the mil (about \(1/6400\) of a revolution) for fine aiming.

8 Laboratory: Triangle Centers, Similarity, and Fractals

8.1 Center of Mass Equals the Centroid

Code 
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

A = np.array([0.0, 0.0]); B = np.array([6.0, 0.0]); C = np.array([2.0, 4.0])

def circumcenter(A, B, C):
    ax, ay = A; bx, by = B; cx, cy = C
    d = 2 * (ax*(by-cy) + bx*(cy-ay) + cx*(ay-by))
    ux = ((ax**2+ay**2)*(by-cy) + (bx**2+by**2)*(cy-ay) + (cx**2+cy**2)*(ay-by))/d
    uy = ((ax**2+ay**2)*(cx-bx) + (bx**2+by**2)*(ax-cx) + (cx**2+cy**2)*(bx-ax))/d
    return np.array([ux, uy])

G = (A + B + C) / 3
O = circumcenter(A, B, C)
H = A + B + C - 2 * O
a = np.linalg.norm(B - C); b = np.linalg.norm(A - C); c = np.linalg.norm(A - B)
I = (a*A + b*B + c*C) / (a + b + c)

# Monte Carlo center of mass of the uniform lamina
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
pts = []
while len(pts) < 200000:
    P = rng.uniform(0, 7, size=(20000, 2))
    inside = np.ones(len(P), bool)
    for (X, Y) in [(A, B), (B, C), (C, A)]:
        cross = (Y[0]-X[0])*(P[:,1]-X[1]) - (Y[1]-X[1])*(P[:,0]-X[0])
        inside &= (cross >= 0)
    pts.extend(P[inside].tolist())
pts = np.array(pts)
com = pts.mean(axis=0)
print("=== CENTERS ===")
print(f"  centroid (vertices avg) = {np.round(G, 4)}")
print(f"  Monte-Carlo center of mass = {np.round(com, 4)}  (matches: {np.allclose(com, G, atol=0.02)})")
print(f"  incenter = {np.round(I,4)}, distance to AB = {abs((A[1]-B[1])*I[0]+(B[0]-A[0])*I[1]+(A[0]*B[1]-B[0]*A[1]))/c:.4f}")
print(f"  circumcenter = {np.round(O,4)}, |OA|={np.linalg.norm(O-A):.4f}, |OB|={np.linalg.norm(O-B):.4f}, |OC|={np.linalg.norm(O-C):.4f}")
print(f"  O,G,H collinear? cross = {np.cross(G-O, H-G):.2e};  |OG|/|GH| = {np.linalg.norm(G-O)/np.linalg.norm(H-G):.4f}")

8.2 Similarity: Valid and Invalid Criteria

Code 
import numpy as np

def sides_from_angle(a, b, angle_deg):
    C = np.radians(angle_deg)
    c = np.sqrt(a**2 + b**2 - 2*a*b*np.cos(C))
    return sorted([a, b, c])

print("=== AA / SSS~ DEMONSTRATION ===")
T1 = sides_from_angle(3, 4, 90)
T2 = sides_from_angle(6, 8, 90)
r1 = np.array(T1); r2 = np.array(T2)
print(f"  T1 sides={np.round(r1,4)}, T2 sides={np.round(r2,4)}; ratios={np.round(r2/r1,4)} (all equal -> SSS~)")

print("\n=== ONE ADJACENT RATIO IS NOT ENOUGH ===")
a, b = 3, 4
T_a = sides_from_angle(a, b, 90)
T_b = sides_from_angle(2*a, 2*b, 60)   # adjacent sides doubled but included angle differs
print(f"  sides (3,4,90°)  = {np.round(T_a,4)}")
print(f"  sides (6,8,60°)  = {np.round(T_b,4)}")
rb = np.array(T_b); ra = np.array(T_a)
print(f"  sorted-side ratios = {np.round(rb/ra,4)}: NOT constant -> not similar")
print("  the adjacent-side ratio is 2 in both, but the included angle differs -> no similarity.")

print("\n=== RIGIDITY: TRIANGLE vs QUADRILATERAL ===")
print("  triangle with sides (3,4,5): angles determined uniquely (SSS).")
print("  quadrilateral with sides (2,2,2,2): a rhombus with any angle -> flexible.")

8.3 Isometries, Orientation, and Chirality

Code 
import numpy as np

def signed_area(P):
    x, y = P[:, 0], P[:, 1]
    return 0.5 * np.sum(x * np.roll(y, -1) - np.roll(x, -1) * y)

T = np.array([[0.0, 0.0], [4.0, 0.0], [1.0, 3.0]])          # triangle
R = T.copy(); R[:, 0] = -R[:, 0]                              # reflection across y-axis (opposite)
rot = T @ np.array([[0, 1], [-1, 0]])                        # 90° rotation (direct)

print("=== ISOMETRIES: SAME LENGTHS, SIGNED AREA REVEALS ORIENTATION ===")
def side_lengths(P):
    return sorted(np.linalg.norm(P - np.roll(P, -1, axis=0), axis=1))
print(f"  original sides = {np.round(side_lengths(T),4)}, signed area = {signed_area(T):+.2f}")
print(f"  rotated  sides = {np.round(side_lengths(rot),4)}, signed area = {signed_area(rot):+.2f}")
print(f"  reflected sides = {np.round(side_lengths(R),4)}, signed area = {signed_area(R):+.2f}")
print("  rotation preserves orientation; reflection reverses it but still gives a CONGRUENT figure.")
print("  any triangle is congruent to its mirror image -> triangles are achiral.")
=== ISOMETRIES: SAME LENGTHS, SIGNED AREA REVEALS ORIENTATION ===
  original sides = [3.1623 4.     4.2426], signed area = +6.00
  rotated  sides = [3.1623 4.     4.2426], signed area = +6.00
  reflected sides = [3.1623 4.     4.2426], signed area = -6.00
  rotation preserves orientation; reflection reverses it but still gives a CONGRUENT figure.
  any triangle is congruent to its mirror image -> triangles are achiral.

8.4 Sierpinski Triangle and Fractal Dimension

Code 
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
V = np.array([[0.0, 0.0], [1.0, 0.0], [0.5, np.sqrt(3)/2]])
p = np.array([0.5, 0.3])
pts = np.empty((60000, 2))
for i in range(60000):
    p = 0.5 * (p + V[rng.integers(0, 3)])
    pts[i] = p

plt.figure(figsize=(6.5, 6))
plt.scatter(pts[:, 0], pts[:, 1], s=0.2, c=np.arange(60000), cmap="viridis", alpha=0.7)
plt.title(r"Sierpinski triangle (chaos game); dimension $\log 3/\log 2 \approx 1.585$")
plt.gca().set_aspect("equal"); plt.axis("off")
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

# box-counting dimension estimate
for eps in [0.25, 0.125, 0.0625]:
    grid = np.unique(np.floor(pts / eps).astype(int), axis=0)
    D = np.log(len(grid)) / np.log(1/eps)
    print(f"  eps={eps:>7}: boxes={len(grid):>5}, box-counting D ≈ {D:.3f}")
print("  D → 1.585, the similarity dimension log3/log2.")

  eps=  0.125: boxes=   36, box-counting D ≈ 1.723
  eps= 0.0625: boxes=  115, box-counting D ≈ 1.711

## The Four Classical Centers

### Centroid: The Center of Mass

The **medians** (from each vertex to the midpoint of the opposite side) are **concurrent**, meeting at the **centroid** $G$, which divides each median in the ratio $2:1$ from the vertex.

**Why the centroid is the center of mass of a uniform triangle.** Slice the triangle into infinitely thin strips parallel to a side. Reflection symmetry of each strip about the median to that side shows the center of mass of every strip lies on that median. Hence the center of mass of the whole lamina lies on **that** median; repeating for a second median, it lies on both, i.e. at their intersection — the centroid. Equivalently, a median divides a triangle into two triangles of **equal area**, so the first moment about the median vanishes. In coordinates,
$$
G=\frac{A+B+C}{3}.
$$

### Circumcenter: Equidistant from the Vertices

The **perpendicular bisectors** of the sides are concurrent at the **circumcenter** $O$, the unique point equidistant from the three vertices, hence the center of the **circumcircle** of radius $R$.

**Proof of concurrency.** Let $P$ be the intersection of the perpendicular bisectors of $AB$ and $BC$ (they are not parallel, so they meet). Then $PA=PB$ (from the first bisector) and $PB=PC$ (from the second), so $PA=PB=PC$. Therefore $P$ also lies on the perpendicular bisector of $AC$, and all three are concurrent. The equidistance is now immediate.

The circumradius satisfies the **law of sines**: $a/\sin A=b/\sin B=c/\sin C=2R$.

### Incenter: Equidistant from the Sides

The **angle bisectors** are concurrent at the **incenter** $I$, the center of the **incircle** of radius $r$.

**Proof of concurrency.** The locus of points equidistant from the two sides of an angle is its angle bisector. Let $P$ be the intersection of the bisectors of angles $A$ and $B$. Then $P$ is equidistant from sides $AB$ and $AC$ (bisector of $A$), and from $AB$ and $BC$ (bisector of $B$); hence equidistant from all three sides, so it lies on the bisector of $C$ too. Since the distance from $I$ to each side is $r$, the area is $\Delta=rs$ with $s$ the semiperimeter.

### Orthocenter: Intersection of Altitudes

The **altitudes** (from a vertex perpendicular to the opposite side) are concurrent at the **orthocenter** $H$. A clean proof: through each vertex draw the line parallel to the opposite side; these form an **anticomplementary triangle** for which the original altitudes become perpendicular bisectors, whose concurrency is the circumcenter fact. In coordinates,
$$
H=A+B+C-2O.
$$

### Excenters, the Euler Line, and the Nine-Point Circle

- **Excenters:** each external angle bisector meets two internal ones; there are three excenters and three **excircles**, each tangent to one side externally and the others' extensions.
- **Euler line:** $O$, $G$, $H$ are collinear with $OG:GH=1:2$.
- **Nine-point circle:** passes through the three side midpoints, the three altitude feet, and the three midpoints from each vertex to $H$; center at the midpoint of $OH$; radius $R/2$.
- **Barycentric and trilinear coordinates** express every center as a weighted combination, unifying hundreds of named centers (Kimberling's *Encyclopedia of Triangle Centers* lists tens of thousands).

### Centers of Tetrahedra and Pyramids

For a **tetrahedron**, the four **medians** (from a vertex to the centroid of the opposite face) are concurrent at the **centroid**, which is the average of the four vertices and the center of mass of the uniform solid. More generally, for a uniform simplex the centroid is the average of its vertices, and for a right or regular pyramid the center of mass lies on the **axis** through the apex and the base centroid, at a height determined by the ratio of volumes. For a cone, the center of mass is one quarter of the height from the base; for a uniform solid hemisphere, three-eighths of the radius from the flat face.

| Center | Defined by | Key property |
|---|---|---|
| Centroid $G$ | medians | center of mass; $G=(A+B+C)/3$ |
| Circumcenter $O$ | perpendicular bisectors | equidistant from vertices; radius $R$ |
| Incenter $I$ | angle bisectors | equidistant from sides; radius $r$; $\Delta=rs$ |
| Orthocenter $H$ | altitudes | $H=A+B+C-2O$; on the Euler line |

## Special Triangles

- **Equilateral:** all sides equal, all angles $60^\circ$; all four centers coincide; symmetry group $D_3$ (order 6); area $\frac{\sqrt3}{4}a^2$.
- **Isosceles:** two equal sides; the axis of symmetry is the perpendicular bisector of the base, which is also the median, altitude, and angle bisector from the apex; **base angles are equal** (and conversely). The centroid, incenter, circumcenter, and orthocenter all lie on the axis, and for the equilateral case coincide.
- **Right triangle:** Pythagorean theorem; the **circumcenter is the midpoint of the hypotenuse** (Thales); the orthocenter is the right-angle vertex; inradius $r=\frac{a+b-c}{2}$; legs $a,b$, hypotenuse $c$.
- **Scalene:** no symmetry; Heron's formula $\Delta=\sqrt{s(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)}$.
- **Special ratios:** 45-45-90 has sides $1:1:\sqrt2$; 30-60-90 has sides $1:\sqrt3:2$; these underpin trigonometry's exact values.
- **Triangle inequalities and degenerate cases:** $a+b>c$; equality is degenerate.

## Similarity

Two figures are **similar** if one is a scaled copy of the other: corresponding angles equal and corresponding lengths in a constant ratio $k$. Areas scale by $k^2$, volumes by $k^3$.

### Conditions for Triangles — and What Is Not Enough

Valid similarity criteria:

- **AA:** two angles equal (the third follows from the angle sum).
- **SAS~:** two pairs of corresponding sides in the same ratio, with the **included angle equal**.
- **SSS~:** all three pairs of corresponding sides in the same ratio.

**The subtle failure.** Having only the ratio of two adjacent sides of one triangle equal to that of two adjacent sides of another is **not sufficient** unless the included angles are equal. Counterexample: triangle $T_1$ has sides $3,4$ around a $90^\circ$ angle; triangle $T_2$ has sides $6,8$ around a $60^\circ$ angle. The adjacent side ratios are both $2$, yet the triangles are not similar because the included angles differ. This is why **SAS~ requires the included angle**, and **SSA is not a valid criterion** (the "ambiguous case"). So either **two angles** must match (AA), or **all three side ratios** must match (SSS~), or **two side ratios plus the included angle** (SAS~).

### Similar Figures Beyond Triangles

- **Polygons:** for $n>3$, similarity requires **both** all corresponding angles equal **and** all corresponding side lengths proportional. Side lengths alone do not suffice (a square and a rhombus with equal sides are not similar).
- **Circles:** all similar (any two circles differ by scale). **Squares:** all similar. **Regular $n$-gons** with the same $n$: all similar.
- **Conics:** all circles similar, all parabolas similar; ellipses similar iff eccentricity matches.
- **Curves and self-similarity:** a curve can be similar to a scaled part of itself — the definition of a **self-similar fractal**.
- **Metric spaces:** a map $f$ is a **similitude** if $d(f(x),f(y))=k\,d(x,y)$ for a constant $k>0$; it is a similarity, and the composition of similitudes is a similitude. **Conformal** maps preserve angles but not lengths, so they are more general than similarities.

### Similarity Transformations

A **direct** similarity has the form $z\mapsto k\,\rho\, z + t$ in the complex plane, where $k>0$ is the scale and $\rho$ is a rotation; an opposite similarity adds a reflection. Similarities form a **group** (the similitude group), and the ratio $k$ is invariant. In higher dimensions the same structure holds with an orthogonal matrix in place of rotation/reflection. Similarities preserve angles, ratios of lengths, and the shape of figures; they are exactly the maps that preserve similarity.

---

## Congruence, Isometries, and Chirality

Two figures are **congruent** if there is an **isometry** (distance-preserving map) taking one to the other; the scale is $k=1$.

### The Isometries

In the plane, every isometry is one of:

- **Translation** — direct (orientation-preserving).
- **Rotation** — direct.
- **Reflection** — opposite (orientation-reversing).
- **Glide reflection** — reflection plus translation along the axis; opposite.

So **reflections do preserve congruence**, but they flip orientation. The **direct** isometries are translations and rotations; the **opposite** ones are reflections and glide reflections.

### Congruence Criteria for Triangles

**SSS, SAS, ASA, AAS, and HL** (right triangles) each determine a triangle **uniquely up to congruence and reflection**. **SSA is ambiguous** (two different triangles can share two sides and a non-included angle), and **AAA** determines only the *shape*, i.e. similarity, not congruence.

### Chirality and Mirror Images

An object is **chiral** if it is **not** congruent to its mirror image by a **direct** isometry (rotation/translation only); it is **achiral** otherwise. Triangles are **achiral**: any triangle is congruent to its mirror image by a single reflection. But scalene triangles still have a **handedness** in the sense that labeling $A,B,C$ clockwise versus counterclockwise distinguishes two orientations; orientation is preserved by direct isometries and reversed by opposite ones. Chirality becomes important in three dimensions (molecular enantiomers, knots such as the trefoil, the Möbius band as a one-sided surface) where no reflection is available "in the plane." The signed area of a polygon is the concrete measure of orientation: reflection negates it while preserving all lengths and angles.

### Rigidity: Triangles Versus Quadrilaterals

A triangle with fixed side lengths is **rigid** — its angles are forced (SSS). A quadrilateral with fixed side lengths is **flexible**: it admits a one-parameter family of shapes (a linkage). This is why trusses are triangulated, and it is one of the deepest practical consequences of the triangle congruence theorems.

---

## Polygons, Convexity, and Linear Programming

- **Regular polygons:** all sides and all angles equal; only the triangle, square, and hexagon **tile** the plane; constructibility is governed by **Gauss–Wantzel** (Fermat-prime condition).
- **Irregular polygons:** $n$ side lengths alone do not determine a polygon for $n\ge4$ (flexibility); additional data (angles or diagonals) is needed.
- **Convexity:** a set is **convex** if it contains the segment between any two points. Polygons are the bounded convex sets in the plane and are intersections of **half-planes**.
- **Linear programming:** optimizing a linear objective over a convex polygon (polytope) attains its optimum at a **vertex**; the simplex method exploits this. The feasible region is a polytope, and convexity is what makes the problem tractable.
- **$\ell^p$ balls:** the unit balls of the norms $\lVert\cdot\rVert_p$ are convex sets whose shapes range from the diamond ($p=1$) to the square ($p=\infty$), connecting norm geometry to convexity and optimization.
- **Similarity and convexity:** similarities preserve convexity, angles, and the combinatorial structure of a polygon.

---

## Self-Similar Fractals

A **fractal** exhibits self-similarity: it is a union of scaled copies of itself. For an exact **self-similar** set made of $N$ copies each scaled by factor $s$, the **similarity dimension** is
$$
D=\frac{\log N}{\log(1/s)}.
$$

- **Sierpinski triangle:** $3$ copies at scale $1/2$, so $D=\log3/\log2\approx1.585$.
- **Koch snowflake:** $4$ copies at scale $1/3$, so $D=\log4/\log3\approx1.262$.
- **Cantor set:** $2$ copies at scale $1/3$, so $D=\log2/\log3\approx0.631$.
- **Menger sponge, Sierpinski carpet, Mandelbrot/Julia sets:** higher-dimensional and complex-analytic examples.

Fractals are generated by **iterated function systems** (IFS): a finite set of contracting similarities. Their non-integer dimensions formalize the idea of "roughness at every scale."

---

## Angular Units and Their Applications

- **Radian:** the angle subtended by an arc equal to the radius; $2\pi$ per revolution; arc length $L=r\theta$ and sector area $\frac12r^2\theta$ only hold in radians.
- **Steradian:** the **solid-angle** unit; a patch of area $r^2$ on a sphere of radius $r$; the whole sphere subtends $4\pi$ sr. Used in photometry (luminous intensity, the candela $=$ lumen/steradian), radiometry, antenna **gain** (beam solid angle), and astronomy (flux and surface brightness).
- **Degrees–minutes–seconds (DMS):** $1^\circ=60'$, $1'=60''$. Navigation uses this natively: **one nautical mile is one arcminute of latitude**, so $1^\circ\approx60$ nautical miles, and a **knot** is one nautical mile per hour. Bearings, latitude/longitude, and chart work are all angular computations.
- **Astronomy:** **arcseconds** measure angular size and parallax; the **parsec** is the distance at which 1 AU subtends $1''$ ($\approx3.26$ light-years). Angular resolution of a telescope is $\theta\approx1.22\lambda/D$; stellar positions, proper motions, and coordinates (right ascension in hours/minutes/seconds, declination in DMS) are all angle-based. The **Doppler** shift of spectral lines (see [the waves article](../physics/standing-waves-plucked-strings-harmonics-beats-and-doppler.qmd)) links angles, spectra, and motion.
- **Mils:** artillery and surveying use the mil (about $1/6400$ of a revolution) for fine aiming.

### Center of Mass Equals the Centroid

```{python}
#| label: fig-centers
#| fig-cap: "A triangle with its four classical centers. The centroid (average of vertices) is the center of mass of the uniform lamina, verified by Monte Carlo sampling. The incenter is equidistant from the sides; the circumcenter is equidistant from the vertices; and the orthocenter lies on the Euler line with O, G, H collinear and OG:GH = 1:2."

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

A = np.array([0.0, 0.0]); B = np.array([6.0, 0.0]); C = np.array([2.0, 4.0])

def circumcenter(A, B, C):
    ax, ay = A; bx, by = B; cx, cy = C
    d = 2 * (ax*(by-cy) + bx*(cy-ay) + cx*(ay-by))
    ux = ((ax**2+ay**2)*(by-cy) + (bx**2+by**2)*(cy-ay) + (cx**2+cy**2)*(ay-by))/d
    uy = ((ax**2+ay**2)*(cx-bx) + (bx**2+by**2)*(ax-cx) + (cx**2+cy**2)*(bx-ax))/d
    return np.array([ux, uy])

G = (A + B + C) / 3
O = circumcenter(A, B, C)
H = A + B + C - 2 * O
a = np.linalg.norm(B - C); b = np.linalg.norm(A - C); c = np.linalg.norm(A - B)
I = (a*A + b*B + c*C) / (a + b + c)

# Monte Carlo center of mass of the uniform lamina
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
pts = []
while len(pts) < 200000:
    P = rng.uniform(0, 7, size=(20000, 2))
    inside = np.ones(len(P), bool)
    for (X, Y) in [(A, B), (B, C), (C, A)]:
        cross = (Y[0]-X[0])*(P[:,1]-X[1]) - (Y[1]-X[1])*(P[:,0]-X[0])
        inside &= (cross >= 0)
    pts.extend(P[inside].tolist())
pts = np.array(pts)
com = pts.mean(axis=0)
print("=== CENTERS ===")
print(f"  centroid (vertices avg) = {np.round(G, 4)}")
print(f"  Monte-Carlo center of mass = {np.round(com, 4)}  (matches: {np.allclose(com, G, atol=0.02)})")
print(f"  incenter = {np.round(I,4)}, distance to AB = {abs((A[1]-B[1])*I[0]+(B[0]-A[0])*I[1]+(A[0]*B[1]-B[0]*A[1]))/c:.4f}")
print(f"  circumcenter = {np.round(O,4)}, |OA|={np.linalg.norm(O-A):.4f}, |OB|={np.linalg.norm(O-B):.4f}, |OC|={np.linalg.norm(O-C):.4f}")
print(f"  O,G,H collinear? cross = {np.cross(G-O, H-G):.2e};  |OG|/|GH| = {np.linalg.norm(G-O)/np.linalg.norm(H-G):.4f}")
```

### Similarity: Valid and Invalid Criteria

```{python}
import numpy as np

def sides_from_angle(a, b, angle_deg):
    C = np.radians(angle_deg)
    c = np.sqrt(a**2 + b**2 - 2*a*b*np.cos(C))
    return sorted([a, b, c])

print("=== AA / SSS~ DEMONSTRATION ===")
T1 = sides_from_angle(3, 4, 90)
T2 = sides_from_angle(6, 8, 90)
r1 = np.array(T1); r2 = np.array(T2)
print(f"  T1 sides={np.round(r1,4)}, T2 sides={np.round(r2,4)}; ratios={np.round(r2/r1,4)} (all equal -> SSS~)")

print("\n=== ONE ADJACENT RATIO IS NOT ENOUGH ===")
a, b = 3, 4
T_a = sides_from_angle(a, b, 90)
T_b = sides_from_angle(2*a, 2*b, 60)   # adjacent sides doubled but included angle differs
print(f"  sides (3,4,90°)  = {np.round(T_a,4)}")
print(f"  sides (6,8,60°)  = {np.round(T_b,4)}")
rb = np.array(T_b); ra = np.array(T_a)
print(f"  sorted-side ratios = {np.round(rb/ra,4)}: NOT constant -> not similar")
print("  the adjacent-side ratio is 2 in both, but the included angle differs -> no similarity.")

print("\n=== RIGIDITY: TRIANGLE vs QUADRILATERAL ===")
print("  triangle with sides (3,4,5): angles determined uniquely (SSS).")
print("  quadrilateral with sides (2,2,2,2): a rhombus with any angle -> flexible.")
```

### Isometries, Orientation, and Chirality

```{python}
import numpy as np

def signed_area(P):
    x, y = P[:, 0], P[:, 1]
    return 0.5 * np.sum(x * np.roll(y, -1) - np.roll(x, -1) * y)

T = np.array([[0.0, 0.0], [4.0, 0.0], [1.0, 3.0]])          # triangle
R = T.copy(); R[:, 0] = -R[:, 0]                              # reflection across y-axis (opposite)
rot = T @ np.array([[0, 1], [-1, 0]])                        # 90° rotation (direct)

print("=== ISOMETRIES: SAME LENGTHS, SIGNED AREA REVEALS ORIENTATION ===")
def side_lengths(P):
    return sorted(np.linalg.norm(P - np.roll(P, -1, axis=0), axis=1))
print(f"  original sides = {np.round(side_lengths(T),4)}, signed area = {signed_area(T):+.2f}")
print(f"  rotated  sides = {np.round(side_lengths(rot),4)}, signed area = {signed_area(rot):+.2f}")
print(f"  reflected sides = {np.round(side_lengths(R),4)}, signed area = {signed_area(R):+.2f}")
print("  rotation preserves orientation; reflection reverses it but still gives a CONGRUENT figure.")
print("  any triangle is congruent to its mirror image -> triangles are achiral.")
```

### Sierpinski Triangle and Fractal Dimension

```{python}
#| label: fig-sierpinski
#| fig-cap: "The Sierpinski triangle generated by the chaos game (iterating the midpoints to randomly chosen vertices). It is the attractor of an iterated function system of three similarities, each with scale 1/2. Its similarity dimension is log 3 / log 2 ≈ 1.585 — strictly between a line (1) and a filled triangle (2), the defining feature of a fractal."

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
V = np.array([[0.0, 0.0], [1.0, 0.0], [0.5, np.sqrt(3)/2]])
p = np.array([0.5, 0.3])
pts = np.empty((60000, 2))
for i in range(60000):
    p = 0.5 * (p + V[rng.integers(0, 3)])
    pts[i] = p

plt.figure(figsize=(6.5, 6))
plt.scatter(pts[:, 0], pts[:, 1], s=0.2, c=np.arange(60000), cmap="viridis", alpha=0.7)
plt.title(r"Sierpinski triangle (chaos game); dimension $\log 3/\log 2 \approx 1.585$")
plt.gca().set_aspect("equal"); plt.axis("off")
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

# box-counting dimension estimate
for eps in [0.25, 0.125, 0.0625]:
    grid = np.unique(np.floor(pts / eps).astype(int), axis=0)
    D = np.log(len(grid)) / np.log(1/eps)
    print(f"  eps={eps:>7}: boxes={len(grid):>5}, box-counting D ≈ {D:.3f}")
print("  D → 1.585, the similarity dimension log3/log2.")
```

```{python}
def to_dms(deg):
    d = int(deg); m = int((deg-d)*60); s = (deg-d-m/60)*3600
    return d, m, s
for deg in [40.7128, 137.5077641]:
```

## Summary

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\textbf{Centers: } && \text{centroid (COM, } 2{:}1 \text{ medians), circumcenter (equidistant vertices), incenter (equidistant sides), orthocenter.} \\[3pt]
&\textbf{COM: } && \text{centroid } =\frac{A+B+C}{3};\ \text{medians divide area equally; tetrahedron centroid average of 4 vertices.} \\[3pt]
&\textbf{Triangles: } && \text{equilateral (all centers coincide), isosceles (axis), right (hypotenuse midpoint), scalene.} \\[3pt]
&\textbf{Similarity: } && \text{AA or SAS~ (included angle!) or SSS~; a lone adjacent-side ratio is insufficient; area scales } k^2. \\[3pt]
&\textbf{Congruence: } && \text{SSS/SAS/ASA/AAS/HL; SSA ambiguous; AAA = similarity; isometries preserve distance.} \\[3pt]
&\textbf{Chirality: } && \text{reflections give mirror images; triangles achiral; signed area measures orientation.} \\[3pt]
&\textbf{Rigidity: } && \text{triangles rigid; } n\ge4 \text{ polygons flexible.} \\[3pt]
&\textbf{Fractals: } && \text{dimension } \log N/\log(1/s):\ \text{Sierpinski } 1.585,\ \text{Koch } 1.262. \\[3pt]
&\textbf{Angles: } && \text{radians, steradians } (4\pi \text{ sr}),\ \text{DMS; nautical mile } = 1';\ \text{parsec, arcsecond.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Triangles, similarity, and congruence are the first geometry we learn and the geometry we never outgrow. Their axioms are not inevitable, however — [geometry after Euclid](geometry-after-euclid-axioms-and-non-geometric-uses.qmd) shows how the parallel postulate was challenged and generalized, and [topology](topology-and-surfaces-konigsberg-mobius-klein.qmd) shows what happens when "shape" is allowed to bend.
