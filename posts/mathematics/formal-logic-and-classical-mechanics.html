Formal mathematical logic establishes the rigorous machinery through which statements are constructed, manipulated, and evaluated. By decoupling pure syntax (symbolic grammar and proof theory) from semantics (truth valuations, models, and physical states), formal logic provides the common foundation for pure mathematics, computer science compiler theory, and analytical physical theories.

1. Syntax: WFFs, Alphabet, and Formation Rules

Formal logic begins with pure syntax—mechanical symbol manipulation without any prior concept of “truth” or “meaning.”

The Alphabet of a Formal Language \(\mathcal{L}\)

A formal propositional language \(\mathcal{L}\) is defined over an alphabet consisting of three disjoint sets:

  1. Propositional Variables (Atoms): A countably infinite set of atomic propositions: \[\text{Prop} = \{p, q, r, p_1, p_2, p_3, \dots\}\] Atoms represent primitive declarative statements that cannot be further decomposed syntactically (e.g., \(p \equiv \text{"The particle has positive momentum"}\)).

  2. Logical Connectives:

    • Nullary Connectives (Constants): \(\bot\) (falsity / absurdity), \(\top\) (truth).
    • Unary Connective: \(\neg\) (negation).
    • Binary Connectives: \(\wedge\) (conjunction / “and”), \(\vee\) (disjunction / “or”), \(\to\) (material implication / “if… then…”).

  3. Punctuation Symbols: Left and right parentheses \((, )\). These eliminate syntactic ambiguity and uniquely define operator precedence.

Inductive Definition of Well-Formed Formulas (WFFs)

Not every arbitrary sequence of symbols is grammatically valid (e.g., \(p \wedge \to q\) is syntactic gibberish). The set of Well-Formed Formulas, denoted \(\text{WFF}(\mathcal{L})\), is defined inductively:

  • Base Clause: Every propositional atom \(p \in \text{Prop}\) is a WFF (\(p \in \text{WFF}(\mathcal{L})\)). The logical constants \(\bot\) and \(\top\) are WFFs.
  • Inductive Clause: If \(\varphi, \psi \in \text{WFF}(\mathcal{L})\), then:
    • \((\neg \varphi) \in \text{WFF}(\mathcal{L})\)
    • \((\varphi \wedge \psi) \in \text{WFF}(\mathcal{L})\)
    • \((\varphi \vee \psi) \in \text{WFF}(\mathcal{L})\)
    • \((\varphi \to \psi) \in \text{WFF}(\mathcal{L})\)
  • Extremal Clause: Nothing else is a WFF. A string belongs to \(\text{WFF}(\mathcal{L})\) if and only if it can be constructed through a finite number of applications of the Base and Inductive clauses.

Concrete Examples & Syntactic Non-Examples

String Status Syntactic Analysis
\(((p \wedge q) \to (\neg r))\) Valid WFF Built from atoms \(p, q, r \in \text{Prop}\), forming \((p \wedge q)\) and \((\neg r)\), joined by \(\to\).
\((\neg (\bot \vee (p \to q)))\) Valid WFF Valid composite using constant \(\bot\), implication, disjunction, and negation.
\(p \wedge \to q\) ❌ Invalid Binary connective \(\wedge\) followed immediately by binary connective \(\to\); lacks right operand for \(\wedge\) and left operand for \(\to\).
\((\neg \vee p)\) ❌ Invalid Unary connective \(\neg\) applied directly to binary operator \(\vee\).
\(p \wedge q \vee r\) ❌ Ambiguous Missing parentheses; ambiguous between \(((p \wedge q) \vee r)\) and \((p \wedge (q \vee r))\).

Unique Readability & Parse Trees

A fundamental theorem of formal grammar is the Unique Readability Theorem:

Theorem (Unique Readability): Every formula \(\varphi \in \text{WFF}(\mathcal{L})\) satisfies exactly one of the following conditions: 1. \(\varphi\) is an atomic variable \(p \in \text{Prop}\), or \(\bot\), or \(\top\). 2. \(\varphi\) is of the form \((\neg \psi)\) for a unique formula \(\psi\). 3. \(\varphi\) is of the form \((\psi_1 \circ \psi_2)\) for a unique binary connective \(\circ \in \{\wedge, \vee, \to\}\) and unique formulas \(\psi_1, \psi_2\).

This guarantees that every WFF corresponds to a unique rooted parse tree. For example, the formula \(((p \wedge (\neg q)) \to (r \vee \bot))\) has the syntax tree:

          [ → ]
         /     \
      [ ∧ ]    [ ∨ ]
      /   \    /   \
     p   [¬]  r     ⊥
          |
          q

Structural Induction Principle

Because WFFs are defined inductively, any property \(\mathcal{P}\) holds for all formulas \(\varphi \in \text{WFF}(\mathcal{L})\) if: 1. \(\mathcal{P}(p)\) holds for all \(p \in \text{Prop}\), and \(\mathcal{P}(\bot)\), \(\mathcal{P}(\top)\) hold. 2. If \(\mathcal{P}(\varphi)\) holds, then \(\mathcal{P}((\neg \varphi))\) holds. 3. If \(\mathcal{P}(\varphi)\) and \(\mathcal{P}(\psi)\) hold, then \(\mathcal{P}((\varphi \circ \psi))\) holds for all \(\circ \in \{\wedge, \vee, \to\}\).

Example Lemma (Parenthesis Balance): In every formula \(\varphi \in \text{WFF}(\mathcal{L})\), the number of left parentheses \(L(\varphi)\) equals the number of right parentheses \(R(\varphi)\).

Proof by structural induction: * Base: For any atom \(p \in \text{Prop}\), \(L(p) = 0 = R(p)\). For \(\bot, \top\), \(L = 0 = R\). * Inductive (Negation): Let \(\varphi = (\neg \psi)\). Then \(L(\varphi) = 1 + L(\psi)\) and \(R(\varphi) = 1 + R(\psi)\). By hypothesis \(L(\psi) = R(\psi)\), so \(L(\varphi) = R(\varphi)\). * Inductive (Binary): Let \(\varphi = (\psi_1 \circ \psi_2)\). Then \(L(\varphi) = 1 + L(\psi_1) + L(\psi_2)\) and \(R(\varphi) = 1 + R(\psi_1) + R(\psi_2)\). By inductive hypothesis, \(L(\psi_1) = R(\psi_1)\) and \(L(\psi_2) = R(\psi_2)\). Hence \(L(\varphi) = R(\varphi)\). \(\blacksquare\)

2. Deduction Rules: Classical vs. Intuitionistic Logic

In Gerhard Gentzen’s natural deduction system (\(\mathbf{NJ}\) for intuitionistic, \(\mathbf{NK}\) for classical), logical connectives are governed by dual Introduction (\(I\)) and Elimination (\(E\)) rules:

\[ \begin{array}{ccc} \dfrac{\varphi \quad \psi}{\varphi \wedge \psi} \ (\wedge I) & \dfrac{\varphi \wedge \psi}{\varphi} \ (\wedge E_1) & \dfrac{\varphi \wedge \psi}{\psi} \ (\wedge E_2) \\[1.8em] \dfrac{\varphi}{\varphi \vee \psi} \ (\vee I_1) & \dfrac{\psi}{\varphi \vee \psi} \ (\vee I_2) & \dfrac{\varphi \vee \psi \quad \begin{matrix} [\varphi]^u \\ \vdots \\ \chi \end{matrix} \quad \begin{matrix} [\psi]^v \\ \vdots \\ \chi \end{matrix}}{\chi} \ (\vee E)^{u, v} \\[2.2em] \dfrac{\begin{matrix} [\varphi]^u \\ \vdots \\ \psi \end{matrix}}{\varphi \to \psi} \ (\to I)^u & \dfrac{\varphi \to \psi \quad \varphi}{\psi} \ (\to E \text{ / MP}) & \dfrac{\bot}{\varphi} \ (\bot E \text{ / EFQ}) \end{array} \]

Negation is defined constructively as refutation of a proposition leading to absurdity: \[\neg \varphi \equiv \varphi \to \bot\]

Under this definition, Negation Introduction (\(\neg I\)) and Negation Elimination (\(\neg E\)) are special cases of implication rules: \[ \dfrac{\begin{matrix} [\varphi]^u \\ \vdots \\ \bot \end{matrix}}{\neg \varphi} \ (\neg I)^u \qquad\qquad \dfrac{\varphi \quad \neg \varphi}{\bot} \ (\neg E) \]

The Core Divergence: Intuitionism vs. Classical Logic

Intuitionistic Propositional Calculus (\(\mathbf{IPC}\)) consists strictly of the introduction and elimination rules above.

Classical Propositional Calculus (\(\mathbf{CPC}\)) is obtained by appending any one of the following mutually equivalent deductive principles to \(\mathbf{IPC}\):

  1. Law of Excluded Middle (LEM): \(\varphi \vee \neg \varphi\)
  2. Double Negation Elimination (DNE): \(\neg\neg\varphi \to \varphi\)
  3. Classical Reductio Ad Absurdum (RAA): \[ \dfrac{\begin{matrix} [\neg\varphi]^u \\ \vdots \\ \bot \end{matrix}}{\varphi} \ (\text{RAA})^u \]
  4. Peirce’s Law: \(((\varphi \to \psi) \to \varphi) \to \varphi\)

Constructive Refutation vs. Proof by Contradiction

A frequent confusion in mathematical practice is the conflation of two fundamentally different proof techniques:

1. Constructive Refutation (¬I) [Valid Intuitionistically]:
   Assume φ holds ────────> Derive ⊥ ────────> Conclude ¬φ

2. Classical Proof by Contradiction (RAA) [Rejected Intuitionistically]:
   Assume ¬φ holds ───────> Derive ⊥ ────────> Conclude φ
  • Constructive Refutation (\(\neg I\)): Proves that \(\varphi\) is impossible by converting a hypothetical witness of \(\varphi\) into an absurdity \(\bot\). This builds a valid negation.
  • Classical Reductio Ad Absurdum (RAA): Infers that \(\varphi\) must exist purely because assuming its non-existence yields a contradiction. In intuitionism, this only proves \(\neg\neg\varphi\) (that \(\varphi\) is not refutable), but fails to construct a witness or algorithm for \(\varphi\).

The Brouwer–Heyting–Kolmogorov (BHK) Semantics

Under the BHK interpretation, mathematical propositions are computational tasks:

  • A proof of \(\varphi \wedge \psi\) is a pair \(\langle a, b \rangle\) where \(a\) is a proof of \(\varphi\) and \(b\) is a proof of \(\psi\).
  • A proof of \(\varphi \vee \psi\) is a pair \(\langle i, p \rangle\) where \(i \in \{0, 1\}\) explicitly specifies which disjunct holds, and \(p\) proves that disjunct.
  • A proof of \(\varphi \to \psi\) is an executable function/algorithm converting any proof of \(\varphi\) into a proof of \(\psi\).
  • A proof of \(\neg \varphi\) is an algorithm mapping any hypothetical proof of \(\varphi\) to a proof of \(\bot\).
  • There is no proof of \(\bot\).

Under BHK semantics, asserting the Law of Excluded Middle (\(\varphi \vee \neg \varphi\)) asserts a universal decision procedure (a decider for the Halting Problem). Hence, intuitionistic logic strictly preserves the Curry–Howard Isomorphism: proofs are programs, and formulas are types.

Concrete Deduction Tree Examples

Example 1: Modus Tollens (\(\varphi \to \psi, \neg \psi \vdash \neg \varphi\))

Valid intuitionistically and classically: \[ \dfrac{\dfrac{\varphi \to \psi \quad [\varphi]^1}{\psi} \ (\to E) \quad \neg \psi}{\dfrac{\bot}{\neg \varphi} \ (\neg I)^1} \ (\neg E) \]

Example 2: Double Negation Introduction (\(\varphi \vdash \neg\neg\varphi\))

Valid intuitionistically and classically: \[ \dfrac{\varphi \quad [\neg \varphi]^1}{\dfrac{\bot}{\neg\neg \varphi} \ (\neg I)^1} \ (\neg E) \]

Example 3: Classical Derivation of LEM from RAA

To show \(\vdash \varphi \vee \neg \varphi\) using classical RAA: \[ \cfrac{[\neg(\varphi \vee \neg\varphi)]^1 \quad \cfrac{\cfrac{[\varphi]^2}{\varphi \vee \neg\varphi} \ (\vee I_1)}{\bot} \ (\neg E)^2}{\cfrac{\cfrac{\neg\varphi}{\varphi \vee \neg\varphi} \ (\vee I_2) \quad [\neg(\varphi \vee \neg\varphi)]^1}{\cfrac{\bot}{\varphi \vee \neg\varphi} \ (\text{RAA})^1} \ (\neg E)} \]

3. Ex Falso Quodlibet & Material Implication

Ex Falso Quodlibet (\(EFQ\))

The principle Ex Falso Quodlibet (“from falsehood, anything follows”), or the Principle of Explosion, states: \[\bot \vdash \varphi \quad \text{or} \quad \vdash \bot \to \varphi\]

Formal Derivation from Disjunction

Even without taking \(\bot E\) as a primitive axiom, \(EFQ\) follows immediately from Disjunctive Syllogism:

  1. Assume \(\bot\).
  2. By Disjunction Introduction (\(\vee I_1\)), infer \(\bot \vee \varphi\).
  3. We already have \(\neg \bot \equiv \bot \to \bot\) (provable trivially by identity \(\to I\)).
  4. By Disjunctive Syllogism (\(\bot \vee \varphi\) and \(\neg \bot\)), we conclude \(\varphi\).

The “Paradoxes” of Material Implication

In natural language, the conditional statement “If \(P\), then \(Q\)” carries causal, semantic, or temporal relevance: \[\text{"If you heat the copper rod, it expands."} \quad (\text{Physical causal entailment})\]

In formal classical logic, people routinely conflate Material Implication with: * Syntactic Entailment (\(\varphi \vdash \psi\)): There exists a deductive proof transforming \(\varphi\) into \(\psi\). * Semantic Entailment (\(\varphi \models \psi\)): In every model where \(\varphi\) is true, \(\psi\) is true. * Strict Implication (\(\Box(\varphi \to \psi)\)): Modal necessity; in all possible worlds where \(\varphi\) holds, \(\psi\) holds.

Why Classical Logic Enforces Material Implication

Classical propositional logic demands truth functionality: the truth value of a compound formula must depend only on the truth values of its constituents, independent of semantic meaning.

Let \(v(\varphi \to \psi) = f(v(\varphi), v(\psi)) \in \{0, 1\}\):

\(v(\varphi)\) \(v(\psi)\) \(v(\varphi \to \psi)\) Mathematical Necessity
1 1 1 If the premise is true and the conclusion is true, the conditional must hold.
1 0 0 Essential for Modus Ponens (\(\varphi, \varphi \to \psi \vdash \psi\)). A true premise cannot imply a false conclusion.
0 1 1 If we set \(f(0, 1) = 0\), then \(\varphi \to \psi\) would become identical to biconditional equivalence (\(\varphi \leftrightarrow \psi\)).
0 0 1 If we set \(f(0, 0) = 0\), then \(\varphi \to \psi\) would become identical to logical conjunction (\(\varphi \wedge \psi\)).

Therefore, the only definition of \(f: \{0, 1\}^2 \to \{0, 1\}\) that preserves truth functionality while maintaining Modus Ponens is: \[ \varphi \to \psi \equiv (\neg \varphi) \vee \psi \]

This explains why counterintuitive statements such as “If the Moon is made of green cheese, then \(2 + 2 = 5\)” are formally true in classical propositional calculus: the antecedent is false (\(\bot\)), so the implication evaluates to \(\top\).

4. Boolean Algebra: Algebraic Semantics of Classical Logic

While natural deduction provides the syntactic proof theory, Boolean Algebra provides the algebraic semantics of classical logic.

Axiomatic Structure

A Boolean algebra is an algebraic tuple \(\mathcal{B} = \langle B, \wedge, \vee, \neg, 0, 1 \rangle\) such that \(\langle B, \wedge, \vee \rangle\) is a bounded distributive lattice with complements satisfying:

  1. Associativity & Commutativity: \[x \wedge (y \wedge z) = (x \wedge y) \wedge z, \quad x \vee (y \vee z) = (x \vee y) \vee z\] \[x \wedge y = y \wedge x, \quad x \vee y = y \vee x\]
  2. Absorption: \[x \wedge (x \vee y) = x, \quad x \vee (x \wedge y) = x\]
  3. Distributivity: \[x \wedge (y \vee z) = (x \wedge y) \vee (x \wedge z)\] \[x \vee (y \wedge z) = (x \vee y) \wedge (x \vee z)\]
  4. Complementation: \[x \vee (\neg x) = 1, \quad x \wedge (\neg x) = 0\]

The Stone Representation Theorem

Theorem (Stone, 1936): Every Boolean algebra \(\mathcal{B}\) is isomorphic to a field of sets (a collection of subsets of a topological space \(X\) closed under set union \(\cup\), intersection \(\cap\), and complementation \(\setminus\)).

For classical propositional calculus, the algebraic semantic model is the two-element Boolean algebra: \[\mathbf{2} = \langle \{0, 1\}, \wedge, \vee, \neg, 0, 1 \rangle\] A classical truth valuation is a Boolean homomorphism \(v: \text{WFF}(\mathcal{L}) \to \mathbf{2}\) preserving all lattice operations: \[v(\varphi \wedge \psi) = \min(v(\varphi), v(\psi)), \quad v(\varphi \vee \psi) = \max(v(\varphi), v(\psi)), \quad v(\neg \varphi) = 1 - v(\varphi)\]

5. Truth Valuations in Physics: Classical Mechanics

Physical theories evaluate propositions regarding measurable reality by mapping physical states to truth valuations.

Property Classical Mechanics (Phase Space \(\Gamma\))
Physical State Point \(\omega = (\mathbf{q}, \mathbf{p}) \in \Gamma\)
Propositions Measurable Borel subsets \(S \subseteq \Gamma\)
Algebra Complete Boolean \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\mathcal{B}(\Gamma)\) (Distributive)
Logic Classical Propositional Logic (\(\mathbf{CPC}\))
Truth Valuation Global deterministic \(\{0, 1\}\) indicator function

Phase Space Geometry & The Indicator Map

In Hamiltonian classical mechanics, a system of \(N\) particles is defined on a \(6N\)-dimensional symplectic manifold \(\Gamma = \mathbb{R}^{6N}\) with coordinates \(\omega = (\mathbf{q}, \mathbf{p}) = (q_1, \dots, q_{3N}, p_1, \dots, p_{3N})\).

An experimental proposition \(P\) is an assertion about a physical observable \(A: \Gamma \to \mathbb{R}\): \[P \equiv \text{"Measurement of observable } A \text{ yields a value in Borel set } E \subset \mathbb{R}\text{"}\]

The proposition \(P\) corresponds uniquely to the phase space region: \[S_P = \{\omega \in \Gamma \mid A(\omega) \in E\} = A^{-1}(E) \subseteq \Gamma\]

Concrete Example: One-Dimensional Harmonic Oscillator

Consider a harmonic oscillator with mass \(m\) and spring constant \(k\). The Hamiltonian is: \[H(q, p) = \frac{p^2}{2m} + \frac{1}{2} k q^2\]

Define the proposition: \[P \equiv \text{"Total energy is at most } E_0\text{"}\]

The corresponding Borel subset \(S_P \subset \mathbb{R}^2\) is the solid ellipse: \[S_P = \left\{(q, p) \in \mathbb{R}^2 \;\middle|\; \frac{p^2}{2m} + \frac{1}{2} k q^2 \le E_0\right\}\]

Now consider a second proposition: \[Q \equiv \text{"Particle position is positive: } q > 0\text{"}\] with half-plane \(S_Q = \{(q, p) \in \mathbb{R}^2 \mid q > 0\}\).

The composite propositions correspond strictly to set-theoretic operations on \(\mathbb{R}^2\): * Conjunction (\(P \wedge Q\)): \(S_P \cap S_Q\) (the right half of the energy ellipse). * Disjunction (\(P \vee Q\)): \(S_P \cup S_Q\) (the union of the ellipse and the right half-plane). * Negation (\(\neg P\)): \(\Gamma \setminus S_P\) (all phase space points outside the ellipse).

         p ^
           |       ..---..
           |     .'   |   '.     S_P = Entire Ellipse
           |    /     |     \    S_Q = Entire Right Half (q > 0)
    -------+---+------+------+--> q
           |    \     |     /    S_P ∩ S_Q = Right half of Ellipse
           |     '.   |   .'
           |       ''---''

Deterministic Truth Valuation

Given an exact microstate \(\omega = (\mathbf{q}, \mathbf{p}) \in \Gamma\), the truth valuation \(v_\omega(P)\) is governed by the set indicator function: \[ v_\omega(P) = \chi_{S_P}(\omega) = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } \omega \in S_P \\ 0 & \text{if } \omega \notin S_P \end{cases} \]

Because the power set \(\mathcal{P}(\Gamma)\) under \(\cap\), \(\cup\), and set complement \(\setminus\) satisfies all Boolean lattice axioms: \[\chi_{S_P \cap (S_Q \cup S_K)}(\omega) = \chi_{(S_P \cap S_Q) \cup (S_P \cap S_K)}(\omega)\] distributivity holds unconditionally. Classical mechanics is intrinsically Boolean and non-contextual: every physical proposition has a definite truth value in \(\{0, 1\}\) simultaneously, independent of any measurement apparatus.

6. Synthesis: The Logical Hierarchy

The journey through formal logic reveals that the laws of deduction are dictated by the geometry of the underlying mathematical or physical state space:

  1. Syntax (\(\text{WFF}\)): Provides the inductive grammar and parse tree hierarchy.
  2. Proof Theory (\(\mathbf{IPC}\) vs. \(\mathbf{CPC}\)): Intuitionism preserves constructivity and algorithmic witness extraction; classical logic adds non-constructive principles (LEM, DNE, RAA) to collapse double negation.
  3. Algebraic Semantics (\(\mathbf{2}\) vs. Heyting Algebras): Classical logic maps to distributive Boolean algebras; intuitionistic logic maps to Heyting algebras.
  4. Classical Physics (Phase Space \(\Gamma\)): Implements a Boolean field of subsets where all observables commute, guaranteeing that classical mechanics is ruled by classical logic.
