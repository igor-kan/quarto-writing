1. Syntax: WFFs, Alphabet, and Formation Rules

Formal logic separates pure syntax (symbolic manipulation) from semantics (truth and models).

Alphabet: A formal language \(\mathcal{L}\) consists of: * Propositional variables: \(\text{Prop} = \{p, q, r, p_1, p_2, \dots\}\) * Logical connectives: Primitive nullary (\(\bot\) for falsity, \(\top\) for truth), unary (\(\neg\) for negation), and binary (\(\wedge\) conjunction, \(\vee\) disjunction, \(\to\) implication). * Punctuation: Left and right parentheses \((, )\).

Inductive Definition of Well-Formed Formulas (WFFs): The set \(\text{WFF}(\mathcal{L})\) is the smallest set generated by the following formation rules: * Base Clause: Every propositional variable \(p \in \text{Prop}\) is a WFF; \(\bot\) and \(\top\) are WFFs. * Inductive Clause: If \(\varphi, \psi \in \text{WFF}(\mathcal{L})\), then \((\neg \varphi)\), \((\varphi \wedge \psi)\), \((\varphi \vee \psi)\), and \((\varphi \to \psi)\) are WFFs. * Extremal Clause: Nothing else is a WFF.

2. Deduction Rules: Classical vs. Intuitionistic Logic

In Gentzen-style Natural Deduction, logical connectives are governed by Introduction (\(I\)) and Elimination (\(E\)) rules:

\[ \begin{array}{ccc} \dfrac{\varphi \quad \psi}{\varphi \wedge \psi} \ (\wedge I) & \dfrac{\varphi \wedge \psi}{\varphi} \ (\wedge E_1) & \dfrac{\varphi \wedge \psi}{\psi} \ (\wedge E_2) \\[1.5em] \dfrac{\varphi}{\varphi \vee \psi} \ (\vee I_1) & \dfrac{\psi}{\varphi \vee \psi} \ (\vee I_2) & \dfrac{\varphi \vee \psi \quad \begin{matrix} [\varphi] \\ \vdots \\ \chi \end{matrix} \quad \begin{matrix} [\psi] \\ \vdots \\ \chi \end{matrix}}{\chi} \ (\vee E) \\[2em] \dfrac{\begin{matrix} [\varphi] \\ \vdots \\ \psi \end{matrix}}{\varphi \to \psi} \ (\to I) & \dfrac{\varphi \to \psi \quad \varphi}{\psi} \ (\to E) & \dfrac{\bot}{\varphi} \ (\bot E \text{ / EFQ}) \end{array} \]

Negation is defined constructively as refutation: \(\neg \varphi \equiv \varphi \to \bot\).

Negation Introduction (\(\neg I\)): If assuming \(\varphi\) yields \(\bot\), conclude \(\neg \varphi\). This rule is valid in both intuitionistic and classical logic.

The Core Divergence

Intuitionistic Propositional Logic (\(\mathbf{IPC}\)) contains all the rules above. Classical Propositional Logic (\(\mathbf{CPC}\)) is obtained by adding any one of the following mutually equivalent principles to \(\mathbf{IPC}\):

  • Law of Excluded Middle (LEM): \(\varphi \vee \neg \varphi\)
  • Double Negation Elimination (DNE): \(\neg\neg\varphi \to \varphi\)
  • Classical Reductio Ad Absurdum (RAA): \[ \dfrac{\begin{matrix} [\neg\varphi] \\ \vdots \\ \bot \end{matrix}}{\varphi} \]
  • Peirce’s Law: \(((\varphi \to \psi) \to \varphi) \to \varphi\)

Why Proof by Contradiction is Eliminated in Intuitionism

People frequently conflate two different proof techniques: 1. Constructive Refutation (\(\neg I\)): Assume \(A\), deduce \(\bot\), infer \(\neg A\). Accepted by intuitionists. 2. Classical Proof by Contradiction (\(\text{RAA}\)): Assume \(\neg A\), deduce \(\bot\), infer \(A\). Rejected by intuitionists.

Under the Brouwer-Heyting-Kolmogorov (\(\mathbf{BHK}\)) interpretation: * A proof of \(A \vee B\) requires an explicit algorithm selecting and proving either \(A\) or \(B\). * A proof of \(\exists x \, P(x)\) requires constructing a witness \(c\) and a proof of \(P(c)\). * A proof of \(\neg A\) is an algorithm converting any hypothetical proof of \(A\) into an absurdity (\(\bot\)).

If you prove \(\neg\neg A\), you have only demonstrated that it is impossible to refute \(A\). You have provided no algorithm or witness constructing \(A\) itself. Concluding \(A\) from \(\neg\neg A\) breaks the constructivity of mathematical objects (erasing the Curry-Howard isomorphism between proofs and executable programs).

3. Ex Falso Quodlibet & The “Paradoxes” of Implication

Ex Falso Quodlibet (\(EFQ\), the Principle of Explosion) states:

\[ \bot \vdash \varphi \quad \text{or} \quad \vdash \bot \to \varphi \]

“From an absurdity, anything follows.”

Why People Confuse Implication & Why \(EFQ\) Feels Unnatural

In natural language, “if… then…” implies a causal connection, relevance, or deductive entailment: \[ \text{"If it rains, the grass gets wet."} \quad (\text{Relevant / Causal implication}) \]

People regularly confuse Material Implication (\(\varphi \to \psi\)) with: * Syntactic Entailment (\(\varphi \vdash \psi\)): There is a mechanical proof deriving \(\psi\) from \(\varphi\). * Semantic Entailment (\(\varphi \models \psi\)): In every model where \(\varphi\) is true, \(\psi\) is true. * Strict Implication (\(\Box(\varphi \to \psi)\)): It is impossible for \(\varphi\) to be true and \(\psi\) false.

Why Classical Logic Uses Material Implication

Classical propositional logic demands truth functionality (the truth value of a compound formula depends only on the truth values of its atomic components, not on meaning or physics).

If \(\varphi \to \psi\) is a binary truth function \(f(v(\varphi), v(\psi)) \in \{0, 1\}\): * Modus Ponens requires that if \(\varphi = 1\) and \(\varphi \to \psi = 1\), then \(\psi = 1\). Thus, \(f(1, 0) = 0\). * If \(\varphi\) and \(\psi\) are both true, the implication must be true: \(f(1, 1) = 1\).

What if \(\varphi = 0\)? If we made \(f(0, 1) = 0\), then \(\varphi \to \psi\) would be equivalent to logical conjunction (\(\varphi \wedge \psi\)). If we made it depend on whether \(\varphi\) “causes” \(\psi\), truth functionality would collapse, requiring infinite modal possible worlds or relevance frames.

Setting \(f(0, 0) = f(0, 1) = 1\) is the only convention that preserves classical truth functionality: \[ \varphi \to \psi \equiv \neg \varphi \vee \psi \]

\(EFQ\) is simply the boundary consequence: if \(\varphi = \bot\) (false), the disjunction \(\neg \bot \vee \psi \equiv \top \vee \psi \equiv \top\) holds universally.

4. Boolean Algebra: Algebraic Semantics of Classical Logic

A Boolean algebra is an algebraic structure \(\mathcal{B} = \langle B, \wedge, \vee, \neg, 0, 1 \rangle\) such that \(\langle B, \wedge, \vee \rangle\) is a bounded distributive lattice with complements:

  • Associativity & Commutativity: \(x \wedge (y \wedge z) = (x \wedge y) \wedge z\), and \(x \wedge y = y \wedge x\) (same for \(\vee\)).
  • Absorption: \(x \wedge (x \vee y) = x\), and \(x \vee (x \wedge y) = x\).
  • Distributivity: \[ x \wedge (y \vee z) = (x \wedge y) \vee (x \wedge z) \] \[ x \vee (y \wedge z) = (x \vee y) \wedge (x \vee z) \]
  • Complementation: \(x \vee \neg x = 1\) and \(x \wedge \neg x = 0\).

By the Stone Representation Theorem, every Boolean algebra is isomorphic to a field of sets (subsets of a topological space under \(\cap\), \(\cup\), and set complement \(S^c\)).

The algebraic semantic model of classical propositional logic is the 2-element Boolean algebra \(\mathbf{2} = \{0, 1\}\). A model/valuation is a homomorphism \(v: \text{WFF} \to \mathbf{2}\).

5. Truth Valuations in Physics: Classical Mechanics

Physical theories evaluate propositions about measurable reality by mapping physical states to truth values.

Property Classical Mechanics (Phase Space)
State Point \(\omega = (\mathbf{q}, \mathbf{p}) \in \Gamma\)
Propositions Borel subsets \(S \subseteq \Gamma\)
Algebra Boolean \(\sigma\)-algebra (Distributive)
Logic Classical
Truth Global \(\{0, 1\}\) indicator valuation

Classical Mechanics (CM)

  • State Space: Phase space \(\Gamma = \mathbb{R}^{6N}\) with points \(\omega = (\mathbf{q}, \mathbf{p})\).
  • Propositions: An assertion \(P \equiv\) “Observable \(A\) takes values in set \(E \subset \mathbb{R}\).” \[ S_P = \{\omega \in \Gamma : A(\omega) \in E\} \subseteq \Gamma \]
  • Model Valuation: The state \(\omega\) assigns a deterministic truth value via the indicator function: \[ v_\omega(P) = \chi_{S_P}(\omega) = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } \omega \in S_P \\ 0 & \text{if } \omega \notin S_P \end{cases} \]
  • Logic: The power set \(\mathcal{P}(\Gamma)\) forms a complete Boolean algebra:
    • \(P \wedge Q \iff S_P \cap S_Q\)
    • \(P \vee Q \iff S_P \cup S_Q\)
    • \(\neg P \iff \Gamma \setminus S_P\)

Because set operations are strictly distributive, classical mechanics satisfies classical logic unconditionally.
