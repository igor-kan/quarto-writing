The rational numbers are the scaffold on which the real line is built. They are dense — between any two of them lies another, and between any two real numbers lies a rational — yet they are riddled with holes at exactly the points where \(\sqrt{2}\), \(e\), and \(\pi\) should be. Filling those holes gives \(\mathbb{R}\); generalizing “distance” gives metric spaces; discarding distance while keeping “openness” gives topology. This essay traces that entire ascent.

1 1. Introduction: A Line Full of Holes

The rationals \(\mathbb{Q}\) look like they are the number line. They support all four arithmetic operations, they are totally ordered, and between any two of them sits another. Pythagoras’ discovery that \(\sqrt{2}\notin\mathbb{Q}\) shattered this picture: the line has gaps. This essay answers a chain of natural questions:

  1. How exactly are the rationals counted, and why does “order by denominator, then numerator” list every rational in \((0,1)\)?
  2. What does it mean for a set to be dense, and how is denseness proved?
  3. What are Cauchy sequences and Dedekind cuts, and how do they repair \(\mathbb{Q}\)?
  4. What is a metric space, and how does it generalize the real line?
  5. What is a topology, and how does it generalize a metric space?

The unifying theme is the passage from order and distance to pure openness.

2 2. The Rationals: Construction, Order, and Order Type

2.1 2.1 Construction

Definition 1 (Definition 2.1 (The rational field)) On \(\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}^{+}\) (with \(\mathbb{Z}^{+}=\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}\)) define \[ (a,b)\sim(c,d) \iff ad = bc, \] and set \[ \mathbb{Q} := (\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}^{+})/{\sim},\qquad \frac{a}{b} := [(a,b)]. \] Addition and multiplication are defined by the familiar formulas and are well-defined on classes: \[ \frac{a}{b}+\frac{c}{d} = \frac{ad+cb}{bd},\qquad \frac{a}{b}\cdot\frac{c}{d} = \frac{ac}{bd}. \]

Each class has a unique reduced representative \(p/q\) with \(q>0\) and \(\gcd(p,q)=1\). This uniqueness is the key to the enumeration in Section 4.

2.2 2.2 The Order

For \(a/b\) and \(c/d\) with \(b,d>0\): \[ \frac{a}{b} < \frac{c}{d} \iff ad < cb. \] This makes \((\mathbb{Q},<)\) a totally ordered field:

  • \(x<y \Rightarrow x+z<y+z\) (translation invariance);
  • \(x<y\) and \(z>0 \Rightarrow xz<yz\) (scaling invariance);
  • Archimedean property: for any \(x>0\), there is \(n\in\mathbb{N}\) with \(n>x\). Equivalently, \(\mathbb{N}\) is unbounded in \(\mathbb{Q}\).

2.3 2.3 Order Type

\(\mathbb{Q}\) has the order type \(\eta\): it is a countable dense linear order without endpoints. By Cantor’s back-and-forth theorem, every countable dense linear order without endpoints is order-isomorphic to \((\mathbb{Q},<)\). In particular: \[ (\mathbb{Q},<) \;\cong\; (\mathbb{Q}\cap(0,1),<) \;\cong\; (\mathbb{Q}\cap(-\infty,0)\cup(0,\infty),<)\ \text{(after relabeling)}. \] But \((\mathbb{Q},<) \not\cong (\mathbb{Z},<)\) and \(\not\cong (\mathbb{N},<)\): those are discrete (every point has an immediate successor), whereas \(\mathbb{Q}\) is dense.

3 3. Denseness: Definition and Proof

3.1 3.1 Two Definitions

Definition 2 (Definition 3.1 (Dense in an order)) Let \((X,<)\) be a linear order. A subset \(D\subseteq X\) is dense in \(X\) if \[ \forall a,b\in X,\ a<b:\quad \exists d\in D\ \text{with } a<d<b. \]

Definition 3 (Definition 3.2 (Dense in a topological space)) Let \(X\) be a topological space. A subset \(D\subseteq X\) is dense if its closure is all of \(X\): \[ \overline{D} = X, \] equivalently, every nonempty open set \(U\subseteq X\) satisfies \(D\cap U\neq\varnothing\). In a metric space this reads: every open ball meets \(D\).

For ordered sets with the order topology the two notions coincide: \(D\) is order-dense iff \(D\cap(a,b)\neq\varnothing\) for all \(a<b\), i.e. \(D\) meets every basic open interval.

3.2 3.2 Q Is Dense in R

Theorem 1 (Theorem 3.1 (Density of \(\mathbb{Q}\) in \(\mathbb{R}\))) For all real \(a<b\) there exists \(q\in\mathbb{Q}\) with \(a<q<b\).

Proof (Archimedean). Let \(a<b\) be real. By the Archimedean property choose \(n\in\mathbb{N}\) with \[ n(b-a) > 1. \] Let \(m\) be the least integer strictly greater than \(na\) (which exists because \(\mathbb{Z}\) is well-ordered below and \(\mathbb{N}\) is unbounded). Then \[ m-1 \le na < m \implies \frac{m}{n} > a. \] On the other hand \(m \le na+1\), so \[ \frac{m}{n} \le a + \frac1n < a + (b-a) = b. \] Hence \(a < m/n < b\), and \(m/n\in\mathbb{Q}\). \(\blacksquare\)

3.3 3.3 The Irrationals Are Dense in R

Theorem 2 (Theorem 3.2) For all real \(a<b\) there exists irrational \(\xi\) with \(a<\xi<b\).

Proof. By Theorem 3.1 applied to \(a/\sqrt2 < b/\sqrt2\), choose nonzero \(q\in\mathbb{Q}\) with \(a/\sqrt2<q<b/\sqrt2\). Then \(\xi := q\sqrt2\) satisfies \(a<\xi<b\). If \(\xi\) were rational, then \(\xi/q=\sqrt2\) would be rational, a contradiction. \(\blacksquare\)

3.4 3.4 What Denseness Does and Does Not Mean

  • Denseness is not completeness. \(\mathbb{Q}\) is dense in \(\mathbb{R}\) but incomplete: \(\{q\in\mathbb{Q}: q^2<2\}\) has no supremum in \(\mathbb{Q}\).
  • Denseness is not uncountability. \(\mathbb{Q}\) is dense and countable. Density concerns where a set sits; cardinality concerns how many points it has.
  • Discrete sets are not dense. \(\mathbb{Z}\) is not dense in \(\mathbb{R}\): there is no integer strictly between \(0\) and \(1\).
  • Topological density is a closure statement. In \(\mathbb{R}\), \(\overline{\mathbb{Q}}=\mathbb{R}\) and \(\overline{\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}}=\mathbb{R}\): both the rationals and the irrationals are dense, though they are disjoint and one is countable while the other is not.

4 4. Counting the Rationals: “Denominator, then Numerator”

4.1 4.1 The Enumeration of \(\mathbb{Q}\cap(0,1)\)

Definition 4 (Definition 4.1 (The denominator-then-numerator order)) Enumerate the rationals in \((0,1)\) by \[ q=2,3,4,5,\dots;\qquad \text{for each } q,\ p=1,2,\dots,q-1 \text{ with } \gcd(p,q)=1, \] emitting \(p/q\) in the order encountered: \[ \frac12,\ \frac13,\ \frac23,\ \frac14,\ \frac34,\ \frac15,\ \frac25,\ \frac35,\ \frac45,\ \frac16,\ \frac56,\ \dots \]

Theorem 3 (Theorem 4.1) This list is a bijection \(\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{Q}\cap(0,1)\).

Proof. (Well-defined.) Each emitted fraction \(p/q\) is reduced and satisfies \(1\le p<q\), so \(0<p/q<1\); the block for a fixed finite \(q\) has exactly \(\varphi(q)\) entries (Euler’s totient), where \(\varphi(q)=|\{1\le p<q:\gcd(p,q)=1\}|\), hence is finite.

(Surjective.) Every \(x\in\mathbb{Q}\cap(0,1)\) has a unique reduced form \(p/q\) with \(1\le p<q\), \(\gcd(p,q)=1\). That fraction is emitted in the block indexed by \(q\), at position determined by \(p\).

(Injective.) Reduced forms are unique, and we skip non-reduced pairs, so no \(x\) is emitted twice.

Concatenating the finite blocks therefore gives a well-defined bijection. \(\blacksquare\)

The number of fractions with denominator \(\le Q\) is the totient summatory function \[ \Phi(Q) = \sum_{q=2}^{Q}\varphi(q) \sim \frac{3}{\pi^2}Q^{2}, \] so this ordering reaches about \(0.304\,Q^{2}\) rationals after the block of denominator \(Q\). Density is not the same as being evenly spread: the “denominator-first” order is a counting device, not the numeric order.

4.2 4.2 Why the Same Trick Fails on All of \(\mathbb{Q}\)

If one tries the naive rule “for each denominator \(q=1,2,3,\dots\), list all reduced numerators \(p\),” the very first block \(q=1\) has infinitely many members \(p/1=p\) (all integers), so the list never advances to \(q=2\). The block-by-block method requires each block to be finite, which holds exactly on \((0,1)\) (and more generally on \((0,1]\setminus\mathbb{Q}\)-free intervals) because \(p\) is bounded below by \(1\) and above by \(q\).

To enumerate all of \(\mathbb{Q}\), one subdivides: \[ \mathbb{Q} = \underbrace{\mathbb{Z}}_{\text{integers}} \;\cup\; \bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}} \bigl(\mathbb{Q}\cap(n,n+1)\bigr), \] i.e. the integers plus the countably many translated copies of \(\mathbb{Q}\cap(0,1)\). A countable union of countable sets is countable, so \(\mathbb{Q}\) is countable. Explicitly, use the Cantor pairing function \[ \pi(a,b) = \frac{(a+b)(a+b+1)}{2}+b, \] a bijection \(\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}\), and send \((a,b)\mapsto a/b\) for \(b\ge1\) (with sign handling). Since \(\mathbb{Q}\cong\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}^{+}/{\sim}\) is a quotient of a subset of \(\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}\), it is countable.

4.3 4.3 Farey Sequences and the Mediant

Definition 5 (Definition 4.2 (Farey sequence)) \(F_n\) is the increasing list of reduced fractions in \([0,1]\) with denominator \(\le n\): \[ F_1=\Bigl\{\tfrac01,\tfrac11\Bigr\},\quad F_2=\Bigl\{\tfrac01,\tfrac12,\tfrac11\Bigr\},\quad F_3=\Bigl\{\tfrac01,\tfrac13,\tfrac12,\tfrac23,\tfrac11\Bigr\},\ \dots \]

Two facts make Farey sequences a constructive proof of density:

  1. Mediant property. If \(a/b<c/d\) are adjacent in some \(F_n\), then the mediant \[ \frac{a+c}{b+d} \] lies strictly between them, and it is the fraction of smallest denominator strictly between \(a/b\) and \(c/d\).
  2. Unimodular adjacency. Adjacent Farey fractions \(a/b<c/d\) satisfy \[ bc-ad = 1. \]

Iterating the mediant between \(0/1\) and \(1/1\) generates the Stern–Brocot tree, in which every positive rational appears exactly once in lowest terms:

                     1/1
              /               \
           1/2                 2/1
         /    \              /    \
       1/3     2/3        3/2      3/1
      /  \    /  \       /  \     /  \
    1/4 2/5 3/5 3/4   4/3 5/3  5/2  4/1   ...
   (each node = mediant of its two nearest ancestors)

The in-order traversal of the tree (left to right at each depth) gives the fractions in increasing numeric order — a refinement of the denominator-first counting into the true order. The Stern–Brocot tree is the basis of the continued-fraction algorithm and of best rational approximation: the deepest node between two reals is the smallest-denominator fraction between them.

5 5. The Holes of \(\mathbb{Q}\): Cauchy Sequences

5.1 5.1 Definition

Definition 6 (Definition 5.1 (Cauchy sequence)) A sequence \((x_n)\) in a metric space \((X,d)\) is Cauchy if \[ \forall \varepsilon>0\ \exists N\ \forall m,n\ge N:\quad d(x_m,x_n)<\varepsilon. \] For \(X=\mathbb{Q}\) or \(\mathbb{R}\) this is \(|x_m-x_n|<\varepsilon\).

Intuitively: a Cauchy sequence is one whose terms eventually huddle together, regardless of whether they huddle around a point of the space.

5.2 5.2 Convergence Implies Cauchy, But Not Conversely

5.2.1 Proposition 5.1

Every convergent sequence is Cauchy.

Proof. If \(x_n\to L\), choose \(N\) with \(d(x_n,L)<\varepsilon/2\) for \(n\ge N\). Then for \(m,n\ge N\), the triangle inequality gives \(d(x_m,x_n)\le d(x_m,L)+d(L,x_n)<\varepsilon\). \(\blacksquare\)

The converse fails in \(\mathbb{Q}\). Three constructions:

  • Decimal truncations of \(\sqrt2\): \(x_n = \lfloor 10^n\sqrt2\rfloor/10^n\). Then \(|x_m-x_n|\to 0\), so \((x_n)\) is Cauchy, but its only possible limit is \(\sqrt2\notin\mathbb{Q}\).
  • Newton iterates: \(x_{n+1} = \tfrac12(x_n + 2/x_n)\) from \(x_1=3/2\). This is Cauchy and converges to \(\sqrt2\), again outside \(\mathbb{Q}\).
  • Partial sums of \(e\): \(x_n=\sum_{k=0}^n 1/k!\) is Cauchy, but its limit \(e\) is irrational.

Definition 7 (Definition 5.2 (Complete metric space)) \((X,d)\) is complete if every Cauchy sequence in \(X\) converges to a point of \(X\). Thus \(\mathbb{Q}\) is not complete, while \(\mathbb{R}\) is.

5.3 5.3 Constructing \(\mathbb{R}\) from Cauchy Sequences

Let \(\mathcal{C}\) be the set of all Cauchy sequences in \(\mathbb{Q}\), and define \[ (x_n)\sim (y_n) \iff \lim_{n\to\infty}(x_n-y_n)=0. \] This is an equivalence relation (the set of null sequences is an additive ideal inside the ring of Cauchy sequences). Define \[ \mathbb{R} := \mathcal{C}/{\sim}. \] On classes, set \([x]+[y]:=[x+y]\) and \([x]\cdot[y]:=[xy]\); these are well-defined. Order by \([x]<[y]\) iff \(y-x\) is eventually positive and bounded away from \(0\). The map \(q\mapsto[(q,q,q,\dots)]\) embeds \(\mathbb{Q}\) densely, and every Cauchy sequence in \(\mathbb{R}\) converges: \(\mathbb{R}\) is complete.

Theorem 4 (Theorem 5.1 (Uniqueness)) Any two complete ordered fields are isomorphic, and the isomorphism is unique. Hence the Cauchy construction and the Dedekind construction (Section 6) yield the same object up to unique isomorphism.

6 6. Dedekind Cuts

6.1 6.1 Definition

Definition 8 (Definition 6.1 (Dedekind cut)) A Dedekind cut is a set \(A\subseteq\mathbb{Q}\) such that 1. \(A\neq\varnothing\) and \(A\neq\mathbb{Q}\); 2. \(A\) is downward closed: \(x\in A\) and \(y<x\) imply \(y\in A\); 3. \(A\) has no greatest element.

The three conditions build the “left half” of a partition. Order cuts by inclusion: \(A\le B\iff A\subseteq B\). Then define \[ \mathbb{R} := \{\, A : A \text{ is a Dedekind cut} \,\}. \]

6.2 6.2 The Cut for \(\sqrt2\)

The archetypal cut is \[ A_{\sqrt2} = \{\, x\in\mathbb{Q} : x<0 \ \text{or}\ x^2<2 \,\},\qquad B_{\sqrt2} = \{\, x\in\mathbb{Q} : x>0 \ \text{and}\ x^2>2 \,\}. \] \(A_{\sqrt2}\) has no maximum: given \(x\in A_{\sqrt2}\) with \(x\ge0\), density supplies a rational \(\delta>0\) with \((x+\delta)^2<2\) (choose \(\delta\) small enough that \(2x\delta+\delta^2<2-x^2\), possible by the Archimedean property), so \(x+\delta\in A_{\sqrt2}\). Yet there is no rational \(q\) with \(A_{\sqrt2}=\{x:x<q\}\). The gap is filled by the cut itself — which is \(\sqrt2\).

6.3 6.3 Completeness

Theorem 5 (Theorem 6.1 (Least upper bound property)) Every nonempty subset \(\mathcal{S}\) of cuts that is bounded above has a least upper bound, namely \[ \sup \mathcal{S} = \bigcup_{A\in\mathcal{S}} A. \]

Proof. Let \(U=\bigcup_{A\in\mathcal{S}}A\). Then \(U\) is downward closed (a union of downward closed sets), nonempty, and proper (bounded above by some cut). If \(U\) had a greatest element \(u\), then \(u\in A\) for some \(A\in\mathcal{S}\), and since \(A\) has no greatest element there is \(u'\in A\subseteq U\) with \(u'>u\), contradiction. So \(U\) is a cut. It clearly contains every \(A\), and any upper bound contains each \(A\) hence contains \(U\); thus \(U\) is the least upper bound. \(\blacksquare\)

The Cauchy method generalizes to arbitrary metric spaces (Section 7); the Dedekind method characterizes the order-theoretic completion. Both produce the unique complete ordered field.

7 7. Metric Spaces

7.1 7.1 Definition and Examples

Definition 9 (Definition 7.1 (Metric space)) A metric space is a pair \((X,d)\) where \(X\) is a set and \(d:X\times X\to[0,\infty)\) satisfies, for all \(x,y,z\in X\): \[ \begin{aligned} \text{(M1)}&\quad d(x,y)=0 \iff x=y && \text{(identity of indiscernibles)}\\ \text{(M2)}&\quad d(x,y)=d(y,x) && \text{(symmetry)}\\ \text{(M3)}&\quad d(x,z)\le d(x,y)+d(y,z) && \text{(triangle inequality)} \end{aligned} \]

Space Distance \(d(x,y)\) Notes
\(\mathbb{R}\) \(\lvert x-y\rvert\) the archetype
\(\mathbb{R}^n\) \(\sqrt{\sum_i (x_i-y_i)^2}\) Euclidean
\(\mathbb{R}^n\) \(\sum_i\lvert x_i-y_i\rvert\) taxicab / Manhattan

All norms on a finite-dimensional vector space over \(\mathbb{R}\) are equivalent, so Euclidean, taxicab, and sup metrics induce the same topology on \(\mathbb{R}^n\).

7.2 7.2 Balls, Open Sets, Convergence

The open ball of radius \(r>0\) about \(x\) is \[ B(x,r) = \{\, y\in X : d(x,y)<r \,\}. \] A set \(U\subseteq X\) is open if every \(x\in U\) has some \(B(x,r)\subseteq U\). The collection of open sets forms the metric topology \(\tau_d\) on \(X\) (Section 8).

  • \(x_n\to x\) iff \(d(x_n,x)\to0\).
  • Cauchy sequences and completeness are as in Definition 5.2; \(\mathbb{Q}\) is incomplete, \(\mathbb{R}\) and \(C[0,1]\) (sup metric) are complete.
  • Bounded sets: \(\operatorname{diam}(A)=\sup_{x,y\in A}d(x,y)<\infty\).

7.3 7.3 Completion

Theorem 6 (Theorem 7.1 (Completion)) Every metric space \((X,d)\) has a completion \((\widehat{X},\widehat{d})\): a complete metric space containing an isometric dense copy of \(X\), unique up to isometry fixing \(X\).

The construction is the Cauchy-sequence one of Section 5.3, verbatim: \(\widehat{X}\) is the set of equivalence classes of Cauchy sequences in \(X\), with \(\widehat{d}([x],[y])=\lim_n d(x_n,y_n)\).

7.4 7.4 Contraction Mapping and Baire

Theorem 7 (Theorem 7.2 (Banach fixed-point theorem)) Let \((X,d)\) be complete and \(T:X\to X\) a contraction: \(d(Tx,Ty)\le k\,d(x,y)\) for some \(0\le k<1\). Then \(T\) has a unique fixed point \(x^*\), and \(T^n x\to x^*\) for every \(x\).

Proof sketch. The orbit \(x, Tx, T^2x,\dots\) is Cauchy because \(d(T^mx,T^{m+1}x)\le k^m d(x,Tx)\) and \(\sum k^m<\infty\). Completeness gives a limit \(x^*\), continuity of \(T\) gives \(Tx^*=x^*\), and uniqueness follows from \(d(x^*,y^*)\le k\,d(x^*,y^*)\). \(\blacksquare\)

Theorem 8 (Theorem 7.3 (Baire category theorem)) A complete metric space is Baire: a countable intersection of dense open sets is dense. Equivalently, it is not the union of countably many nowhere-dense sets.

Baire’s theorem powers the open mapping and uniform boundedness principles of functional analysis, and the existence of nowhere-differentiable continuous functions. It is one of the deepest reasons completeness matters.

7.5 7.5 Compactness in Metric Spaces

\(K\subseteq X\) is compact if every open cover has a finite subcover. In metric spaces this is equivalent to: every sequence has a convergent subsequence (sequential compactness), which is equivalent to \(K\) being complete and totally bounded. In \(\mathbb{R}^n\) this reduces to Heine–Borel: closed and bounded \(\iff\) compact. Compact sets inherit closedness, boundedness, and the extreme value property (a continuous real function attains its max and min).

8 8. Topology: Openness Without Distance

8.1 8.1 Definition

Definition 10 (Definition 8.1 (Topological space)) A topology on a set \(X\) is a family \(\tau\subseteq\mathcal{P}(X)\) such that 1. \(\varnothing\in\tau\) and \(X\in\tau\); 2. \(\tau\) is closed under arbitrary unions: if \(\{U_i\}_{i\in I}\subseteq\tau\) then \(\bigcup_i U_i\in\tau\); 3. \(\tau\) is closed under finite intersections: if \(U_1,\dots,U_n\in\tau\) then \(\bigcap_{k=1}^n U_k\in\tau\).

The pair \((X,\tau)\) is a topological space; members of \(\tau\) are open sets.

Every metric space is a topological space via its metric topology (open sets as in Section 7.2), but not conversely (Section 8.5).

8.3 8.3 Bases, Closure, Interior, Boundary

  • A basis \(\mathcal{B}\subseteq\tau\) generates \(\tau\) if every open set is a union of members of \(\mathcal{B}\) (e.g. open balls in a metric space; open intervals in \(\mathbb{R}\)).
  • A subbasis generates a basis by finite intersections.
  • Closure \(\overline{A}\): the smallest closed set containing \(A\); \(x\in\overline{A}\) iff every open neighborhood of \(x\) meets \(A\).
  • Interior \(A^\circ\): the largest open set contained in \(A\).
  • Boundary \(\partial A = \overline{A}\setminus A^\circ\).
  • \(A\) is dense iff \(\overline{A}=X\); nowhere dense iff \(\overline{A}\) has empty interior.

In \(\mathbb{R}\): \(\overline{\mathbb{Q}}=\mathbb{R}\), \(\mathbb{Q}^\circ=\varnothing\), \(\partial\mathbb{Q}=\mathbb{R}\). The rationals are everywhere and yet occupy no open space — the precise sense of “dense but full of holes.”

8.4 8.4 Continuity and Homeomorphism

Definition 11 (Definition 8.2 (Continuity)) \(f:(X,\tau_X)\to(Y,\tau_Y)\) is continuous if \(f^{-1}(V)\in\tau_X\) for every \(V\in\tau_Y\) (preimages of open sets are open).

Equivalent characterizations: \(f(\overline{A})\subseteq\overline{f(A)}\); \(f^{-1}(F)\) closed for closed \(F\); continuity at each point via neighborhoods. For \(f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\) this recovers the \(\varepsilon\)–\(\delta\) definition with \(\varepsilon,\delta\) replaced by arbitrary neighborhoods.

A homeomorphism is a bijective continuous map whose inverse is continuous: the notion of “same shape” for topological spaces. Properties preserved by homeomorphism (compactness, connectedness, Hausdorffness, cardinal invariants) are topological invariants. A classic illustration: any open interval is homeomorphic to \(\mathbb{R}\) via \(\tan\), even though it is bounded as a metric subspace.

8.5 8.5 Separation Axioms

Axiom Condition Meaning
\(T_0\) points distinguished by some open set Kolmogorov
\(T_1\) \(\{x\}\) closed for all \(x\) points are closed
\(T_2\) (Hausdorff) disjoint neighborhoods for any two points limits of sequences are unique
\(T_3\) (regular) point and closed set separated —
\(T_4\) (normal) two disjoint closed sets separated Urysohn / Tietze hold

Every metric space is Hausdorff (take \(r=d(x,y)/2\)), which is why limits are unique there. Metrizability is characterized by the Urysohn metrization theorem (regular \(T_1\) + second countable \(\Rightarrow\) metrizable) and the Nagata–Smirnov theorem; hence a non-Hausdorff space, like the cofinite topology on an infinite set, is not metrizable.

8.6 8.6 Compactness, Connectedness, and the Limits of Sequences

  • Compactness: a space is compact if every open cover has a finite subcover. Continuous images of compact sets are compact; a continuous real function on compact \(K\) attains its bounds (extreme value theorem). Tychonoff’s theorem says arbitrary products of compact spaces are compact — equivalent to the Axiom of Choice.
  • Connectedness: a space is connected if it cannot be written as a union of two disjoint nonempty open sets. Continuous images of connected sets are connected; the connected subsets of \(\mathbb{R}\) are exactly the intervals (Intermediate Value Theorem). Path-connectedness is stronger in general and equal for open subsets of \(\mathbb{R}^n\).
  • Sequences are not enough in general topology. In a first-countable space (e.g. any metric space) sequences detect closure and continuity, but in general one needs nets or filters. This is precisely the extra structure a metric supplies.

## 1. Introduction: A Line Full of Holes

The rationals $\mathbb{Q}$ look like they *are* the number line. They support all four arithmetic operations, they are totally ordered, and between any two of them sits another. Pythagoras' discovery that $\sqrt{2}\notin\mathbb{Q}$ shattered this picture: the line has **gaps**. This essay answers a chain of natural questions:

1. How exactly are the rationals **counted**, and why does "order by denominator, then numerator" list every rational in $(0,1)$?
2. What does it mean for a set to be **dense**, and how is denseness proved?
3. What are **Cauchy sequences** and **Dedekind cuts**, and how do they repair $\mathbb{Q}$?
4. What is a **metric space**, and how does it generalize the real line?
5. What is a **topology**, and how does it generalize a metric space?

The unifying theme is the passage from **order** and **distance** to pure **openness**.

## 2. The Rationals: Construction, Order, and Order Type

### 2.1 Construction

::: {#def-rationals}
#### Definition 2.1 (The rational field)
On $\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}^{+}$ (with $\mathbb{Z}^{+}=\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}$) define
$$
(a,b)\sim(c,d) \iff ad = bc,
$$
and set
$$
\mathbb{Q} := (\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}^{+})/{\sim},\qquad
\frac{a}{b} := [(a,b)].
$$
Addition and multiplication are defined by the familiar formulas and are well-defined on classes:
$$
\frac{a}{b}+\frac{c}{d} = \frac{ad+cb}{bd},\qquad
\frac{a}{b}\cdot\frac{c}{d} = \frac{ac}{bd}.
$$
:::

Each class has a **unique reduced representative** $p/q$ with $q>0$ and $\gcd(p,q)=1$. This uniqueness is the key to the enumeration in Section 4.

### 2.2 The Order

For $a/b$ and $c/d$ with $b,d>0$:
$$
\frac{a}{b} < \frac{c}{d} \iff ad < cb.
$$
This makes $(\mathbb{Q},<)$ a **totally ordered field**:

- $x<y \Rightarrow x+z<y+z$ (translation invariance);
- $x<y$ and $z>0 \Rightarrow xz<yz$ (scaling invariance);
- **Archimedean property**: for any $x>0$, there is $n\in\mathbb{N}$ with $n>x$. Equivalently, $\mathbb{N}$ is unbounded in $\mathbb{Q}$.

### 2.3 Order Type

$\mathbb{Q}$ has the order type $\eta$: it is a **countable dense linear order without endpoints**. By Cantor's back-and-forth theorem, *every* countable dense linear order without endpoints is order-isomorphic to $(\mathbb{Q},<)$. In particular:
$$
(\mathbb{Q},<) \;\cong\; (\mathbb{Q}\cap(0,1),<) \;\cong\; (\mathbb{Q}\cap(-\infty,0)\cup(0,\infty),<)\ \text{(after relabeling)}.
$$
But $(\mathbb{Q},<) \not\cong (\mathbb{Z},<)$ and $\not\cong (\mathbb{N},<)$: those are **discrete** (every point has an immediate successor), whereas $\mathbb{Q}$ is dense.

```text
   ORDER TYPES AT CARDINALITY ℵ0 (all countable, but not isomorphic)

   (N, <):   0 — 1 — 2 — 3 — ...        discrete, has least element
   (Z, <):   ... — (-1) — 0 — 1 — ...   discrete, no endpoints
   (Q, <):   ═══════════════════════    dense, no endpoints  (type η)
   (Q+, <):  0 ────── dense ──────→     dense, has least element
```

## 3. Denseness: Definition and Proof

### 3.1 Two Definitions

::: {#def-dense-order}
#### Definition 3.1 (Dense in an order)
Let $(X,<)$ be a linear order. A subset $D\subseteq X$ is **dense in $X$** if
$$
\forall a,b\in X,\ a<b:\quad \exists d\in D\ \text{with } a<d<b.
$$
:::

::: {#def-dense-top}
#### Definition 3.2 (Dense in a topological space)
Let $X$ be a topological space. A subset $D\subseteq X$ is **dense** if its closure is all of $X$:
$$
\overline{D} = X,
$$
equivalently, every nonempty open set $U\subseteq X$ satisfies $D\cap U\neq\varnothing$.
In a metric space this reads: every open ball meets $D$.
:::

For ordered sets with the order topology the two notions coincide: $D$ is order-dense iff $D\cap(a,b)\neq\varnothing$ for all $a<b$, i.e. $D$ meets every basic open interval.

### 3.2 Q Is Dense in R

::: {#thm-q-dense}
#### Theorem 3.1 (Density of $\mathbb{Q}$ in $\mathbb{R}$)
For all real $a<b$ there exists $q\in\mathbb{Q}$ with $a<q<b$.
:::

*Proof (Archimedean).* Let $a<b$ be real. By the Archimedean property choose $n\in\mathbb{N}$ with
$$
n(b-a) > 1.
$$
Let $m$ be the least integer strictly greater than $na$ (which exists because $\mathbb{Z}$ is well-ordered below and $\mathbb{N}$ is unbounded). Then
$$
m-1 \le na < m \implies \frac{m}{n} > a.
$$
On the other hand $m \le na+1$, so
$$
\frac{m}{n} \le a + \frac1n < a + (b-a) = b.
$$
Hence $a < m/n < b$, and $m/n\in\mathbb{Q}$. $\blacksquare$

### 3.3 The Irrationals Are Dense in R

::: {#thm-irr-dense}
#### Theorem 3.2
For all real $a<b$ there exists irrational $\xi$ with $a<\xi<b$.
:::

*Proof.* By Theorem 3.1 applied to $a/\sqrt2 < b/\sqrt2$, choose nonzero $q\in\mathbb{Q}$ with $a/\sqrt2<q<b/\sqrt2$. Then $\xi := q\sqrt2$ satisfies $a<\xi<b$. If $\xi$ were rational, then $\xi/q=\sqrt2$ would be rational, a contradiction. $\blacksquare$

### 3.4 What Denseness Does and Does Not Mean

- **Denseness is not completeness.** $\mathbb{Q}$ is dense in $\mathbb{R}$ but incomplete: $\{q\in\mathbb{Q}: q^2<2\}$ has no supremum *in $\mathbb{Q}$*.
- **Denseness is not uncountability.** $\mathbb{Q}$ is dense and countable. Density concerns *where* a set sits; cardinality concerns *how many* points it has.
- **Discrete sets are not dense.** $\mathbb{Z}$ is not dense in $\mathbb{R}$: there is no integer strictly between $0$ and $1$.
- **Topological density is a closure statement.** In $\mathbb{R}$, $\overline{\mathbb{Q}}=\mathbb{R}$ and $\overline{\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}}=\mathbb{R}$: both the rationals and the irrationals are dense, though they are disjoint and one is countable while the other is not.

## 4. Counting the Rationals: "Denominator, then Numerator"

### 4.1 The Enumeration of $\mathbb{Q}\cap(0,1)$

::: {#def-den-num-order}
#### Definition 4.1 (The denominator-then-numerator order)
Enumerate the rationals in $(0,1)$ by
$$
q=2,3,4,5,\dots;\qquad \text{for each } q,\ p=1,2,\dots,q-1 \text{ with } \gcd(p,q)=1,
$$
emitting $p/q$ in the order encountered:
$$
\frac12,\ \frac13,\ \frac23,\ \frac14,\ \frac34,\ \frac15,\ \frac25,\ \frac35,\ \frac45,\ \frac16,\ \frac56,\ \dots
$$
:::

::: {#thm-unit-countable}
#### Theorem 4.1
This list is a bijection $\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{Q}\cap(0,1)$.
:::

*Proof.*
**(Well-defined.)** Each emitted fraction $p/q$ is reduced and satisfies $1\le p<q$, so $0<p/q<1$; the block for a fixed finite $q$ has exactly $\varphi(q)$ entries (Euler's totient), where $\varphi(q)=|\{1\le p<q:\gcd(p,q)=1\}|$, hence is finite.

**(Surjective.)** Every $x\in\mathbb{Q}\cap(0,1)$ has a unique reduced form $p/q$ with $1\le p<q$, $\gcd(p,q)=1$. That fraction is emitted in the block indexed by $q$, at position determined by $p$.

**(Injective.)** Reduced forms are unique, and we skip non-reduced pairs, so no $x$ is emitted twice.

Concatenating the finite blocks therefore gives a well-defined bijection. $\blacksquare$

The number of fractions with denominator $\le Q$ is the **totient summatory function**
$$
\Phi(Q) = \sum_{q=2}^{Q}\varphi(q) \sim \frac{3}{\pi^2}Q^{2},
$$
so this ordering reaches about $0.304\,Q^{2}$ rationals after the block of denominator $Q$. Density is not the same as being evenly spread: the "denominator-first" order is a counting device, not the numeric order.

### 4.2 Why the Same Trick Fails on All of $\mathbb{Q}$

If one tries the naive rule "for each denominator $q=1,2,3,\dots$, list all reduced numerators $p$," the very first block $q=1$ has infinitely many members $p/1=p$ (all integers), so the list never advances to $q=2$. The block-by-block method requires each block to be **finite**, which holds exactly on $(0,1)$ (and more generally on $(0,1]\setminus\mathbb{Q}$-free intervals) because $p$ is bounded below by $1$ and above by $q$.

To enumerate all of $\mathbb{Q}$, one subdivides:
$$
\mathbb{Q} = \underbrace{\mathbb{Z}}_{\text{integers}} \;\cup\; \bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}} \bigl(\mathbb{Q}\cap(n,n+1)\bigr),
$$
i.e. the integers plus the countably many translated copies of $\mathbb{Q}\cap(0,1)$. A **countable union of countable sets is countable**, so $\mathbb{Q}$ is countable. Explicitly, use the **Cantor pairing function**
$$
\pi(a,b) = \frac{(a+b)(a+b+1)}{2}+b,
$$
a bijection $\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}$, and send $(a,b)\mapsto a/b$ for $b\ge1$ (with sign handling). Since $\mathbb{Q}\cong\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}^{+}/{\sim}$ is a quotient of a subset of $\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}$, it is countable.

### 4.3 Farey Sequences and the Mediant

::: {#def-farey}
#### Definition 4.2 (Farey sequence)
$F_n$ is the increasing list of reduced fractions in $[0,1]$ with denominator $\le n$:
$$
F_1=\Bigl\{\tfrac01,\tfrac11\Bigr\},\quad
F_2=\Bigl\{\tfrac01,\tfrac12,\tfrac11\Bigr\},\quad
F_3=\Bigl\{\tfrac01,\tfrac13,\tfrac12,\tfrac23,\tfrac11\Bigr\},\ \dots
$$
:::

Two facts make Farey sequences a *constructive* proof of density:

1. **Mediant property.** If $a/b<c/d$ are adjacent in some $F_n$, then the **mediant**
$$
\frac{a+c}{b+d}
$$
lies strictly between them, and it is the fraction of smallest denominator strictly between $a/b$ and $c/d$.
2. **Unimodular adjacency.** Adjacent Farey fractions $a/b<c/d$ satisfy
$$
bc-ad = 1.
$$

Iterating the mediant between $0/1$ and $1/1$ generates the **Stern–Brocot tree**, in which every positive rational appears **exactly once** in lowest terms:

```text
                     1/1
              /               \
           1/2                 2/1
         /    \              /    \
       1/3     2/3        3/2      3/1
      /  \    /  \       /  \     /  \
    1/4 2/5 3/5 3/4   4/3 5/3  5/2  4/1   ...
   (each node = mediant of its two nearest ancestors)
```

The in-order traversal of the tree (left to right at each depth) gives the fractions in increasing numeric order — a *refinement* of the denominator-first counting into the true order. The Stern–Brocot tree is the basis of the **continued-fraction** algorithm and of best rational approximation: the deepest node between two reals is the smallest-denominator fraction between them.

## 5. The Holes of $\mathbb{Q}$: Cauchy Sequences

### 5.1 Definition

::: {#def-cauchy}
#### Definition 5.1 (Cauchy sequence)
A sequence $(x_n)$ in a metric space $(X,d)$ is **Cauchy** if
$$
\forall \varepsilon>0\ \exists N\ \forall m,n\ge N:\quad d(x_m,x_n)<\varepsilon.
$$
For $X=\mathbb{Q}$ or $\mathbb{R}$ this is $|x_m-x_n|<\varepsilon$.
:::

Intuitively: a Cauchy sequence is one whose terms eventually huddle together, regardless of whether they huddle around a *point of the space*.

### 5.2 Convergence Implies Cauchy, But Not Conversely

::: {#prop-conv-cauchy}
#### Proposition 5.1
Every convergent sequence is Cauchy.
:::

*Proof.* If $x_n\to L$, choose $N$ with $d(x_n,L)<\varepsilon/2$ for $n\ge N$. Then for $m,n\ge N$, the triangle inequality gives $d(x_m,x_n)\le d(x_m,L)+d(L,x_n)<\varepsilon$. $\blacksquare$

The converse **fails in $\mathbb{Q}$**. Three constructions:

- **Decimal truncations of $\sqrt2$:** $x_n = \lfloor 10^n\sqrt2\rfloor/10^n$. Then $|x_m-x_n|\to 0$, so $(x_n)$ is Cauchy, but its only possible limit is $\sqrt2\notin\mathbb{Q}$.
- **Newton iterates:** $x_{n+1} = \tfrac12(x_n + 2/x_n)$ from $x_1=3/2$. This is Cauchy and converges to $\sqrt2$, again outside $\mathbb{Q}$.
- **Partial sums of $e$:** $x_n=\sum_{k=0}^n 1/k!$ is Cauchy, but its limit $e$ is irrational.

::: {#def-complete}
#### Definition 5.2 (Complete metric space)
$(X,d)$ is **complete** if every Cauchy sequence in $X$ converges to a point of $X$. Thus $\mathbb{Q}$ is **not** complete, while $\mathbb{R}$ is.
:::

### 5.3 Constructing $\mathbb{R}$ from Cauchy Sequences

Let $\mathcal{C}$ be the set of all Cauchy sequences in $\mathbb{Q}$, and define
$$
(x_n)\sim (y_n) \iff \lim_{n\to\infty}(x_n-y_n)=0.
$$
This is an equivalence relation (the set of null sequences is an additive ideal inside the ring of Cauchy sequences). Define
$$
\mathbb{R} := \mathcal{C}/{\sim}.
$$
On classes, set $[x]+[y]:=[x+y]$ and $[x]\cdot[y]:=[xy]$; these are well-defined. Order by $[x]<[y]$ iff $y-x$ is eventually positive and bounded away from $0$. The map $q\mapsto[(q,q,q,\dots)]$ embeds $\mathbb{Q}$ densely, and every Cauchy sequence in $\mathbb{R}$ converges: $\mathbb{R}$ is **complete**.

::: {#thm-uniqueness-r}
#### Theorem 5.1 (Uniqueness)
Any two complete ordered fields are isomorphic, and the isomorphism is unique. Hence the Cauchy construction and the Dedekind construction (Section 6) yield the same object up to unique isomorphism.
:::

## 6. Dedekind Cuts

### 6.1 Definition

::: {#def-cut}
#### Definition 6.1 (Dedekind cut)
A **Dedekind cut** is a set $A\subseteq\mathbb{Q}$ such that
1. $A\neq\varnothing$ and $A\neq\mathbb{Q}$;
2. $A$ is **downward closed**: $x\in A$ and $y<x$ imply $y\in A$;
3. $A$ has **no greatest element**.
:::

The three conditions build the "left half" of a partition. Order cuts by inclusion: $A\le B\iff A\subseteq B$. Then define
$$
\mathbb{R} := \{\, A : A \text{ is a Dedekind cut} \,\}.
$$

### 6.2 The Cut for $\sqrt2$

The archetypal cut is
$$
A_{\sqrt2} = \{\, x\in\mathbb{Q} : x<0 \ \text{or}\ x^2<2 \,\},\qquad
B_{\sqrt2} = \{\, x\in\mathbb{Q} : x>0 \ \text{and}\ x^2>2 \,\}.
$$
$A_{\sqrt2}$ has **no maximum**: given $x\in A_{\sqrt2}$ with $x\ge0$, density supplies a rational $\delta>0$ with $(x+\delta)^2<2$ (choose $\delta$ small enough that $2x\delta+\delta^2<2-x^2$, possible by the Archimedean property), so $x+\delta\in A_{\sqrt2}$. Yet there is no rational $q$ with $A_{\sqrt2}=\{x:x<q\}$. The gap is filled by the cut itself — which *is* $\sqrt2$.

### 6.3 Completeness

::: {#thm-dedekind-complete}
#### Theorem 6.1 (Least upper bound property)
Every nonempty subset $\mathcal{S}$ of cuts that is bounded above has a least upper bound, namely
$$
\sup \mathcal{S} = \bigcup_{A\in\mathcal{S}} A.
$$
:::

*Proof.* Let $U=\bigcup_{A\in\mathcal{S}}A$. Then $U$ is downward closed (a union of downward closed sets), nonempty, and proper (bounded above by some cut). If $U$ had a greatest element $u$, then $u\in A$ for some $A\in\mathcal{S}$, and since $A$ has no greatest element there is $u'\in A\subseteq U$ with $u'>u$, contradiction. So $U$ is a cut. It clearly contains every $A$, and any upper bound contains each $A$ hence contains $U$; thus $U$ is the least upper bound. $\blacksquare$

### 6.4 Cauchy versus Dedekind

| Feature | Cauchy completion | Dedekind cuts |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Data | sequences + null-equivalence | downward-closed subsets of $\mathbb{Q}$ |
| Order built from | eventual positivity | set inclusion |
| Completeness | limits of Cauchy sequences | least upper bounds |
| Generalizes to | every metric space | every linear order |
| Same result | $\mathbb{R}$ | $\mathbb{R}$ |

The Cauchy method generalizes to arbitrary metric spaces (Section 7); the Dedekind method characterizes the order-theoretic completion. Both produce the unique complete ordered field.

## 7. Metric Spaces

### 7.1 Definition and Examples

::: {#def-metric}
#### Definition 7.1 (Metric space)
A **metric space** is a pair $(X,d)$ where $X$ is a set and $d:X\times X\to[0,\infty)$ satisfies, for all $x,y,z\in X$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{(M1)}&\quad d(x,y)=0 \iff x=y && \text{(identity of indiscernibles)}\\
\text{(M2)}&\quad d(x,y)=d(y,x) && \text{(symmetry)}\\
\text{(M3)}&\quad d(x,z)\le d(x,y)+d(y,z) && \text{(triangle inequality)}
\end{aligned}
$$
:::

| Space | Distance $d(x,y)$ | Notes |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\mathbb{R}$ | $\lvert x-y\rvert$ | the archetype |
| $\mathbb{R}^n$ | $\sqrt{\sum_i (x_i-y_i)^2}$ | Euclidean |
| $\mathbb{R}^n$ | $\sum_i\lvert x_i-y_i\rvert$ | taxicab / Manhattan |
| $\mathbb{R}^n$ | $\max_i\lvert x_i-y_i\rvert$ | sup / Chebyshev |
| any set $X$ | $0$ if equal, else $1$ | **discrete metric** |
| $C[0,1]$ | $\sup_{t}\lvert f(t)-g(t)\rvert$ | continuous functions |
| $\ell^p$ | $\bigl(\sum_i\lvert x_i-y_i\rvert^p\bigr)^{1/p}$ | sequence spaces |
| words / graphs | shortest-path length | combinatorial metrics |

All norms on a finite-dimensional vector space over $\mathbb{R}$ are equivalent, so Euclidean, taxicab, and sup metrics induce the same topology on $\mathbb{R}^n$.

### 7.2 Balls, Open Sets, Convergence

The **open ball** of radius $r>0$ about $x$ is
$$
B(x,r) = \{\, y\in X : d(x,y)<r \,\}.
$$
A set $U\subseteq X$ is **open** if every $x\in U$ has some $B(x,r)\subseteq U$. The collection of open sets forms the **metric topology** $\tau_d$ on $X$ (Section 8).

- $x_n\to x$ iff $d(x_n,x)\to0$.
- Cauchy sequences and completeness are as in Definition 5.2; $\mathbb{Q}$ is incomplete, $\mathbb{R}$ and $C[0,1]$ (sup metric) are complete.
- **Bounded** sets: $\operatorname{diam}(A)=\sup_{x,y\in A}d(x,y)<\infty$.

### 7.3 Completion

::: {#thm-completion}
#### Theorem 7.1 (Completion)
Every metric space $(X,d)$ has a **completion** $(\widehat{X},\widehat{d})$: a complete metric space containing an isometric dense copy of $X$, unique up to isometry fixing $X$.
:::

The construction is the Cauchy-sequence one of Section 5.3, verbatim: $\widehat{X}$ is the set of equivalence classes of Cauchy sequences in $X$, with $\widehat{d}([x],[y])=\lim_n d(x_n,y_n)$.

### 7.4 Contraction Mapping and Baire

::: {#thm-banach}
#### Theorem 7.2 (Banach fixed-point theorem)
Let $(X,d)$ be complete and $T:X\to X$ a **contraction**: $d(Tx,Ty)\le k\,d(x,y)$ for some $0\le k<1$. Then $T$ has a unique fixed point $x^*$, and $T^n x\to x^*$ for every $x$.
:::

*Proof sketch.* The orbit $x, Tx, T^2x,\dots$ is Cauchy because $d(T^mx,T^{m+1}x)\le k^m d(x,Tx)$ and $\sum k^m<\infty$. Completeness gives a limit $x^*$, continuity of $T$ gives $Tx^*=x^*$, and uniqueness follows from $d(x^*,y^*)\le k\,d(x^*,y^*)$. $\blacksquare$

::: {#thm-baire}
#### Theorem 7.3 (Baire category theorem)
A complete metric space is **Baire**: a countable intersection of dense open sets is dense. Equivalently, it is not the union of countably many nowhere-dense sets.
:::

Baire's theorem powers the **open mapping** and **uniform boundedness** principles of functional analysis, and the existence of nowhere-differentiable continuous functions. It is one of the deepest reasons completeness matters.

### 7.5 Compactness in Metric Spaces

$K\subseteq X$ is **compact** if every open cover has a finite subcover. In metric spaces this is equivalent to: every sequence has a convergent subsequence (**sequential compactness**), which is equivalent to $K$ being complete and **totally bounded**. In $\mathbb{R}^n$ this reduces to **Heine–Borel**: closed and bounded $\iff$ compact. Compact sets inherit closedness, boundedness, and the extreme value property (a continuous real function attains its max and min).

## 8. Topology: Openness Without Distance

### 8.1 Definition

::: {#def-topology}
#### Definition 8.1 (Topological space)
A **topology** on a set $X$ is a family $\tau\subseteq\mathcal{P}(X)$ such that
1. $\varnothing\in\tau$ and $X\in\tau$;
2. $\tau$ is closed under **arbitrary unions**: if $\{U_i\}_{i\in I}\subseteq\tau$ then $\bigcup_i U_i\in\tau$;
3. $\tau$ is closed under **finite intersections**: if $U_1,\dots,U_n\in\tau$ then $\bigcap_{k=1}^n U_k\in\tau$.

The pair $(X,\tau)$ is a **topological space**; members of $\tau$ are **open sets**.
:::

Every metric space is a topological space via its metric topology (open sets as in Section 7.2), but not conversely (Section 8.5).

### 8.2 Examples of Topologies

| Topology | Open sets | Properties |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Discrete** | all subsets | finest possible; metrizable (discrete metric) |
| **Indiscrete** | $\{\varnothing,X\}$ | coarsest possible; not Hausdorff if $|X|>1$ |
| **Metric** | unions of balls | from any metric $d$ |
| **Cofinite** | $\varnothing$ and cofinite sets | $T_1$ but not Hausdorff |
| **Cocountable** | $\varnothing$ and cocountable sets | non-metrizable in general |
| **Order** | unions of intervals | on any linear order |
| **Sorgenfrey** | $[a,b)$ | finer than usual on $\mathbb{R}$; separable but not second-countable |
| **Zariski** | complements of algebraic sets | the topology of algebraic geometry |
| **Product** | unions of $\prod U_i$, finitely many proper | the topology of $X\times Y$ |
| **Quotient** | $U$ open iff preimage open | gluing constructions |

### 8.3 Bases, Closure, Interior, Boundary

- A **basis** $\mathcal{B}\subseteq\tau$ generates $\tau$ if every open set is a union of members of $\mathcal{B}$ (e.g. open balls in a metric space; open intervals in $\mathbb{R}$).
- A **subbasis** generates a basis by finite intersections.
- **Closure** $\overline{A}$: the smallest closed set containing $A$; $x\in\overline{A}$ iff every open neighborhood of $x$ meets $A$.
- **Interior** $A^\circ$: the largest open set contained in $A$.
- **Boundary** $\partial A = \overline{A}\setminus A^\circ$.
- $A$ is **dense** iff $\overline{A}=X$; **nowhere dense** iff $\overline{A}$ has empty interior.

In $\mathbb{R}$: $\overline{\mathbb{Q}}=\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbb{Q}^\circ=\varnothing$, $\partial\mathbb{Q}=\mathbb{R}$. The rationals are everywhere and yet occupy no open space — the precise sense of "dense but full of holes."

### 8.4 Continuity and Homeomorphism

::: {#def-continuous}
#### Definition 8.2 (Continuity)
$f:(X,\tau_X)\to(Y,\tau_Y)$ is **continuous** if $f^{-1}(V)\in\tau_X$ for every $V\in\tau_Y$ (preimages of open sets are open).
:::

Equivalent characterizations: $f(\overline{A})\subseteq\overline{f(A)}$; $f^{-1}(F)$ closed for closed $F$; continuity at each point via neighborhoods. For $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ this recovers the $\varepsilon$–$\delta$ definition with $\varepsilon,\delta$ replaced by arbitrary neighborhoods.

A **homeomorphism** is a bijective continuous map whose inverse is continuous: the notion of "same shape" for topological spaces. Properties preserved by homeomorphism (compactness, connectedness, Hausdorffness, cardinal invariants) are **topological invariants**. A classic illustration: any open interval is homeomorphic to $\mathbb{R}$ via $\tan$, even though it is bounded as a metric subspace.

### 8.5 Separation Axioms

| Axiom | Condition | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $T_0$ | points distinguished by some open set | Kolmogorov |
| $T_1$ | $\{x\}$ closed for all $x$ | points are closed |
| $T_2$ (**Hausdorff**) | disjoint neighborhoods for any two points | limits of sequences are unique |
| $T_3$ (regular) | point and closed set separated | — |
| $T_4$ (normal) | two disjoint closed sets separated | Urysohn / Tietze hold |

**Every metric space is Hausdorff** (take $r=d(x,y)/2$), which is why limits are unique there. Metrizability is characterized by the **Urysohn metrization theorem** (regular $T_1$ + second countable $\Rightarrow$ metrizable) and the **Nagata–Smirnov theorem**; hence a non-Hausdorff space, like the cofinite topology on an infinite set, is not metrizable.

### 8.6 Compactness, Connectedness, and the Limits of Sequences

- **Compactness:** a space is compact if every open cover has a finite subcover. Continuous images of compact sets are compact; a continuous real function on compact $K$ attains its bounds (extreme value theorem). **Tychonoff's theorem** says arbitrary products of compact spaces are compact — equivalent to the Axiom of Choice.
- **Connectedness:** a space is connected if it cannot be written as a union of two disjoint nonempty open sets. Continuous images of connected sets are connected; the connected subsets of $\mathbb{R}$ are exactly the intervals (Intermediate Value Theorem). Path-connectedness is stronger in general and equal for open subsets of $\mathbb{R}^n$.
- **Sequences are not enough in general topology.** In a first-countable space (e.g. any metric space) sequences detect closure and continuity, but in general one needs **nets** or **filters**. This is precisely the extra structure a metric supplies.

```text
        THE ASCENT OF STRUCTURE

   ordered set          (Q, <)          order, density, cuts
        │  + distance
        ▼
   metric space         (X, d)          balls, Cauchy, completeness,
        │                                compactness, Baire
        │  forget the metric, keep the open sets
        ▼
   topological space    (X, τ)          openness, continuity, homeomorphism,
                                         separation, compactness, connectedness
```

def is_topology(tau, X):
    Xs, tau = set(X), [frozenset(t) for t in tau]
    if frozenset() not in tau or Xs not in tau: return False
    for r in range(len(tau) + 1):
        for sub in itertools.combinations(tau, r):
            if frozenset().union(*sub) not in tau: return False
    if any((A & B) not in tau for A in tau for B in tau): return False

def rational_in(a, b, cap=200000):
    for q in range(1, cap + 1):
        p = int(a * q)
        while Fraction(p, q) <= a:
            p += 1
        if Fraction(p, q) < b:
            return Fraction(p, q)
    return None

def irrational_in(a, b):
