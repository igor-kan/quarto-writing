“The flapping of a butterfly’s wings in Brazil could set off a tornado in Texas.” — attributed to Edward Lorenz

A dynamical system is a rule for how a state evolves. When the rule is nonlinear, tiny differences in the initial condition can grow exponentially, so long-term prediction becomes impossible even though the system is fully deterministic. This is chaos, and it is everywhere: weather, ecosystems, fluids, circuits, orbits, populations.

1 What Is a Dynamical System?

A dynamical system consists of a state space \(M\) and an evolution rule:

  • Discrete-time (map): \(x_{n+1}=f(x_n)\), \(f:M\to M\). This is iteration.
  • Continuous-time (flow): \(\dot x=F(x)\), a differential equation; the solution is a curve \(\varphi_t(x_0)\).

The system is deterministic: the future is uniquely fixed by the present state. Determinism, however, does not imply predictability — that is the central lesson of chaos.

1.1 Fixed Points, Periodic Orbits, Stability

A fixed point \(x^\*\) satisfies \(f(x^\*)=x^\*\) (discrete) or \(F(x^\*)=0\) (continuous). Its stability is decided by linearization:

  • Discrete: the eigenvalue(s) of \(Df(x^\*)\); stable if all \(|\lambda|<1\); the multiplier.
  • Continuous: the eigenvalues of \(DF(x^\*)\); stable if all have negative real part.

A periodic orbit has \(f^p(x)=x\) for some \(p>0\); its stability is decided by the derivative of the \(p\)-th iterate, the multiplier \(\prod_{i=0}^{p-1}f'(x_i)\).

Bifurcations are qualitative changes as a parameter varies: saddle-node, transcritical, pitchfork, Hopf, and period-doubling.

2 The Logistic Map

The textbook discrete system is the logistic map

\[ x_{n+1}=r\,x_n(1-x_n),\qquad x\in[0,1],\ r\in[0,4], \]

a model of population with growth rate \(r\) and crowding.

2.1 Fixed Points and Stability

Nonzero fixed point: \(x^\*=1-1/r\) (for \(r>1\)). The multiplier is \(f'(x^\*)=2-r\), so:

  • \(0<r<1\): \(x^\*=0\) stable; population dies out.
  • \(1<r<3\): \(x^\*=1-1/r\) stable.
  • \(r=3\): period-doubling — the fixed point loses stability and a stable 2-cycle appears.
  • \(r=3.449\ldots\): the 2-cycle doubles to a 4-cycle.
  • Cascades continue at \(r=3.544, 3.564, \dots\) accumulating at \(r_\infty\approx3.5699\).

2.2 Period-Doubling and Feigenbaum Universality

The bifurcation points \(r_n\) converge geometrically with ratio

\[ \delta=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{r_{n}-r_{n-1}}{r_{n+1}-r_n}\approx4.6692, \]

the Feigenbaum constant. Astonishingly, \(\delta\) (and the scaling \(\alpha\approx2.5029\)) is universal: the same constants appear in any smooth unimodal map undergoing period-doubling — a deep statement about the geometry of bifurcations, not about the logistic map specifically.

Beyond \(r_\infty\), the dynamics is chaotic for most \(r\), punctuated by periodic windows (e.g. a stable 3-cycle near \(r\approx3.83\)).

3 Chaos: Sensitive Dependence

A map is chaotic (on an invariant set) if it has:

  1. Sensitive dependence on initial conditions — nearby states separate exponentially.
  2. Topological transitivity / mixing — the orbit wanders densely through the set.
  3. Dense periodic orbits.

The first is quantified by the Lyapunov exponent \[ \lambda=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac1n\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\ln|f'(x_i)|. \] \(\lambda>0\) means exponential divergence: an initial error \(\epsilon\) grows like \(\epsilon e^{\lambda n}\). Positive Lyapunov exponent is the formal signature of chaos; a predictability horizon follows: to predict \(T\) steps, the initial uncertainty must be below \(\sim e^{-\lambda T}\).

3.1 Stretching and Folding

Chaos requires two competing actions: stretching (which amplifies differences) and folding (which keeps the state bounded). The logistic map’s parabola does both. This is why chaotic attractors are fractal — infinitely layered sets of non-integer dimension.

4 The Butterfly Effect and the Lorenz System

Edward Lorenz (1963) studied a truncated model of atmospheric convection:

\[ \dot x=\sigma(y-x),\qquad \dot y=x(\rho-z)-y,\qquad \dot z=xy-\beta z, \]

with classical parameters \(\sigma=10\), \(\rho=28\), \(\beta=8/3\). The flow has a strange attractor — the “butterfly.” Two trajectories from almost identical initial conditions separate exponentially, so weather forecasts lose skill beyond roughly two weeks. Lorenz’s observation — that rounding a printed initial value from six digits to three changed a long-term forecast — became the butterfly effect.

The same phenomenon appears in the three-body problem (Poincaré, 1890): no general closed-form solution, and arbitrarily small changes produce qualitatively different orbits. Deterministic chaos is the norm in nonlinear systems, not the exception.

5 Attractors and Routes to Chaos

An attractor is a set toward which nearby trajectories converge:

Attractor Dimension Example
Fixed point 0 damped pendulum at rest
Limit cycle 1 Van der Pol oscillator, heartbeat
Torus integer quasiperiodic motion
Strange attractor fractal Lorenz, Rössler, logistic map

Routes to chaos (all observed in experiment):

  • Period-doubling (Feigenbaum): the logistic route; universal.
  • Intermittency (Pomeau–Manneville): long regular phases interrupted by chaotic bursts.
  • Quasiperiodicity (Ruelle–Takens–Newhouse): two/three incommensurate frequencies destabilize into a strange attractor.

Chaos links to ergodic theory (invariant measures, mixing, Birkhoff’s ergodic theorem), fractal geometry (Hausdorff dimension, the Mandelbrot and Julia sets of \(z\mapsto z^2+c\)), and information theory (positive entropy = information generation).

6 Why It Matters

  • Weather and climate: prediction horizons; ensemble forecasting.
  • Ecology and epidemiology: population cycles, tipping points.
  • Engineering: turbulence, laser dynamics, secure communication via chaos synchronization.
  • Physics: the KAM theorem and near-integrable systems; chaos in the solar system.
  • Computation: Newton’s method is itself a dynamical system — its fractal basins of attraction explain why some initial guesses fail. The Collatz map is another deterministic iteration whose global behavior resists proof.

The synthesis: nonlinearity plus iteration generates complexity; determinism and predictability are different properties.

def logistic_orbit(r, x0, n, discard=0):

7.2 Lyapunov Exponent

Code 
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def lyapunov(r, n=2000, discard=500):
    x = 0.4
    s = 0.0
    for i in range(n + discard):
        x = r * x * (1 - x)
        d = abs(r * (1 - 2 * x))
        if i >= discard and d > 0:
            s += np.log(d)
    return s / n

rs = np.linspace(2.5, 4.0, 800)
lam = np.array([lyapunov(r) for r in rs])
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(12, 4.6))
ax.plot(rs, lam, color="#333", lw=0.8)
ax.fill_between(rs, 0, lam, where=lam > 0, color="#e74c3c", alpha=0.6, label="chaotic (λ>0)")
ax.fill_between(rs, 0, lam, where=lam <= 0, color="#2980b9", alpha=0.4, label="periodic (λ<0)")
ax.axhline(0, color="k", lw=0.6)
ax.set_title("Lyapunov exponent of the logistic map")
ax.set_xlabel("r"); ax.set_ylabel(r"$\lambda$"); ax.legend(); ax.grid(alpha=0.3)
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

print("=== LYAPUNOV EXPONENT ===")
for r in [2.8, 3.2, 3.5, 3.83, 3.9, 4.0]:
    print(f"  r={r:<5}: lambda = {lyapunov(r):+.4f}  ({'chaotic' if lyapunov(r)>0 else 'periodic'})")
print("  r=3.83 is a periodic window (stable 3-cycle) inside the chaotic range.")

7.4 The Lorenz Attractor

Code 
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp

sigma, rho, beta = 10.0, 28.0, 8.0 / 3.0

def lorenz(t, s):
    x, y, z = s
    return [sigma * (y - x), x * (rho - z) - y, x * y - beta * z]

t = np.linspace(0, 40, 20000)
sol = solve_ivp(lorenz, [0, 40], [1.0, 1.0, 1.0], t_eval=t, rtol=1e-9, atol=1e-9)
sol2 = solve_ivp(lorenz, [0, 40], [1.0 + 1e-8, 1.0, 1.0], t_eval=t, rtol=1e-9, atol=1e-9)
dist = np.linalg.norm(sol.y - sol2.y, axis=0)

fig = plt.figure(figsize=(13, 5.5))
ax1 = fig.add_subplot(1, 2, 1, projection="3d")
ax1.plot(sol.y[0], sol.y[1], sol.y[2], lw=0.5, color="#2980b9")
ax1.set_title("Lorenz strange attractor"); ax1.set_xlabel("x"); ax1.set_ylabel("y"); ax1.set_zlabel("z")

ax2 = fig.add_subplot(1, 2, 2)
ax2.semilogy(t, dist, color="#e74c3c")
ax2.set_title(r"Separation of two orbits ($\Delta x_0=10^{-8}$)")
ax2.set_xlabel("time"); ax2.set_ylabel("separation"); ax2.grid(alpha=0.3, which="both")
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

print("=== LORENZ SEPARATION ===")
for k in [0, 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30]:
    print(f"  t={k:>2}: separation = {dist[np.argmin(abs(t - k))]:.3e}")
print("  Exponential divergence from a 1e-8 error -> finite predictability horizon.")

The laboratory makes chaos visible and quantitative: a single parameter sweeps the system from order to chaos (bifurcation diagram); the Lyapunov exponent separates the two regimes; infinitesimal differences explode (butterfly effect); and the Lorenz system shows a bounded, non-repeating, fractal attractor.

8 Summary

\[ \begin{aligned} &\textbf{Dynamical system: } && \text{state space + evolution } x_{n+1}=f(x_n) \text{ or } \dot x=F(x). \\[3pt] &\textbf{Fixed points: } && \text{stability via } |f'(x^*)|<1 \text{ or } \operatorname{Re}\lambda<0. \\[3pt] &\textbf{Logistic map: } && \text{fixed point} \to \text{period-doubling} \to \text{chaos};\\ &&& r_\infty=3.5699,\ \text{Feigenbaum } \delta=4.6692 \text{ (universal).} \\[3pt] &\textbf{Chaos: } && \text{sensitive dependence, mixing, dense periodic orbits; } \lambda>0. \\[3pt] &\textbf{Butterfly effect: } && \text{Lorenz; exponential error growth; predictability horizon.} \\[3pt] &\textbf{Attractors: } && \text{fixed point, limit cycle, torus, strange (fractal) attractor.} \\[3pt] &\textbf{Routes: } && \text{period-doubling, intermittency, quasiperiodicity.} \\[3pt] &\textbf{Lesson: } && \text{deterministic} \ne \text{predictable.} \end{aligned} \]

Chaos is not a failure of mathematics but a feature of nonlinearity. The same iteration that makes the Collatz conjecture intractable makes weather unpredictable and gives fractals their infinite complexity — and it is why iterative numerical methods must be analyzed, not just run.

## What Is a Dynamical System?

A **dynamical system** consists of a **state space** $M$ and an **evolution rule**:

- **Discrete-time (map):** $x_{n+1}=f(x_n)$, $f:M\to M$. This is iteration.
- **Continuous-time (flow):** $\dot x=F(x)$, a differential equation; the solution is a curve $\varphi_t(x_0)$.

The system is **deterministic**: the future is uniquely fixed by the present state. Determinism, however, does **not** imply predictability — that is the central lesson of chaos.

### Fixed Points, Periodic Orbits, Stability

A **fixed point** $x^\*$ satisfies $f(x^\*)=x^\*$ (discrete) or $F(x^\*)=0$ (continuous). Its **stability** is decided by linearization:

- Discrete: the eigenvalue(s) of $Df(x^\*)$; stable if all $|\lambda|<1$; the **multiplier**.
- Continuous: the eigenvalues of $DF(x^\*)$; stable if all have negative real part.

A **periodic orbit** has $f^p(x)=x$ for some $p>0$; its stability is decided by the derivative of the $p$-th iterate, the **multiplier** $\prod_{i=0}^{p-1}f'(x_i)$.

**Bifurcations** are qualitative changes as a parameter varies: saddle-node, transcritical, pitchfork, Hopf, and period-doubling.

## Why It Matters

- **Weather and climate:** prediction horizons; ensemble forecasting.
- **Ecology and epidemiology:** population cycles, tipping points.
- **Engineering:** turbulence, laser dynamics, secure communication via chaos synchronization.
- **Physics:** the KAM theorem and near-integrable systems; chaos in the solar system.
- **Computation:** Newton's method is itself a dynamical system — its fractal **basins of attraction** explain why some initial guesses fail. The [Collatz map](collatz-conjecture-3x-plus-1-orbits-and-similar-iterations.qmd) is another deterministic iteration whose global behavior resists proof.

The synthesis: **nonlinearity plus iteration generates complexity; determinism and predictability are different properties.**

## Laboratory: Seeing Chaos

### The Logistic Map: Fixed Points to Chaos

```{python}
#| label: fig-logistic
#| fig-cap: "The logistic map x_{n+1}=r x_n(1−x_n). Left: trajectories for increasing r, showing convergence to a fixed point (r=2.8), a 2-cycle (r=3.2), a 4-cycle (r=3.5), and chaos (r=3.9). Right: the bifurcation diagram — for each r the map's attracting set is plotted, revealing the period-doubling cascade to chaos, the Feigenbaum accumulation point, and periodic windows embedded in the chaotic region."

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def logistic_orbit(r, x0, n, discard=0):
    xs = []
    x = x0
    for i in range(n + discard):
        x = r * x * (1 - x)
        if i >= discard:
            xs.append(x)
    return np.array(xs)

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(14, 5))
for r, col in [(2.8, "#2980b9"), (3.2, "#27ae60"), (3.5, "#e67e22"), (3.9, "#e74c3c")]:
    axes[0].plot(logistic_orbit(r, 0.4, 60), "o-", ms=3, color=col, label=f"r={r}")
axes[0].set_title("Logistic map trajectories")
axes[0].set_xlabel("n"); axes[0].set_ylabel(r"$x_n$"); axes[0].legend(); axes[0].grid(alpha=0.3)

rs = np.linspace(2.5, 4.0, 1200)
for r in rs:
    xs = logistic_orbit(r, 0.4, 300, discard=200)
    axes[1].plot([r] * len(xs), xs, ",", color="#2980b9", alpha=0.25)
axes[1].set_title("Bifurcation diagram")
axes[1].set_xlabel("r"); axes[1].set_ylabel(r"attracting $x$"); axes[1].grid(alpha=0.3)
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

r_inf = 3.5699456
print("=== LOGISTIC MAP BIFURCATIONS ===")
print(f"  fixed point stable : 1 < r < 3")
print(f"  2-cycle starts     : r = 3")
print(f"  cascade accumulates: r_inf = {r_inf} (Feigenbaum)")
print(f"  first three doubling points: 3, 3.449, 3.544")
print(f"  Feigenbaum delta   = 4.669201609...  (universal)")
```

### Lyapunov Exponent

```{python}
#| label: fig-lyapunov
#| fig-cap: "The Lyapunov exponent of the logistic map versus r. Where λ > 0 (red) the dynamics is chaotic and nearby trajectories diverge exponentially; where λ < 0 (blue) it converges to a periodic orbit. The sharp downward spikes are the periodic windows inside the chaotic region. Positive Lyapunov exponent is the quantitative definition of sensitive dependence."

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def lyapunov(r, n=2000, discard=500):
    x = 0.4
    s = 0.0
    for i in range(n + discard):
        x = r * x * (1 - x)
        d = abs(r * (1 - 2 * x))
        if i >= discard and d > 0:
            s += np.log(d)
    return s / n

rs = np.linspace(2.5, 4.0, 800)
lam = np.array([lyapunov(r) for r in rs])
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(12, 4.6))
ax.plot(rs, lam, color="#333", lw=0.8)
ax.fill_between(rs, 0, lam, where=lam > 0, color="#e74c3c", alpha=0.6, label="chaotic (λ>0)")
ax.fill_between(rs, 0, lam, where=lam <= 0, color="#2980b9", alpha=0.4, label="periodic (λ<0)")
ax.axhline(0, color="k", lw=0.6)
ax.set_title("Lyapunov exponent of the logistic map")
ax.set_xlabel("r"); ax.set_ylabel(r"$\lambda$"); ax.legend(); ax.grid(alpha=0.3)
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

print("=== LYAPUNOV EXPONENT ===")
for r in [2.8, 3.2, 3.5, 3.83, 3.9, 4.0]:
    print(f"  r={r:<5}: lambda = {lyapunov(r):+.4f}  ({'chaotic' if lyapunov(r)>0 else 'periodic'})")
print("  r=3.83 is a periodic window (stable 3-cycle) inside the chaotic range.")
```

### Sensitivity to Initial Conditions (Butterfly Effect)

```{python}
#| label: fig-sensitivity
#| fig-cap: "Sensitive dependence in the logistic map at r=3.9. Two trajectories starting 10^-6 apart track each other closely for about twenty steps, then diverge completely — the hallmark of chaos. The separation grows exponentially at the Lyapunov rate until it saturates. This is why long-term prediction is impossible even though the rule is exact."

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

r = 3.9
x0, x1 = 0.4, 0.4 + 1e-6
n = 50
traj0, traj1 = logistic_orbit(r, x0, n), logistic_orbit(r, x1, n)
sep = np.abs(traj0 - traj1)

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(10, 4.8))
ax.plot(traj0, "o-", ms=3, color="#2980b9", label=r"$x_0=0.4$")
ax.plot(traj1, "s-", ms=3, color="#e67e22", label=r"$x_0=0.4+10^{-6}$")
ax.set_title("Butterfly effect: initial separation 10⁻⁶, r=3.9")
ax.set_xlabel("step"); ax.set_ylabel(r"$x_n$"); ax.legend(); ax.grid(alpha=0.3)
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

print("=== DIVERGENCE ===")
for k in [0, 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30]:
    print(f"  step {k:>2}: separation = {sep[k]:.3e}")
print(f"  Lyapunov estimate from growth: {np.polyfit(range(1,15), np.log(sep[1:15]), 1)[0]:.3f}")
print("  (separation saturates once it reaches the size of the attractor.)")
```

### The Lorenz Attractor

```{python}
#| label: fig-lorenz
#| fig-cap: "The Lorenz system. Left: the butterfly-shaped strange attractor traced by a single trajectory; the flow is bounded but never repeats, and its fractal geometry is visible. Right: two trajectories from initial conditions differing by 10^-8 separate exponentially, until they occupy different lobes of the attractor — Lorenz's original demonstration that deterministic weather is inherently unpredictable in the long run."

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp

sigma, rho, beta = 10.0, 28.0, 8.0 / 3.0

def lorenz(t, s):
    x, y, z = s
    return [sigma * (y - x), x * (rho - z) - y, x * y - beta * z]

t = np.linspace(0, 40, 20000)
sol = solve_ivp(lorenz, [0, 40], [1.0, 1.0, 1.0], t_eval=t, rtol=1e-9, atol=1e-9)
sol2 = solve_ivp(lorenz, [0, 40], [1.0 + 1e-8, 1.0, 1.0], t_eval=t, rtol=1e-9, atol=1e-9)
dist = np.linalg.norm(sol.y - sol2.y, axis=0)

fig = plt.figure(figsize=(13, 5.5))
ax1 = fig.add_subplot(1, 2, 1, projection="3d")
ax1.plot(sol.y[0], sol.y[1], sol.y[2], lw=0.5, color="#2980b9")
ax1.set_title("Lorenz strange attractor"); ax1.set_xlabel("x"); ax1.set_ylabel("y"); ax1.set_zlabel("z")

ax2 = fig.add_subplot(1, 2, 2)
ax2.semilogy(t, dist, color="#e74c3c")
ax2.set_title(r"Separation of two orbits ($\Delta x_0=10^{-8}$)")
ax2.set_xlabel("time"); ax2.set_ylabel("separation"); ax2.grid(alpha=0.3, which="both")
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

print("=== LORENZ SEPARATION ===")
for k in [0, 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30]:
    print(f"  t={k:>2}: separation = {dist[np.argmin(abs(t - k))]:.3e}")
print("  Exponential divergence from a 1e-8 error -> finite predictability horizon.")
```

The laboratory makes chaos visible and quantitative: a single parameter sweeps the system from order to chaos (bifurcation diagram); the Lyapunov exponent separates the two regimes; infinitesimal differences explode (butterfly effect); and the Lorenz system shows a bounded, non-repeating, fractal attractor.

## Summary

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\textbf{Dynamical system: } && \text{state space + evolution } x_{n+1}=f(x_n) \text{ or } \dot x=F(x). \\[3pt]
&\textbf{Fixed points: } && \text{stability via } |f'(x^*)|<1 \text{ or } \operatorname{Re}\lambda<0. \\[3pt]
&\textbf{Logistic map: } && \text{fixed point} \to \text{period-doubling} \to \text{chaos};\\ &&& r_\infty=3.5699,\ \text{Feigenbaum } \delta=4.6692 \text{ (universal).} \\[3pt]
&\textbf{Chaos: } && \text{sensitive dependence, mixing, dense periodic orbits; } \lambda>0. \\[3pt]
&\textbf{Butterfly effect: } && \text{Lorenz; exponential error growth; predictability horizon.} \\[3pt]
&\textbf{Attractors: } && \text{fixed point, limit cycle, torus, strange (fractal) attractor.} \\[3pt]
&\textbf{Routes: } && \text{period-doubling, intermittency, quasiperiodicity.} \\[3pt]
&\textbf{Lesson: } && \text{deterministic} \ne \text{predictable.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Chaos is not a failure of mathematics but a feature of nonlinearity. The same iteration that makes the [Collatz conjecture](collatz-conjecture-3x-plus-1-orbits-and-similar-iterations.qmd) intractable makes weather unpredictable and gives fractals their infinite complexity — and it is why [iterative numerical methods](iterative-methods-and-computing-constants-pi-and-eulers-constant.qmd) must be analyzed, not just run.
