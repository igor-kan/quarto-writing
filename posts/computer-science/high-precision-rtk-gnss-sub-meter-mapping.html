1. The Physical Limitations of Standard GPS

For everyday consumer navigation—such as driving with Google Maps or tracking a morning run with a smartwatch—standard GPS provides positional accuracy between 3 and 10 meters. Under an open sky with an unobstructed view of the horizon, a modern dual-frequency smartphone might narrow this window down to approximately 2 to 3 meters.

However, in applications such as civil engineering, precision agriculture, boundary surveying, asset management, and cemetery mapping, a 3-meter margin of error is unusable. A 3-meter circle can mean the difference between one property boundary and another, or between digging up an empty patch of grass versus puncturing a high-pressure fiber-optic line.

To locate a single chair in a park, a specific boundary stake, or an unmarked historic gravestone, positioning must reach centimeter-level precision (\(< 2 ext{ cm}\)). Achieving this requires stepping beyond standard GPS into multi-constellation GNSS (Global Navigation Satellite Systems) and carrier-phase differential augmentation technologies like RTK (Real-Time Kinematic).

1.1 Why Standard GPS Drifts

Satellite navigation relies on trilateration. Satellites orbit approximately 20,200 kilometers above the Earth, constantly broadcasting radio signals containing atomic timestamp data and orbital ephemeris parameters. A receiver calculates its distance (pseudorange) to each satellite by measuring the time elapsed between transmission and reception:

\[ ho = c \cdot \Delta t\]

Where \(c\) is the speed of light and \(\Delta t\) is the transit duration. In practice, multiple physical phenomena degrade this calculation: 1. Ionospheric and Tropospheric Delay: Charged particles in the ionosphere and water vapor in the troposphere refract the radio wavefront, altering signal velocity. 2. Satellite Clock and Ephemeris Errors: Slight atomic clock drifts aboard satellites and subtle gravitational orbital variations. 3. Multi-path Interference: Signals bounce off nearby skyscrapers, walls, vehicles, or wet ground before entering the antenna, creating artificial phase delays. 4. Code-Phase vs. Carrier-Phase Limitations: Standard GPS tracks the coarse pseudo-random binary code modulated onto the carrier frequency. Because the modulation bits are relatively wide (\(pprox 300 ext{ meters}\) for the C/A code), signal correlation can only achieve meter-scale resolution.

2. The Multi-Constellation Umbrella: GNSS vs. GPS

While “GPS” is commonly used as a generic term, it refers specifically to the NAVSTAR GPS constellation operated by the United States Space Force. The modern professional standard is GNSS (Global Navigation Satellite System), which integrates signals across four major global constellations:

  • GPS (United States): ~31 operational satellites across 6 orbital planes.
  • GLONASS (Russia): ~24 operational satellites using FDMA/CDMA signals.
  • BeiDou (BDS, China): ~45 satellites in MEO, GEO, and IGSO orbits, providing dense Asian-Pacific and global coverage.
  • Galileo (European Union): ~30 satellites engineered specifically for civilian navigation, timing, and search-and-rescue.

For an overview of multi-constellation advantages in modern survey workflows, see the Emlid Guide to GNSS vs GPS and SEP Geospatial on Multi-Constellation Performance.

2.1 The European Galileo Advantage and HAS (High Accuracy Service)

Unlike early GPS, Europe’s Galileo system was designed from inception for civil precision: * Standard Open Service: Provides horizontal positioning accuracy under 1 meter under clear skies. * Galileo HAS (High Accuracy Service): Launched globally in 2023, Galileo HAS broadcasts free Precise Point Positioning (PPP) corrections directly via the Galileo E6-B signal (1278.75 MHz) and through internet IP streams. * Zero Base-Station Requirement: Galileo HAS delivers horizontal accuracy better than 20 centimeters without requiring a local physical base station or a paid third-party subscription, revolutionizing drone photogrammetry and robotic transport.

For technical specifications, visit the European Commission Galileo Space Policy Portal.

3.1 Carrier-Phase Tracking & Ambiguity Resolution

Rather than measuring the coarse binary code modulated on the satellite signal, an RTK receiver locks directly onto the carrier wave itself (such as the GPS L1 signal at 1575.42 MHz, which has a physical wavelength of \(\lambda pprox 19.05 ext{ cm}\)).

By counting the integer number of whole wave cycles between the satellite and the antenna (known as integer ambiguity resolution) and measuring the fractional phase angle down to a fraction of a millimeter, the system achieves millimeter-scale geometric resolution.

3.2 The Base-Rover Architecture

Because the base station and the rover receiver are located relatively close to one another (typically within 10 to 30 kilometers), the atmospheric disturbances affecting the satellite signals traveling through the ionosphere and troposphere are virtually identical for both units:

  1. The Base Station: Placed over a precisely known geodetic benchmark, the base calculates the exact instantaneous discrepancy between its known physical coordinates and the incoming satellite measurements.
  2. Correction Stream (RTCM): The base broadcasts this error correction vector in real time using the RTCM (Radio Technical Commission for Maritime Services) protocol. Transmission occurs via:
    • Local LoRa / UHF Radio: Useful in remote wilderness areas without cellular coverage.
    • NTRIP (Networked Transport of RTCM via Internet Protocol): Streaming corrections over 4G/5G mobile networks from fixed public or private CORS (Continuously Operating Reference Station) networks.
  3. The Rover: Receives both raw satellite signals and the RTCM correction packet from the base, cancels out atmospheric errors, and locks onto a “FIX” solution—yielding 1 to 2 cm real-time accuracy.

For a detailed exploration of differential corrections and error vectors, see marXact on Standalone vs. Differential vs. RTK and YSI on GNSS Relative Error Vectors.

4. Field Workflow: Mapping a Single Chair or Gravestone

To reliably navigate back to a specific outdoor object—such as a commemorative park chair or a century-old cemetery headstone—a surveyor combines an RTK receiver with a mobile geospatial application.

4.1 Step-by-Step Procedure

  1. Equipment Setup: Mount an RTK GNSS receiver (such as an Emlid Reach RX / RS3 or Bad Elf Flex) onto a calibrated 2-meter carbon-fiber survey pole equipped with a leveling bubble.
  2. Establish NTRIP Link: Connect the receiver via Bluetooth to a smartphone and connect to an NTRIP caster (such as a state DOT CORS network, HxGN SmartNet, or Emlid Caster).
  3. Verify RTK Status: Wait for the status indicator to transition from Single (\(\sim 3 ext{m}\)) to Float (\(\sim 30 ext{cm}\)) and finally to Fix (\(< 2 ext{cm}\)).
  4. Point Logging & Stakeout: Place the pole tip directly against the base of the chair leg or gravestone. Record the point with metadata (e.g., historical name, plot number, condition assessment, photographic record).
  5. Stakeout Navigation: When returning months or years later, the mapping app’s stakeout mode directs the operator with real-time directional arrows until the pole tip is within millimeters of the target.

4.2 Subsurface Discovery: Ground-Penetrating Radar (GPR)

In historical cemeteries where headstones have sunk beneath the turf or wooden markers have decayed, GNSS alone cannot locate the buried remains. Professionals pair RTK GNSS with Ground-Penetrating Radar (GPR).

The GPR cart rolls across the terrain, sending electromagnetic radar pulses into the soil and recording dielectric reflections from subsurface soil disturbances (grave shafts, burial vaults, or tree roots). By linking the GPR antenna directly to an RTK receiver, every subsurface anomaly is automatically tagged with centimeter-accurate 3D coordinates, allowing the creation of complete digital underground cemetery maps.
