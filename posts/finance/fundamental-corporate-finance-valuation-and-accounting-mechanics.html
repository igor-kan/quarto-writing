1 1. Introduction: The Engine of Corporate Capital

At its core, a corporation is a legal entity designed to solve a fundamental economic problem: aggregating capital from disparate investors to deploy into productive commercial assets that generate returns in excess of their cost of capital.

To analyze, value, and understand any business—from early-stage software ventures to capital-intensive industrial giants—one must master three interconnected domains: 1. Equity & Share Mechanics: How ownership is divided, diluted, split, and returned via dividends and repurchases. 2. The Accounting Architecture: How the three financial statements (Income Statement, Balance Sheet, Cash Flow Statement) record economic activity, and why metrics like EBITDA exist. 3. Valuation & Cost of Capital: How Enterprise Value (\(EV\)), Free Cash Flow (\(FCF\)), Multiples (\(EV/EBITDA\), \(P/E\)), and Discounted Cash Flow (\(DCF\)) establish intrinsic worth.

2 2. Shares, Equity Ownership, and Capital Stock Mechanics

2.1 2.1 What is a Share of Stock?

A share is a discrete unit of fractional ownership in a corporation. Under corporate law, holding common stock confers three primary rights: 1. Voting Rights: The right to vote on the board of directors and major corporate matters (mergers, acquisitions, charter amendments). 2. Residual Claim on Cash Flows: The right to receive discretionary dividends when declared by the board. 3. Residual Claim on Assets: In the event of bankruptcy or liquidation, equity holders are entitled to all assets remaining only after all debt holders, vendors, tax authorities, and preferred shareholders have been satisfied in full.

2.2 2.2 Common Stock vs. Preferred Stock

Corporations issue distinct classes of equity with differing risk-return profiles:

Dimension Common Stock (Ordinary Shares) Preferred Stock (Preference Shares)
Voting Rights Full voting power (typically 1 vote per share, or multi-class like Class A vs Class B). Typically non-voting, except under specific default triggers.
Dividend Priority Subordinated to preferred dividends; discretionary and variable. Senior to common; often fixed percentage of par value (\(D_{\text{pref}} = \delta \times \text{Par}\)).
Cumulative Dividends Never cumulative; unpaid dividends are forfeited. Often cumulative: unpaid dividends accrue in arrears and must be paid before common dividends.
Liquidation Priority Lowest priority (absolute residual claim). Senior to common stock; subordinated to debt.
Convertibility Standard equity. Frequently convertible into common shares at a predetermined ratio.

2.3 2.3 The Capital Stock Spectrum: Authorized, Issued, Treasury, and Outstanding

A corporation’s equity structure is governed by a precise hierarchy of share counts:

[ Authorized Shares ] (Maximum allowable by Corporate Charter)
   │
   ├── [ Unissued Shares ] (Reserved for future financing, options, acquisitions)
   │
   └── [ Issued Shares ] (Total shares actually created and issued)
          │
          ├── [ Treasury Shares ] (Shares repurchased by company; held in corporate vault;
          │                        non-voting, pay no dividends)
          │
          └── [ Outstanding Shares ] (Shares held by all external shareholders)
                 │
                 ├── [ Restricted Shares / Insider Holdings ] (Founders, executives)
                 │
                 └── [ Public Float ] (Shares freely tradable on open public markets)

\[\mathbf{\text{Shares Outstanding} = \text{Issued Shares} - \text{Treasury Shares}}\] \[\mathbf{\text{Market Capitalization} = \text{Share Price} \times \text{Shares Outstanding}}\]

2.4 2.4 Corporate Actions: Splits, Dilution, and Repurchases

2.4.1 Forward and Reverse Stock Splits

A stock split changes the nominal number of shares outstanding without altering the proportional ownership, assets, earnings, or aggregate market capitalization of the firm.

  • Forward Split (e.g., \(N\)-for-\(1\)): Each existing share is exchanged for \(N\) new shares. \[\text{New Shares} = \text{Old Shares} \times N, \quad \text{New Price} = \frac{\text{Old Price}}{N}\] Example: A \(2\)-for-\(1\) split of a company trading at $200$ with \(1{,}000{,}000\) shares yields \(2{,}000{,}000\) shares at $100\(. The market capitalization remains \$200{,}000{,}000\).

  • Reverse Split (e.g., \(1\)-for-\(M\)): Used to consolidate low-priced shares (e.g., to prevent delisting below $1.00$ minimum bid rules on NASDAQ/NYSE). \[\text{New Shares} = \frac{\text{Old Shares}}{M}, \quad \text{New Price} = \text{Old Price} \times M\]

2.4.2 Share Dilution & Basic vs. Diluted EPS

Dilution occurs when a company issues new shares—through secondary equity offerings, employee stock-based compensation (SBC), stock option exercises, or convertible bond conversions. While the company may receive cash, the existing shareholders’ fractional ownership of future earnings decreases.

To reflect this, public companies must report two earnings metrics:

  1. Basic Earnings Per Share (Basic EPS): \[\text{Basic EPS} = \frac{\text{Net Income} - \text{Preferred Dividends}}{\text{Weighted Average Common Shares Outstanding}}\]

  2. Diluted Earnings Per Share (Diluted EPS): Calculated using the Treasury Stock Method for options and warrants, and the If-Converted Method for convertible bonds: \[\text{Diluted Shares} = \text{Basic Shares} + \text{In-the-Money Options} \times \left(1 - \frac{\text{Strike Price}}{\text{Market Price}}\right) + \text{Convertible Securities Shares}\]

2.4.3 Share Repurchases (Stock Buybacks)

When a firm uses excess cash to purchase its own shares on the open market or via tender offers: * The repurchased shares become Treasury Stock (reducing shares outstanding). * Direct Consequences: 1. Cash on the balance sheet decreases; Treasury Stock (a contra-equity account) increases, reducing total shareholders’ equity. 2. Because the denominator (shares outstanding) decreases, Earnings Per Share (EPS) increases for any given net income. 3. Proportional ownership for remaining shareholders increases automatically without requiring new cash outlay. 4. Tax Advantage: Unlike cash dividends, which are taxable immediately upon distribution, share buybacks allow shareholders to defer taxes until they sell their appreciated shares.

3 3. Dividends and Capital Return Policy

3.1 3.1 The Dividend Lifecycle and Timeline

Dividends are cash or stock distributions paid out of accumulated corporate earnings (retained earnings). The execution of a dividend follows four strict dates:

Declaration Date ────► Ex-Dividend Date ────► Record Date ────► Payment Date
  (Board declares         (Cut-off date for       (Company logs     (Cash credited
  amount & schedule)       new share buyers)       shareholders)     to accounts)
  1. Declaration Date: The board of directors formally votes and announces the dividend amount per share.
  2. Ex-Dividend Date: The first date on which the stock trades without the right to receive the declared dividend.
    • The Mechanical Price Adjustment: In an efficient market, the stock price drops at market open on the ex-dividend date by approximately the exact pre-tax dividend amount: \[P_{\text{ex}} \approx P_{\text{cum}} - \text{Dividend}\]
    • If an investor buys a share on or after the ex-dividend date, the seller receives the dividend, not the buyer.
  3. Record Date: Typically one business day after the ex-dividend date (governed by \(T+1\) settlement rules). The company records the names of all registered shareholders entitled to the dividend.
  4. Payment Date: The date the company actually deposits funds into shareholder brokerage accounts.

3.2 3.2 Core Dividend Metrics

\[\mathbf{\text{Dividend Yield} = \frac{\text{Annual Dividend Per Share (DPS)}}{\text{Current Share Price } P_0}}\]

\[\mathbf{\text{Dividend Payout Ratio} = \frac{\text{Total Dividends Paid}}{\text{Net Income}} = \frac{\text{DPS}}{\text{EPS}}}\]

\[\mathbf{\text{Retention Ratio } (b) = 1 - \text{Dividend Payout Ratio} = \frac{\text{Retained Earnings Added}}{\text{Net Income}}}\]

The retention ratio dictates the firm’s Sustainable Growth Rate (\(SGR\))—the maximum rate at which earnings can grow without issuing new external equity or altering leverage: \[\mathbf{SGR = b \times \text{ROE} = (1 - \text{Payout Ratio}) \times \text{ROE}}\]

3.3 3.3 The Gordon Growth Model (Dividend Discount Model)

If a firm pays a regular dividend that grows at a constant perpetual rate \(g\), the intrinsic value of the share \(P_0\) is the discounted sum of all future dividend cash flows:

\[P_0 = \sum_{t=1}^\infty \frac{D_t}{(1 + r)^t} = \sum_{t=1}^\infty \frac{D_0 (1 + g)^t}{(1 + r)^t}\]

Evaluating the infinite geometric series (under the necessary convergence condition \(r > g\)):

\[\mathbf{P_0 = \frac{D_1}{r - g} = \frac{D_0 (1 + g)}{r - g}}\]

where: * \(D_0\): Most recent annual dividend paid. * \(D_1\): Expected dividend in the upcoming year (\(D_1 = D_0(1+g)\)). * \(r\): Cost of equity (investor’s required rate of return). * \(g\): Perpetual constant dividend growth rate.

4.1 4.1 The Income Statement: Top Line to Bottom Line

The Income Statement (Profit & Loss / P&L) measures profitability over a specified period (quarterly 10-Q, annual 10-K).

4.1.1 Step-by-Step Anatomy

  1. Revenue (Top Line / Gross Sales): Total dollar value of goods sold and services delivered to customers before any expenses are deducted.
  2. Cost of Goods Sold (COGS): Direct costs attributable to the production of goods or services sold (raw materials, direct manufacturing labor, cloud hosting server costs for software).
  3. Gross Profit: \[\text{Gross Profit} = \text{Revenue} - \text{COGS}, \quad \text{Gross Margin} = \frac{\text{Gross Profit}}{\text{Revenue}}\]
  4. Operating Expenses (OpEx): Indirect costs required to run the enterprise:
    • SG&A: Selling, General, and Administrative expenses (sales commissions, marketing campaigns, executive salaries, office leases, legal fees).
    • R&D: Research and Development (engineering payroll, software development).
  5. Operating Income / EBIT (Earnings Before Interest and Taxes): \[\mathbf{\text{EBIT} = \text{Gross Profit} - \text{OpEx}}\] EBIT measures the operating earning power of the firm’s core assets, independent of how the assets were financed (debt vs. equity) and independent of the corporate tax jurisdiction.

4.2 4.2 Demystifying EBITDA

EBITDA stands for Earnings Before Interest, Taxes, Depreciation, and Amortization:

\[\mathbf{\text{EBITDA} = \text{Operating Income (EBIT)} + \text{Depreciation} + \text{Amortization}}\]

Equivalently, working from the bottom line upwards: \[\text{EBITDA} = \text{Net Income} + \text{Taxes} + \text{Interest Expense} + \text{Depreciation} + \text{Amortization}\]

4.2.1 Why Do Financial Analysts and Private Equity Use EBITDA?

  1. Capital Structure Neutrality: By adding back interest expense, EBITDA isolates operational profitability from whether the company is financed \(100\%\) with equity or \(90\%\) with junk debt.
  2. Tax Neutrality: Adds back tax expense, allowing direct operational comparisons between a company operating in California vs. Ireland vs. Singapore.
  3. Non-Cash Charge Neutrality: Depreciation (for physical assets like factories and vehicles) and Amortization (for acquired intangible assets like patents and customer lists) are historical accounting allocations of past capital outlays. Adding them back provides a crude proxy for pre-tax operating cash flow.

4.2.2 The Dark Side of EBITDA: Buffett’s Critique

EBITDA is frequently abused by management to mask unprofitability (via “Adjusted EBITDA”, where stock-based compensation, restructuring fees, and acquisition costs are added back).

As Warren Buffett famously warned: > “People who use EBITDA are either trying to con you or they’re conning themselves… Does management think the tooth fairy pays for capital expenditures?”

Depreciation is not a phantom expense; machines rust, servers become obsolete, and trucks break down. If a company must spend $50$ each year on Capital Expenditures (CapEx) just to replace worn-out machinery, treating EBITDA as disposable cash flow is a fatal valuation error!

4.3 4.3 The Balance Sheet: The Conservation Law of Assets

The Balance Sheet represents a static snapshot of the company’s financial position at an exact instant in time (e.g., midnight on December 31).

\[\mathbf{\text{Assets} = \text{Liabilities} + \text{Shareholders' Equity}}\]

  • Assets (What the firm owns):
    • Current Assets (\(< 1\) year): Cash and cash equivalents, short-term marketable securities, Accounts Receivable (invoiced revenue not yet collected), Inventory.
    • Non-Current Assets (\(> 1\) year): Property, Plant & Equipment (PP&E, net of accumulated depreciation), Intangibles (patents, software licenses), Goodwill (premium paid in past acquisitions over net identifiable assets).
  • Liabilities (What the firm owes):
    • Current Liabilities (\(< 1\) year): Accounts Payable (bills owed to suppliers), Accrued Expenses, Short-term Debt, Deferred Revenue (cash collected before services are delivered).
    • Non-Current Liabilities (\(> 1\) year): Long-term Debt (corporate bonds, bank term loans), Pension obligations, Deferred Tax Liabilities.
  • Shareholders’ Equity (Net Worth / Book Value): \[\text{Shareholders' Equity} = \text{Assets} - \text{Liabilities} = \text{Common Stock at Par} + \text{APIC} + \text{Retained Earnings} - \text{Treasury Stock}\]

4.3.1 Net Working Capital (NWC)

\[\mathbf{\text{Net Working Capital (NWC)} = \text{Current Assets} - \text{Current Liabilities}}\] Operating Working Capital measures the short-term capital locked up in day-to-day operations: \[\text{Operating NWC} = (\text{Accounts Receivable} + \text{Inventory}) - \text{Accounts Payable}\]

4.4 4.4 The Statement of Cash Flows: Cash is King

While the Income Statement is governed by accrual accounting (revenue is recognized when earned, not when cash is received), the Cash Flow Statement tracks the physical movement of currency through bank accounts across three buckets:

  1. Cash Flow from Operating Activities (CFO): \[\text{CFO} = \text{Net Income} + \text{D&A} - \Delta \text{NWC} + \text{Other Non-Cash Adjustments}\]
  2. Cash Flow from Investing Activities (CFI): \[\text{CFI} = -\text{Capital Expenditures (CapEx)} - \text{Acquisitions} + \text{Asset Sales}\]
  3. Cash Flow from Financing Activities (CFF): \[\text{CFF} = \text{Debt Issuance} - \text{Debt Repayment} + \text{Equity Issuance} - \text{Share Repurchases} - \text{Dividends Paid}\]

4.4.1 Free Cash Flow (FCF)

Free Cash Flow is the true measure of discretionary cash generated by a business after maintaining and expanding its capital base:

\[\mathbf{\text{Free Cash Flow (FCF)} = \text{Operating Cash Flow (CFO)} - \text{CapEx}}\]

4.4.2 FCFF vs. FCFE

  • Free Cash Flow to Firm (FCFF / Unlevered FCF): Cash available to all capital providers (both debt and equity): \[\mathbf{\text{FCFF} = \text{EBIT} \times (1 - t) + \text{D&A} - \text{CapEx} - \Delta \text{NWC}}\] where \(t\) is the marginal corporate tax rate, and \(\text{EBIT} \times (1 - t)\) is NOPAT (Net Operating Profit After Taxes).
  • Free Cash Flow to Equity (FCFE / Levered FCF): Cash available strictly to common equity shareholders after servicing debt: \[\mathbf{\text{FCFE} = \text{FCFF} - \text{Interest} \times (1 - t) + \text{Net Borrowing}}\]

5 5. Enterprise Value vs. Equity Value

One of the most frequent mistakes in corporate analysis is confusing Equity Value with Enterprise Value.

│   │   Attributable to Stockholders│   Diluted Shares)       │

│   │          Total Debt           │  (Short-term Debt +     │
│   │    Attributable to Bondholders│   Long-term Bonds)      │

\[\mathbf{\text{Enterprise Value (EV)} = \text{Market Cap} + \text{Total Debt} + \text{Preferred Stock} + \text{Minority Interest} - \text{Cash and Cash Equivalents}}\]

5.0.1 Why Do We Subtract Cash?

Imagine buying a company for $1{,}000{,}000$ in stock and assuming $200{,}000$ in debt. If the company has $300{,}000$ sitting in its checking account, you can immediately use that cash on day 1 to extinguish the debt and pocket $100{,}000$. Your true net economic cost (Enterprise Value) was: \[\text{EV} = $1{,}000{,}000 + $200{,}000 - $300{,}000 = $900{,}000\]

5.0.2 The Fundamental Consistency Rule of Valuation Multiples

  • When using a metric that belongs to equity holders only (Net Income, EPS, Book Value), use Market Cap or Share Price in the numerator (e.g., \(P/E\), \(P/B\)).
  • When using a metric that belongs to all capital providers (Revenue, EBITDA, EBIT, FCFF), use Enterprise Value (\(EV\)) in the numerator (e.g., \(EV/\text{EBITDA}\), \(EV/\text{Sales}\)).
  • Mistake: Comparing Price-to-EBITDA (\(P/\text{EBITDA}\)) is an error because EBITDA generates cash to pay both bondholders and equity holders, while Price only counts equity.

6 6. Financial Ratios and DuPont Analysis

6.1 6.1 Valuation Multiples

Multiple Formula Ideal Use Case Limitations
\(P/E\) (Price-to-Earnings) \(\frac{P_0}{\text{EPS}}\) or \(\frac{\text{Market Cap}}{\text{Net Income}}\) Mature, profitable businesses with stable capital structures. Distorted by debt levels, accounting conventions, and one-off write-downs.
\(EV/\text{EBITDA}\) \(\frac{\text{Enterprise Value}}{\text{EBITDA}}\) M&A transactions, leveraged buyouts (LBOs), cross-border comps. Masks high capital intensity (CapEx) and depreciation.
\(EV/\text{EBIT}\) \(\frac{\text{Enterprise Value}}{\text{EBIT}}\) Capital-intensive industries (manufacturing, airlines, telecoms). Still subject to depreciation accounting assumptions.
\(P/B\) (Price-to-Book) \(\frac{\text{Share Price}}{\text{Book Value Per Share}}\) Financial institutions, banks, insurance, asset-heavy firms. Meaningless for tech/asset-light firms with heavy off-balance-sheet R&D.
\(PEG\) Ratio \(\frac{P/E}{\text{Annual EPS Growth Rate \%}}\) High-growth companies. Sensitive to growth estimates; assumes linear relationship between P/E and growth.

6.2 6.2 DuPont Analysis: Deconstructing Return on Equity (ROE)

Return on Equity (ROE) measures how effectively management generates profits from each dollar of shareholder capital: \[\text{ROE} = \frac{\text{Net Income}}{\text{Shareholders' Equity}}\]

The DuPont 3-Step Identity reveals that high ROE can stem from three completely different drivers: operational excellence, asset velocity, or dangerous financial leverage:

\[\mathbf{\text{ROE} = \underbrace{\frac{\text{Net Income}}{\text{Revenue}}}_{\text{Net Profit Margin}} \times \underbrace{\frac{\text{Revenue}}{\text{Total Assets}}}_{\text{Asset Turnover}} \times \underbrace{\frac{\text{Total Assets}}{\text{Shareholders' Equity}}}_{\text{Equity Multiplier (Financial Leverage)}}}\]

  1. Net Profit Margin (\(\frac{\text{Net Income}}{\text{Revenue}}\)): Operational efficiency and pricing power.
  2. Asset Turnover (\(\frac{\text{Revenue}}{\text{Total Assets}}\)): Capital efficiency—how many dollars of sales are generated per dollar of assets.
  3. Equity Multiplier (\(\frac{\text{Total Assets}}{\text{Shareholders' Equity}}\)): Financial risk—how much debt the company employs to finance its assets.

6.3 6.3 Return on Invested Capital (ROIC) vs. WACC

The ultimate test of corporate value creation is whether the firm earns more on its capital than the providers of that capital demand:

\[\mathbf{\text{ROIC} = \frac{\text{NOPAT}}{\text{Invested Capital}} = \frac{\text{EBIT} \times (1 - t)}{\text{Total Debt} + \text{Equity} - \text{Cash}}}\]

  • Economic Value Added (EVA): \[\text{Value Creation} \iff \mathbf{\text{ROIC} > \text{WACC}}\] If a firm grows rapidly while \(\text{ROIC} < \text{WACC}\), every dollar of new growth actively destroys shareholder wealth!

7 7. Cost of Capital and Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) Valuation

7.1 7.1 The Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM)

The required return on equity \(r_e\) accounts for time value of money and non-diversifiable systematic risk (\(\beta\)):

\[\mathbf{r_e = R_f + \beta \left(\mathbb{E}[R_m] - R_f\right)}\]

  • \(R_f\): Risk-Free Rate (yield on 10-year US Treasury bond).
  • \(\beta\): Systematic volatility of the stock relative to the broader market index (\(\beta = \frac{\text{Cov}(R_i, R_m)}{\text{Var}(R_m)}\)).
  • \(\mathbb{E}[R_m] - R_f\): Equity Risk Premium (ERP), historically \(5.0\% - 6.5\%\).

7.2 7.2 Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC)

WACC represents the blended hurdle rate of the firm across debt and equity:

\[\mathbf{\text{WACC} = \left(\frac{E}{V}\right) r_e + \left(\frac{D}{V}\right) r_d (1 - t)}\]

where: * \(E\): Market value of equity; \(D\): Market value of debt; \(V = E + D\). * \(r_d\): Pre-tax cost of debt (yield to maturity on corporate bonds). * \((1 - t)\): The corporate interest tax shield (because interest is tax-deductible).

7.3 7.3 The Two-Stage DCF Formula

The intrinsic Enterprise Value is the present value of explicit discrete cash flows plus the present value of the terminal value:

\[\mathbf{\text{EV} = \sum_{t=1}^T \frac{\text{FCFF}_t}{(1 + \text{WACC})^t} + \frac{\text{Terminal Value}_T}{(1 + \text{WACC})^T}}\]

Where Terminal Value (\(TV_T\)) is calculated using the Gordon Growth formula on normalized cash flow: \[\mathbf{TV_T = \frac{\text{FCFF}_{T+1}}{\text{WACC} - g} = \frac{\text{FCFF}_T (1 + g)}{\text{WACC} - g}}\]

Finally, Intrinsic Equity Value per share is derived: \[\mathbf{\text{Equity Value} = \text{EV} - \text{Debt} + \text{Cash}, \quad P_{\text{intrinsic}} = \frac{\text{Equity Value}}{\text{Diluted Shares}}}\]

8 8. Interactive Python Financial Modeling Laboratory

The following executable Python model computes the full financial statement waterfall, calculates DuPont ratios, evaluates WACC, and runs a comprehensive Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) valuation with sensitivity tables.

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

print("=== 1. INCOME STATEMENT TO FREE CASH FLOW WATERFALL ===")

# Corporate Financial Inputs ($ in Millions)
revenue = 10000.0       # $10 Billion
cogs = 4000.0           # Direct product costs
sga = 2500.0            # Sales, general & admin
rd = 1000.0             # Research & development
depreciation = 600.0    # Non-cash depreciation
amortization = 150.0    # Non-cash amortization
interest_expense = 200.0
tax_rate = 0.21         # 21% US Corporate tax rate
capex = 700.0           # Capital expenditures
delta_nwc = 150.0       # Investment in working capital

# Waterfall Computations
gross_profit = revenue - cogs
ebit = gross_profit - sga - rd
ebitda = ebit + depreciation + amortization
ebt = ebit - interest_expense
tax_expense = max(0.0, ebt * tax_rate)
net_income = ebt - tax_expense

# Cash Flow Derivations
nopat = ebit * (1 - tax_rate)
cfo = net_income + (depreciation + amortization) - delta_nwc
fcf = cfo - capex
fcff = nopat + (depreciation + amortization) - capex - delta_nwc

print(f"Revenue:                ${revenue:,.1f}M")
print(f"Gross Profit:           ${gross_profit:,.1f}M (Margin: {gross_profit/revenue*100:.1f}%)")
print(f"EBIT (Operating Inc):   ${ebit:,.1f}M (Margin: {ebit/revenue*100:.1f}%)")
print(f"EBITDA:                 ${ebitda:,.1f}M (Margin: {ebitda/revenue*100:.1f}%)")
print(f"Net Income:             ${net_income:,.1f}M")
print(f"Operating Cash Flow:    ${cfo:,.1f}M")
print(f"Free Cash Flow (FCF):   ${fcf:,.1f}M")
print(f"Unlevered FCFF:         ${fcff:,.1f}M")

print("\n=== 2. DUPONT 3-STEP ROE DECOMPOSITION ===")
total_assets = 12000.0
total_equity = 5000.0
total_debt = 3000.0
cash = 1000.0

net_margin = net_income / revenue
asset_turnover = revenue / total_assets
equity_multiplier = total_assets / total_equity
roe = net_margin * asset_turnover * equity_multiplier

print(f"Net Profit Margin:      {net_margin*100:.2f}%")
print(f"Asset Turnover:         {asset_turnover:.3f}x")
print(f"Equity Multiplier:      {equity_multiplier:.2f}x")
print(f"Decomposed ROE:         {roe*100:.2f}% (Direct Check: {net_income/total_equity*100:.2f}%)")

print("\n=== 3. ENTERPRISE VALUE & MULTIPLES ===")
share_price = 75.0
shares_outstanding = 100.0  # 100M shares
market_cap = share_price * shares_outstanding
net_debt = total_debt - cash
enterprise_value = market_cap + net_debt

print(f"Market Capitalization:  ${market_cap:,.1f}M")
print(f"Net Debt:               ${net_debt:,.1f}M")
print(f"Enterprise Value (EV):  ${enterprise_value:,.1f}M")
print(f"EV / EBITDA Multiple:   {enterprise_value / ebitda:.2f}x")
print(f"P / E Multiple:         {market_cap / net_income:.2f}x")
print(f"EV / FCFF Multiple:     {enterprise_value / fcff:.2f}x")

print("\n=== 4. DISCOUNTED CASH FLOW (DCF) INTRINSIC VALUATION ===")
rf = 0.042       # 4.2% Risk-free rate
beta = 1.15      # Beta
erp = 0.055      # 5.5% Equity risk premium
cost_of_equity = rf + beta * erp
cost_of_debt = 0.055 * (1 - tax_rate)  # After-tax cost of debt

e_weight = market_cap / (market_cap + total_debt)
d_weight = total_debt / (market_cap + total_debt)
wacc = (e_weight * cost_of_equity) + (d_weight * cost_of_debt)

print(f"Cost of Equity (CAPM):  {cost_of_equity*100:.2f}%")
print(f"After-Tax Cost of Debt: {cost_of_debt*100:.2f}%")
print(f"WACC:                   {wacc*100:.2f}%")

# 5-Year Forecast with growth decaying from 8% to terminal 2.5%
growth_rates = [0.08, 0.07, 0.06, 0.05, 0.04]
g_terminal = 0.025

pv_discrete_fcff = 0.0
curr_fcff = fcff
forecast_fcff = []

for year, g in enumerate(growth_rates, 1):
    curr_fcff *= (1 + g)
    pv = curr_fcff / ((1 + wacc) ** year)
    pv_discrete_fcff += pv
    forecast_fcff.append((year, curr_fcff, pv))

terminal_fcff = curr_fcff * (1 + g_terminal)
terminal_value = terminal_fcff / (wacc - g_terminal)
pv_terminal_value = terminal_value / ((1 + wacc) ** len(growth_rates))

intrinsic_ev = pv_discrete_fcff + pv_terminal_value
intrinsic_equity = intrinsic_ev - net_debt
intrinsic_share_price = intrinsic_equity / shares_outstanding

print(f"\nPV of 5-Yr Discrete FCFF: ${pv_discrete_fcff:,.1f}M")
print(f"PV of Terminal Value:     ${pv_terminal_value:,.1f}M")
print(f"Intrinsic Enterprise Val: ${intrinsic_ev:,.1f}M")
print(f"Intrinsic Equity Value:   ${intrinsic_equity:,.1f}M")
print(f"Intrinsic Share Price:    ${intrinsic_share_price:.2f} vs Market: ${share_price:.2f}")

upside = ((intrinsic_share_price / share_price) - 1.0) * 100
print(f"Valuation Premium/Margin: {upside:+.2f}%")

9 9. Synthesis: The Executive Valuation Cheat Sheet

Financial Concept Definition & Key Formula Core Strategic Interpretation
Shares Outstanding \(\text{Issued} - \text{Treasury}\) Basis for ownership fraction and EPS calculation. Diluted shares include options/converts.
Ex-Dividend Date First trade date without dividend Price mechanically adjusts downward by dividend amount at open.
EBITDA \(\text{EBIT} + \text{D&A}\) Capital-structure-neutral proxy for operating cash flow. Ignores CapEx!
Free Cash Flow \(\text{CFO} - \text{CapEx}\) Cash that can be extracted from the business without impairing operations.
Enterprise Value \(\text{Market Cap} + \text{Debt} - \text{Cash}\) The true takeover price of the operating entity. Must pair with unlevered cash flows.
DuPont Identity \(\text{Margin} \times \text{Turnover} \times \text{Leverage}\) Unmasks whether high ROE is driven by operational genius or dangerous debt.
WACC \(\frac{E}{V}r_e + \frac{D}{V}r_d(1-t)\) Blended corporate hurdle rate. The firm creates economic value only when \(\text{ROIC} > \text{WACC}\).
Gordon Growth DDM \(P_0 = \frac{D_1}{r - g}\) Capitalizes perpetual constant dividend streams. Requires \(r > g\).
