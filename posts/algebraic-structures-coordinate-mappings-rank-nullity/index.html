1. Introduction: The Hierarchy of Algebraic Structures

Modern linear algebra and theoretical physics do not operate in a vacuum; they rest upon a progressive hierarchy of algebraic structures. Before one can define a vector space, one must define an underlying field of scalars. Before one can define a field, one must understand rings and abelian groups.

The progression of algebraic structures is characterized by the gradual addition of binary operations and structural axioms:

2. What an Algebraic Field Is and Why the Rationals (\(\mathbb{Q}\)), Reals (\(\mathbb{R}\)), and Complex Numbers (\(\mathbb{C}\)) Form Fields

To construct linear algebra, we require a scalar system where addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division by non-zero elements are universally and unconditionally defined. Such a structure is an algebraic field.

2.2 Crucial Distinction: Axioms vs. Corollaries (Uniqueness Theorems)

A common point of confusion in abstract algebra is the question of uniqueness:

Critical Note:
The axioms of a field (and of a group) assert only the existence (\(\exists\)) of an additive identity \(0\), a multiplicative identity \(1\), an additive inverse \(-a\), and a multiplicative inverse \(a^{-1}\).
Uniqueness is not an axiom; it is a theorem (corollary) proved rigorously from the axioms.

Let us state and prove these fundamental uniqueness corollaries:

Corollary 1: Uniqueness of the Additive and Multiplicative Identities

  • Claim: The additive identity \(0\) is unique.
    Proof: Suppose \(0\) and \(0'\) are both additive identities in \(\mathbb{F}\).
    Because \(0\) is an identity: \(0' + 0 = 0'\).
    Because \(0'\) is an identity: \(0 + 0' = 0\).
    By commutativity of addition: \(0' + 0 = 0 + 0'\).
    Therefore, \(0' = 0\). \(\blacksquare\)
  • Claim: The multiplicative identity \(1\) is unique.
    Proof: Suppose \(1\) and \(1'\) are both multiplicative identities in \(\mathbb{F}\).
    Then \(1 = 1 \cdot 1' = 1'\). Thus, \(1\) is unique. \(\blacksquare\)

Corollary 2: Uniqueness of Inverses

  • Claim: For any \(a \in \mathbb{F}\), its additive inverse \(-a\) is unique.
    Proof: Suppose \(b\) and \(c\) are both additive inverses of \(a\), so \(a + b = 0\) and \(a + c = 0\).
    Consider the expression \((b + a) + c\):
    \[(b + a) + c = 0 + c = c\]
    By associativity:
    \[(b + a) + c = b + (a + c) = b + 0 = b\]
    Therefore, \(b = c\). The additive inverse is unique. \(\blacksquare\)
  • Claim: For any non-zero \(a \in \mathbb{F} \setminus \{0\}\), its multiplicative inverse \(a^{-1}\) is unique.
    Proof: Suppose \(b\) and \(c\) are both multiplicative inverses of \(a\), so \(a \cdot b = 1\) and \(a \cdot c = 1\).
    Then \(b = b \cdot 1 = b \cdot (a \cdot c) = (b \cdot a) \cdot c = 1 \cdot c = c\). The reciprocal is unique. \(\blacksquare\)

Corollary 3: Annihilation by Zero (\(0 \cdot a = 0\))

Why does multiplying any element by zero yield zero? This is not an axiom; it follows from distributivity: \[0 \cdot a = (0 + 0) \cdot a = (0 \cdot a) + (0 \cdot a)\] Adding the unique additive inverse \(-(0 \cdot a)\) to both sides: \[(0 \cdot a) + (-(0 \cdot a)) = ((0 \cdot a) + (0 \cdot a)) + (-(0 \cdot a))\] \[0 = (0 \cdot a) + 0 = 0 \cdot a \implies 0 \cdot a = 0 \quad \blacksquare\]

3. What Rings and Ideals Are

To appreciate why fields are so special, we must examine the broader category of rings and their structural sub-components: ideals.

3.1 Definition of a Ring

A ring \((R, +, \cdot)\) is a set equipped with two binary operations satisfying: 1. \((R, +)\) is an Abelian group. 2. Multiplication is associative: \((a \cdot b) \cdot c = a \cdot (b \cdot c)\) for all \(a, b, c \in R\). 3. Distributivity holds: \(a \cdot (b + c) = a \cdot b + a \cdot c\) and \((a + b) \cdot c = a \cdot c + b \cdot c\).

  • If \(a \cdot b = b \cdot a\), the ring is commutative.
  • If there exists an element \(1 \in R\) such that \(1 \cdot a = a \cdot 1 = a\), it is a ring with unity (identity).
  • An integral domain is a commutative ring with \(1 \neq 0\) having no zero divisors (\(a \cdot b = 0 \implies a = 0 \text{ or } b = 0\)).

3.2 Ideals and Quotient Rings

In group theory, normal subgroups allow the construction of quotient groups. In ring theory, the corresponding structure is an ideal:

Definition (Ideal):
A subset \(I \subseteq R\) of a ring \((R, +, \cdot)\) is called a (two-sided) ideal if: 1. \((I, +)\) is an additive subgroup of \((R, +)\) (\(\forall x, y \in I, \; x - y \in I\)). 2. Absorption Property: For every \(r \in R\) and \(x \in I\), both \(r \cdot x \in I\) and \(x \cdot r \in I\).

The absorption property guarantees that the set of cosets \(R/I = \{r + I \mid r \in R\}\) forms a well-defined quotient ring under coset addition and coset multiplication: \[(a + I) + (b + I) = (a + b) + I, \qquad (a + I) \cdot (b + I) = (a \cdot b) + I\]

3.3 Maximal Ideals and the Generation of Fields

Ideals provide a mechanism for generating fields from rings:

  • Prime Ideal: An ideal \(P \subsetneq R\) is prime if \(a \cdot b \in P \implies a \in P \text{ or } b \in P\).
    Theorem: \(R/P\) is an integral domain if and only if \(P\) is a prime ideal.
  • Maximal Ideal: An ideal \(M \subsetneq R\) is maximal if no other proper ideal strictly contains \(M\) (if \(M \subseteq J \subseteq R\), then \(J = M\) or \(J = R\)).
    Theorem: \(R/M\) is an algebraic field if and only if \(M\) is a maximal ideal!

4. Canonical Field Archetypes: \(\mathbb{Q}\), \(\mathbb{R}\), \(\mathbb{C}\), and \(\mathbb{F}_p\)

4.1 The Rational Numbers (\(\mathbb{Q}\)) as the Fraction Field of \(\mathbb{Z}\)

The integers \(\mathbb{Z} = \{\dots, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, \dots\}\) form a commutative ring with identity, but \(\mathbb{Z}\) is not a field because non-zero integers other than \(\pm 1\) do not possess multiplicative inverses in \(\mathbb{Z}\) (e.g., \(2^{-1} = 1/2 \notin \mathbb{Z}\)).

The rational numbers \(\mathbb{Q}\) are constructed as the field of fractions (quotient field) of \(\mathbb{Z}\): \[\mathbb{Q} = \left\{ \frac{a}{b} \;\middle\vert\; a, b \in \mathbb{Z}, \; b \neq 0 \right\} \Big/ \sim \tag{1}\] where the equivalence relation is defined by cross-multiplication: \[\frac{a}{b} \sim \frac{c}{d} \iff a d = b c\]

Every non-zero element \(x = a/b \in \mathbb{Q}\) (\(a \neq 0\)) has the unique reciprocal \(x^{-1} = b/a \in \mathbb{Q}\), satisfying \((a/b)(b/a) = 1\). Because \(\mathbb{Q}\) is a field, every vector space over \(\mathbb{Q}\) supports exact Gaussian elimination without roundoff error.

4.2 The Real Numbers (\(\mathbb{R}\)) and Complex Numbers (\(\mathbb{C}\))

  • The Real Field \(\mathbb{R}\): The field of rationals \(\mathbb{Q}\) is algebraically incomplete—it contains “holes” (e.g., \(\sqrt{2} \notin \mathbb{Q}\)). The real numbers \(\mathbb{R}\) are constructed as the Dedekind cut or Cauchy sequence completion of \(\mathbb{Q}\). \(\mathbb{R}\) is the unique complete Archimedean ordered field.
  • The Complex Field \(\mathbb{C}\): The real field is not algebraically closed (e.g., \(x^2 + 1 = 0\) has no solution in \(\mathbb{R}\)). By quotienting the polynomial ring \(\mathbb{R}[x]\) by the maximal ideal generated by the irreducible polynomial \((x^2 + 1)\): \[\mathbb{C} \cong \mathbb{R}[x] / (x^2 + 1) \tag{2}\] we obtain the field of complex numbers \(\mathbb{C} = \{a + b i \mid a, b \in \mathbb{R}, i^2 = -1\}\). By the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra, \(\mathbb{C}\) is algebraically closed.
  • Finite Fields \(\mathbb{F}_p\) (Galois Fields): For any prime number \(p\), the ideal \(p\mathbb{Z}\) is maximal in \(\mathbb{Z}\). The quotient ring \(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z} = \mathbb{F}_p\) forms a finite field of order \(p\), forming the foundation of modern cryptography.

5. Vector Spaces and the Coordinate Isomorphism Theorem

5.1 Formal Definition of a Vector Space

Let \(\mathbb{F}\) be an algebraic field. A vector space \((V, +, \cdot)\) over \(\mathbb{F}\) is a set \(V\) equipped with vector addition \(+: V \times V \to V\) and scalar multiplication \(\cdot: \mathbb{F} \times V \to V\) such that: 1. \((V, +)\) is an Abelian group (closure, associativity, commutativity, zero vector \(\mathbf{0}\), additive inverse \(-\mathbf{v}\)). 2. Scalar multiplication satisfies compatibility and distributivity: - \(a \cdot (b \cdot \mathbf{v}) = (a \cdot b) \cdot \mathbf{v}\) - \(1 \cdot \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}\) - \(a \cdot (\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) = a \cdot \mathbf{u} + a \cdot \mathbf{v}\) - \((a + b) \cdot \mathbf{v} = a \cdot \mathbf{v} + b \cdot \mathbf{v}\)

5.2 Basis and Coordinates

Let \(V\) be a finite-dimensional vector space over \(\mathbb{F}\). An ordered set of vectors \(\mathcal{B} = (\mathbf{b}_1, \mathbf{b}_2, \dots, \mathbf{b}_n)\) is a basis for \(V\) if: 1. \(\mathcal{B}\) is linearly independent: \(\sum_{i=1}^n c_i \mathbf{b}_i = \mathbf{0} \implies c_1 = c_2 = \dots = c_n = 0\). 2. \(\mathcal{B}\) spans \(V\): \(\operatorname{span}(\mathcal{B}) = V\).

Every vector \(\mathbf{v} \in V\) can be expressed as a linear combination of basis vectors: \[\mathbf{v} = c_1 \mathbf{b}_1 + c_2 \mathbf{b}_2 + \dots + c_n \mathbf{b}_n \tag{3}\]

Lemma: Uniqueness of Coordinate Representations

Claim: The scalar coefficients \((c_1, \dots, c_n)\) in Equation (3) are strictly unique.
Proof: Suppose \(\mathbf{v}\) could also be written as \(\mathbf{v} = \sum_{i=1}^n d_i \mathbf{b}_i\). Subtracting the two representations: \[\mathbf{0} = \mathbf{v} - \mathbf{v} = \sum_{i=1}^n (c_i - d_i) \mathbf{b}_i\] By linear independence of the basis \(\mathcal{B}\), every coefficient must vanish: \[c_i - d_i = 0 \implies c_i = d_i \quad \forall \, i \in \{1, \dots, n\} \quad \blacksquare\]

This unique tuple defines the coordinate vector of \(\mathbf{v}\) relative to basis \(\mathcal{B}\): \[[\mathbf{v}]_\mathcal{B} = \begin{pmatrix} c_1 \\ c_2 \\ \vdots \\ c_n \end{pmatrix} \in \mathbb{F}^n \tag{4}\]

5.3 The Coordinate Isomorphism Theorem: Proof of Equivalence Between Abstract Vectors and Coordinate Tuples

We now prove the central theorem bridging abstract geometry and concrete computational linear algebra:

The Coordinate Mapping Theorem:
Let \(\mathcal{B} = (\mathbf{b}_1, \dots, \mathbf{b}_n)\) be a basis for an \(n\)-dimensional vector space \(V\) over field \(\mathbb{F}\).
The coordinate mapping: \[\Phi_\mathcal{B}: V \to \mathbb{F}^n, \qquad \Phi_\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{v}) = [\mathbf{v}]_\mathcal{B}\] is a vector space isomorphism (\(V \cong \mathbb{F}^n\)). That is, \(\Phi_\mathcal{B}\) is a bijective linear transformation.

Complete Step-by-Step Proof

  1. Proof of Linearity:
    Let \(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in V\) and \(\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{F}\).
    Let \(\mathbf{u} = \sum_{i=1}^n u_i \mathbf{b}_i\) and \(\mathbf{v} = \sum_{i=1}^n v_i \mathbf{b}_i\).
    Then: \[\alpha \mathbf{u} + \beta \mathbf{v} = \alpha \left(\sum_{i=1}^n u_i \mathbf{b}_i\right) + \beta \left(\sum_{i=1}^n v_i \mathbf{b}_i\right) = \sum_{i=1}^n (\alpha u_i + \beta v_i) \mathbf{b}_i\] By definition of coordinates relative to \(\mathcal{B}\): \[[\alpha \mathbf{u} + \beta \mathbf{v}]_\mathcal{B} = \begin{pmatrix} \alpha u_1 + \beta v_1 \\ \vdots \\ \alpha u_n + \beta v_n \end{pmatrix} = \alpha \begin{pmatrix} u_1 \\ \vdots \\ u_n \end{pmatrix} + \beta \begin{pmatrix} v_1 \\ \vdots \\ v_n \end{pmatrix} = \alpha [\mathbf{u}]_\mathcal{B} + \beta [\mathbf{v}]_\mathcal{B}\] Hence \(\Phi_\mathcal{B}\) preserves both vector addition and scalar multiplication.

  2. Proof of Injectivity (One-to-One):
    We examine the kernel of \(\Phi_\mathcal{B}\): \[\ker(\Phi_\mathcal{B}) = \{\mathbf{v} \in V \mid \Phi_\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{v}) = \mathbf{0} \in \mathbb{F}^n\}\] If \([\mathbf{v}]_\mathcal{B} = \mathbf{0}\), then \(\mathbf{v} = 0 \mathbf{b}_1 + \dots + 0 \mathbf{b}_n = \mathbf{0}\).
    Therefore, \(\ker(\Phi_\mathcal{B}) = \{\mathbf{0}\}\). A linear map with a trivial kernel is strictly injective.

  3. Proof of Surjectivity (Onto):
    Let \(\mathbf{c} = (c_1, \dots, c_n)^T\) be an arbitrary column vector in \(\mathbb{F}^n\).
    Construct the vector \(\mathbf{w} = \sum_{i=1}^n c_i \mathbf{b}_i \in V\).
    By inspection, \(\Phi_\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{w}) = [\mathbf{w}]_\mathcal{B} = \mathbf{c}\).
    Thus, every element of \(\mathbb{F}^n\) has a pre-image in \(V\), proving \(\Phi_\mathcal{B}\) is surjective.

Because \(\Phi_\mathcal{B}\) is a bijective homomorphism, it is an isomorphism (\(V \cong \mathbb{F}^n\)).
Conclusion: Any abstract vector calculation (in function spaces, quantum Hilbert states, or dimensional spaces) is formally equivalent to arithmetic on column tuples in \(\mathbb{F}^n\). \(\blacksquare\)

6. Gaussian Elimination and Echelon Forms

Once vectors are represented as coordinates in \(\mathbb{F}^n\), linear systems and subspace bases are computed via Gaussian elimination.

6.1 Elementary Row Operations as Invertible Linear Operators

There are three elementary row operations on a matrix \(A \in \mathbb{F}^{m \times n}\): 1. Row Swap (\(R_i \leftrightarrow R_j\)): Exchanging two rows. 2. Row Scaling (\(R_i \leftarrow k R_i, \; k \neq 0\)): Multiplying a row by a non-zero scalar. 3. Row Addition (\(R_i \leftarrow R_i + k R_j\)): Adding a scalar multiple of row \(j\) to row \(i\).

Every elementary row operation corresponds to multiplying \(A\) on the left by an elementary matrix \(E\): \[A \xrightarrow{\text{row op}} E A\] Because \(k \neq 0\), every elementary matrix is strictly invertible (\(E^{-1}\) exists and is of the exact same type). Consequently: \[\ker(E A) = \ker(A) \quad \text{and} \quad \operatorname{row}(E A) = \operatorname{row}(A) \tag{5}\] Elementary row operations leave the null space and row space strictly invariant.

6.2 Row Echelon Form (REF) vs. Reduced Row Echelon Form (RREF)

A matrix is in Row Echelon Form (REF) if: 1. All zero rows are at the bottom. 2. The leading entry (pivot) of each non-zero row is strictly to the right of the leading entry of the row above it. 3. All entries below a pivot are zero.

A matrix is in Reduced Row Echelon Form (RREF) if, additionally: 4. The leading entry in each non-zero row is equal to \(1\). 5. Each pivot \(1\) is the only non-zero entry in its entire column.

Theorem (Uniqueness of RREF):
While a matrix has infinitely many row echelon forms, its Reduced Row Echelon Form \(\operatorname{rref}(A)\) is strictly unique.

7.2 The Rank-Nullity Theorem

The Rank-Nullity Theorem (Dimension Theorem):
Let \(V\) and \(W\) be vector spaces over field \(\mathbb{F}\) with \(\dim(V) = n\).
For any linear transformation \(T: V \to W\): \[\operatorname{dim}(\ker(T)) + \operatorname{dim}(\operatorname{im}(T)) = \dim(V) \iff \operatorname{nullity}(T) + \operatorname{rank}(T) = n \tag{6}\]

Rigorous Proof

  1. Let \(\operatorname{nullity}(T) = k = \dim(\ker(T))\).
  2. Choose a basis for the kernel: \((\mathbf{u}_1, \dots, \mathbf{u}_k)\) of \(\ker(T)\).
  3. By the Basis Extension Theorem, extend this basis to a complete basis of \(V\): \[\mathcal{B}_V = (\mathbf{u}_1, \dots, \mathbf{u}_k, \mathbf{w}_1, \dots, \mathbf{w}_{n-k})\]
  4. We claim that the image set \(S = (T(\mathbf{w}_1), \dots, T(\mathbf{w}_{n-k}))\) forms a basis for \(\operatorname{im}(T)\):
    • Span: Any \(\mathbf{v} \in V\) can be written as \(\mathbf{v} = \sum_{i=1}^k c_i \mathbf{u}_i + \sum_{j=1}^{n-k} d_j \mathbf{w}_j\).
      Applying \(T\): \[T(\mathbf{v}) = \sum_{i=1}^k c_i T(\mathbf{u}_i) + \sum_{j=1}^{n-k} d_j T(\mathbf{w}_j) = \mathbf{0} + \sum_{j=1}^{n-k} d_j T(\mathbf{w}_j)\] Hence \(S\) spans \(\operatorname{im}(T)\).
    • Linear Independence: Suppose \(\sum_{j=1}^{n-k} d_j T(\mathbf{w}_j) = \mathbf{0}\).
      By linearity: \(T\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n-k} d_j \mathbf{w}_j\right) = \mathbf{0}\).
      This implies \(\sum_{j=1}^{n-k} d_j \mathbf{w}_j \in \ker(T)\).
      Therefore, it must be expressible in terms of the kernel basis: \[\sum_{j=1}^{n-k} d_j \mathbf{w}_j = \sum_{i=1}^k c_i \mathbf{u}_i \implies \sum_{i=1}^k (-c_i) \mathbf{u}_i + \sum_{j=1}^{n-k} d_j \mathbf{w}_j = \mathbf{0}\] Because \(\mathcal{B}_V\) is linearly independent, all coefficients must vanish: \(c_i = 0\) and \(d_j = 0\) for all \(j\).
      Thus, \(S\) is linearly independent.
  5. Since \(S\) is a linearly independent spanning set for \(\operatorname{im}(T)\), it is a basis.
    Therefore, \(\operatorname{rank}(T) = \dim(\operatorname{im}(T)) = n - k\).
    Adding the two dimensions: \[\operatorname{nullity}(T) + \operatorname{rank}(T) = k + (n - k) = n \quad \blacksquare\]

8. Worked Case Study: Coordinate Mappings on Polynomial Function Spaces

To see the coordinate isomorphism in action on an abstract non-Euclidean vector space, consider the space of real polynomials of degree at most 2: \[\mathcal{P}_2 = \{a_0 + a_1 x + a_2 x^2 \mid a_0, a_1, a_2 \in \mathbb{R}\}\] equipped with the ordered basis \(\mathcal{B} = (1, x, x^2)\).

Step 1: Mapping Derivatives to Matrices

Consider the derivative operator \(D = \frac{d}{dx}: \mathcal{P}_2 \to \mathcal{P}_2\).
Compute the action of \(D\) on the basis vectors: \[D(1) = 0 = 0 \cdot 1 + 0 \cdot x + 0 \cdot x^2 \implies [D(1)]_\mathcal{B} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}\] \[D(x) = 1 = 1 \cdot 1 + 0 \cdot x + 0 \cdot x^2 \implies [D(x)]_\mathcal{B} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}\] \[D(x^2) = 2x = 0 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot x + 0 \cdot x^2 \implies [D(x^2)]_\mathcal{B} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 2 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}\]

The matrix representation of \(D\) relative to basis \(\mathcal{B}\) is: \[[D]_\mathcal{B} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \tag{7}\]

Step 2: Rank-Nullity Verification

  • Matrix Size: \(n = 3\) columns (domain dimension \(\dim(\mathcal{P}_2) = 3\)).
  • Columns with pivots: Columns 1 and 2 (indices 1, 2) \(\implies \operatorname{rank}([D]_\mathcal{B}) = 2\).
    The image is \(\operatorname{span}(1, x) = \mathcal{P}_1\), which has dimension 2.
  • Nullity: Free column is Column 0 \(\implies \operatorname{nullity}([D]_\mathcal{B}) = 3 - 2 = 1\).
    The kernel is \(\ker(D) = \{p(x) \mid p'(x) = 0\} = \operatorname{span}(1)\) (the constant polynomials), which has dimension 1.
  • Check: \(2 + 1 = 3 \implies \operatorname{rank} + \operatorname{nullity} = \dim(V)\). The theorem holds perfectly!

9. Foundational Terminology Glossary

  • Abelian Group: A set with an associative, commutative binary operation containing an identity element and an inverse for every element.
  • Ring: An algebraic structure with addition (forming an abelian group) and multiplication (associative and distributive over addition).
  • Ideal: An additive subgroup of a ring that absorbs multiplication by any element in the ring (\(r \cdot x \in I\)).
  • Quotient Ring (\(R/I\)): The partition of a ring into cosets modulo an ideal, forming a new ring.
  • Field: A commutative ring with non-zero unity where every non-zero element has a multiplicative inverse.
  • Fraction Field: The smallest field containing a given integral domain (e.g., \(\mathbb{Q} = \operatorname{Frac}(\mathbb{Z})\)).
  • Vector Space Isomorphism: A bijective linear mapping between vector spaces that preserves addition and scalar multiplication (\(V \cong \mathbb{F}^n\)).
  • Coordinate Mapping: The canonical isomorphism \(\Phi_\mathcal{B}: V \to \mathbb{F}^n\) mapping an abstract vector \(\mathbf{v}\) to its unique coordinate tuple relative to basis \(\mathcal{B}\).
  • RREF (Reduced Row Echelon Form): The unique canonical matrix echelon form where pivots are 1 and are the sole non-zero entries in their columns.
  • Rank-Nullity Theorem: The foundational theorem proving that for any linear map from an \(n\)-dimensional domain, \(\operatorname{rank} + \operatorname{nullity} = n\).

### 2.2 Crucial Distinction: Axioms vs. Corollaries (Uniqueness Theorems)

A common point of confusion in abstract algebra is the question of **uniqueness**:

> **Critical Note:**  
> The axioms of a field (and of a group) assert **only the existence** ($\exists$) of an additive identity $0$, a multiplicative identity $1$, an additive inverse $-a$, and a multiplicative inverse $a^{-1}$.  
> **Uniqueness is not an axiom; it is a theorem (corollary) proved rigorously from the axioms.**

Let us state and prove these fundamental uniqueness corollaries:

#### Corollary 1: Uniqueness of the Additive and Multiplicative Identities
* **Claim:** The additive identity $0$ is unique.  
  *Proof:* Suppose $0$ and $0'$ are both additive identities in $\mathbb{F}$.  
  Because $0$ is an identity: $0' + 0 = 0'$.  
  Because $0'$ is an identity: $0 + 0' = 0$.  
  By commutativity of addition: $0' + 0 = 0 + 0'$.  
  Therefore, $0' = 0$. $\blacksquare$
* **Claim:** The multiplicative identity $1$ is unique.  
  *Proof:* Suppose $1$ and $1'$ are both multiplicative identities in $\mathbb{F}$.  
  Then $1 = 1 \cdot 1' = 1'$. Thus, $1$ is unique. $\blacksquare$

#### Corollary 2: Uniqueness of Inverses
* **Claim:** For any $a \in \mathbb{F}$, its additive inverse $-a$ is unique.  
  *Proof:* Suppose $b$ and $c$ are both additive inverses of $a$, so $a + b = 0$ and $a + c = 0$.  
  Consider the expression $(b + a) + c$:  
  $$(b + a) + c = 0 + c = c$$  
  By associativity:  
  $$(b + a) + c = b + (a + c) = b + 0 = b$$  
  Therefore, $b = c$. The additive inverse is unique. $\blacksquare$
* **Claim:** For any non-zero $a \in \mathbb{F} \setminus \{0\}$, its multiplicative inverse $a^{-1}$ is unique.  
  *Proof:* Suppose $b$ and $c$ are both multiplicative inverses of $a$, so $a \cdot b = 1$ and $a \cdot c = 1$.  
  Then $b = b \cdot 1 = b \cdot (a \cdot c) = (b \cdot a) \cdot c = 1 \cdot c = c$. The reciprocal is unique. $\blacksquare$

#### Corollary 3: Annihilation by Zero ($0 \cdot a = 0$)
Why does multiplying any element by zero yield zero? This is not an axiom; it follows from distributivity:
$$0 \cdot a = (0 + 0) \cdot a = (0 \cdot a) + (0 \cdot a)$$
Adding the unique additive inverse $-(0 \cdot a)$ to both sides:
$$(0 \cdot a) + (-(0 \cdot a)) = ((0 \cdot a) + (0 \cdot a)) + (-(0 \cdot a))$$
$$0 = (0 \cdot a) + 0 = 0 \cdot a \implies 0 \cdot a = 0 \quad \blacksquare$$

## 3. What Rings and Ideals Are

To appreciate why fields are so special, we must examine the broader category of **rings** and their structural sub-components: **ideals**.

### 3.1 Definition of a Ring
A **ring** $(R, +, \cdot)$ is a set equipped with two binary operations satisfying:
1. $(R, +)$ is an Abelian group.
2. Multiplication is associative: $(a \cdot b) \cdot c = a \cdot (b \cdot c)$ for all $a, b, c \in R$.
3. Distributivity holds: $a \cdot (b + c) = a \cdot b + a \cdot c$ and $(a + b) \cdot c = a \cdot c + b \cdot c$.

* If $a \cdot b = b \cdot a$, the ring is **commutative**.
* If there exists an element $1 \in R$ such that $1 \cdot a = a \cdot 1 = a$, it is a **ring with unity (identity)**.
* An **integral domain** is a commutative ring with $1 \neq 0$ having no zero divisors ($a \cdot b = 0 \implies a = 0 \text{ or } b = 0$).

### 3.2 Ideals and Quotient Rings
In group theory, normal subgroups allow the construction of quotient groups. In ring theory, the corresponding structure is an **ideal**:

> **Definition (Ideal):**  
> A subset $I \subseteq R$ of a ring $(R, +, \cdot)$ is called a **(two-sided) ideal** if:
> 1. $(I, +)$ is an additive subgroup of $(R, +)$ ($\forall x, y \in I, \; x - y \in I$).
> 2. **Absorption Property:** For every $r \in R$ and $x \in I$, both $r \cdot x \in I$ and $x \cdot r \in I$.

The absorption property guarantees that the set of cosets $R/I = \{r + I \mid r \in R\}$ forms a well-defined **quotient ring** under coset addition and coset multiplication:
$$(a + I) + (b + I) = (a + b) + I, \qquad (a + I) \cdot (b + I) = (a \cdot b) + I$$

### 3.3 Maximal Ideals and the Generation of Fields
Ideals provide a mechanism for generating fields from rings:

* **Prime Ideal:** An ideal $P \subsetneq R$ is prime if $a \cdot b \in P \implies a \in P \text{ or } b \in P$.  
  **Theorem:** $R/P$ is an integral domain if and only if $P$ is a prime ideal.
* **Maximal Ideal:** An ideal $M \subsetneq R$ is maximal if no other proper ideal strictly contains $M$ (if $M \subseteq J \subseteq R$, then $J = M$ or $J = R$).  
  **Theorem:** $R/M$ is an **algebraic field** if and only if $M$ is a maximal ideal!

## 4. Canonical Field Archetypes: $\mathbb{Q}$, $\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbb{C}$, and $\mathbb{F}_p$

### 4.1 The Rational Numbers ($\mathbb{Q}$) as the Fraction Field of $\mathbb{Z}$
The integers $\mathbb{Z} = \{\dots, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, \dots\}$ form a commutative ring with identity, but **$\mathbb{Z}$ is not a field** because non-zero integers other than $\pm 1$ do not possess multiplicative inverses in $\mathbb{Z}$ (e.g., $2^{-1} = 1/2 \notin \mathbb{Z}$).

The rational numbers $\mathbb{Q}$ are constructed as the **field of fractions (quotient field)** of $\mathbb{Z}$:
$$\mathbb{Q} = \left\{ \frac{a}{b} \;\middle\vert\; a, b \in \mathbb{Z}, \; b \neq 0 \right\} \Big/ \sim \tag{1}$$
where the equivalence relation is defined by cross-multiplication:
$$\frac{a}{b} \sim \frac{c}{d} \iff a d = b c$$

Every non-zero element $x = a/b \in \mathbb{Q}$ ($a \neq 0$) has the unique reciprocal $x^{-1} = b/a \in \mathbb{Q}$, satisfying $(a/b)(b/a) = 1$. Because $\mathbb{Q}$ is a field, every vector space over $\mathbb{Q}$ supports exact Gaussian elimination without roundoff error.

### 4.2 The Real Numbers ($\mathbb{R}$) and Complex Numbers ($\mathbb{C}$)
* **The Real Field $\mathbb{R}$:** The field of rationals $\mathbb{Q}$ is algebraically incomplete—it contains "holes" (e.g., $\sqrt{2} \notin \mathbb{Q}$). The real numbers $\mathbb{R}$ are constructed as the **Dedekind cut** or **Cauchy sequence completion** of $\mathbb{Q}$. $\mathbb{R}$ is the unique complete Archimedean ordered field.
* **The Complex Field $\mathbb{C}$:** The real field is not algebraically closed (e.g., $x^2 + 1 = 0$ has no solution in $\mathbb{R}$). By quotienting the polynomial ring $\mathbb{R}[x]$ by the maximal ideal generated by the irreducible polynomial $(x^2 + 1)$:
  $$\mathbb{C} \cong \mathbb{R}[x] / (x^2 + 1) \tag{2}$$
  we obtain the field of complex numbers $\mathbb{C} = \{a + b i \mid a, b \in \mathbb{R}, i^2 = -1\}$. By the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra, $\mathbb{C}$ is **algebraically closed**.
* **Finite Fields $\mathbb{F}_p$ (Galois Fields):** For any prime number $p$, the ideal $p\mathbb{Z}$ is maximal in $\mathbb{Z}$. The quotient ring $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z} = \mathbb{F}_p$ forms a finite field of order $p$, forming the foundation of modern cryptography.

## 5. Vector Spaces and the Coordinate Isomorphism Theorem

### 5.1 Formal Definition of a Vector Space
Let $\mathbb{F}$ be an algebraic field. A **vector space** $(V, +, \cdot)$ over $\mathbb{F}$ is a set $V$ equipped with vector addition $+: V \times V \to V$ and scalar multiplication $\cdot: \mathbb{F} \times V \to V$ such that:
1. $(V, +)$ is an Abelian group (closure, associativity, commutativity, zero vector $\mathbf{0}$, additive inverse $-\mathbf{v}$).
2. Scalar multiplication satisfies compatibility and distributivity:
   - $a \cdot (b \cdot \mathbf{v}) = (a \cdot b) \cdot \mathbf{v}$
   - $1 \cdot \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}$
   - $a \cdot (\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) = a \cdot \mathbf{u} + a \cdot \mathbf{v}$
   - $(a + b) \cdot \mathbf{v} = a \cdot \mathbf{v} + b \cdot \mathbf{v}$

### 5.2 Basis and Coordinates
Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional vector space over $\mathbb{F}$. An ordered set of vectors $\mathcal{B} = (\mathbf{b}_1, \mathbf{b}_2, \dots, \mathbf{b}_n)$ is a **basis** for $V$ if:
1. $\mathcal{B}$ is **linearly independent**: $\sum_{i=1}^n c_i \mathbf{b}_i = \mathbf{0} \implies c_1 = c_2 = \dots = c_n = 0$.
2. $\mathcal{B}$ **spans** $V$: $\operatorname{span}(\mathcal{B}) = V$.

Every vector $\mathbf{v} \in V$ can be expressed as a linear combination of basis vectors:
$$\mathbf{v} = c_1 \mathbf{b}_1 + c_2 \mathbf{b}_2 + \dots + c_n \mathbf{b}_n \tag{3}$$

#### Lemma: Uniqueness of Coordinate Representations
*Claim:* The scalar coefficients $(c_1, \dots, c_n)$ in Equation (3) are **strictly unique**.  
*Proof:* Suppose $\mathbf{v}$ could also be written as $\mathbf{v} = \sum_{i=1}^n d_i \mathbf{b}_i$. Subtracting the two representations:
$$\mathbf{0} = \mathbf{v} - \mathbf{v} = \sum_{i=1}^n (c_i - d_i) \mathbf{b}_i$$
By linear independence of the basis $\mathcal{B}$, every coefficient must vanish:
$$c_i - d_i = 0 \implies c_i = d_i \quad \forall \, i \in \{1, \dots, n\} \quad \blacksquare$$

This unique tuple defines the **coordinate vector** of $\mathbf{v}$ relative to basis $\mathcal{B}$:
$$[\mathbf{v}]_\mathcal{B} = \begin{pmatrix} c_1 \\ c_2 \\ \vdots \\ c_n \end{pmatrix} \in \mathbb{F}^n \tag{4}$$

### 5.3 The Coordinate Isomorphism Theorem: Proof of Equivalence Between Abstract Vectors and Coordinate Tuples

We now prove the central theorem bridging abstract geometry and concrete computational linear algebra:

> **The Coordinate Mapping Theorem:**  
> Let $\mathcal{B} = (\mathbf{b}_1, \dots, \mathbf{b}_n)$ be a basis for an $n$-dimensional vector space $V$ over field $\mathbb{F}$.  
> The coordinate mapping:
> $$\Phi_\mathcal{B}: V \to \mathbb{F}^n, \qquad \Phi_\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{v}) = [\mathbf{v}]_\mathcal{B}$$
> is a **vector space isomorphism** ($V \cong \mathbb{F}^n$). That is, $\Phi_\mathcal{B}$ is a bijective linear transformation.

#### Complete Step-by-Step Proof

1. **Proof of Linearity:**  
   Let $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in V$ and $\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{F}$.  
   Let $\mathbf{u} = \sum_{i=1}^n u_i \mathbf{b}_i$ and $\mathbf{v} = \sum_{i=1}^n v_i \mathbf{b}_i$.  
   Then:
   $$\alpha \mathbf{u} + \beta \mathbf{v} = \alpha \left(\sum_{i=1}^n u_i \mathbf{b}_i\right) + \beta \left(\sum_{i=1}^n v_i \mathbf{b}_i\right) = \sum_{i=1}^n (\alpha u_i + \beta v_i) \mathbf{b}_i$$
   By definition of coordinates relative to $\mathcal{B}$:
   $$[\alpha \mathbf{u} + \beta \mathbf{v}]_\mathcal{B} = \begin{pmatrix} \alpha u_1 + \beta v_1 \\ \vdots \\ \alpha u_n + \beta v_n \end{pmatrix} = \alpha \begin{pmatrix} u_1 \\ \vdots \\ u_n \end{pmatrix} + \beta \begin{pmatrix} v_1 \\ \vdots \\ v_n \end{pmatrix} = \alpha [\mathbf{u}]_\mathcal{B} + \beta [\mathbf{v}]_\mathcal{B}$$
   Hence $\Phi_\mathcal{B}$ preserves both vector addition and scalar multiplication.

2. **Proof of Injectivity (One-to-One):**  
   We examine the kernel of $\Phi_\mathcal{B}$:
   $$\ker(\Phi_\mathcal{B}) = \{\mathbf{v} \in V \mid \Phi_\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{v}) = \mathbf{0} \in \mathbb{F}^n\}$$
   If $[\mathbf{v}]_\mathcal{B} = \mathbf{0}$, then $\mathbf{v} = 0 \mathbf{b}_1 + \dots + 0 \mathbf{b}_n = \mathbf{0}$.  
   Therefore, $\ker(\Phi_\mathcal{B}) = \{\mathbf{0}\}$. A linear map with a trivial kernel is strictly **injective**.

3. **Proof of Surjectivity (Onto):**  
   Let $\mathbf{c} = (c_1, \dots, c_n)^T$ be an arbitrary column vector in $\mathbb{F}^n$.  
   Construct the vector $\mathbf{w} = \sum_{i=1}^n c_i \mathbf{b}_i \in V$.  
   By inspection, $\Phi_\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{w}) = [\mathbf{w}]_\mathcal{B} = \mathbf{c}$.  
   Thus, every element of $\mathbb{F}^n$ has a pre-image in $V$, proving $\Phi_\mathcal{B}$ is **surjective**.

Because $\Phi_\mathcal{B}$ is a bijective homomorphism, it is an **isomorphism** ($V \cong \mathbb{F}^n$).  
**Conclusion:** Any abstract vector calculation (in function spaces, quantum Hilbert states, or dimensional spaces) is formally equivalent to arithmetic on column tuples in $\mathbb{F}^n$. $\blacksquare$

## 6. Gaussian Elimination and Echelon Forms

Once vectors are represented as coordinates in $\mathbb{F}^n$, linear systems and subspace bases are computed via **Gaussian elimination**.

### 6.1 Elementary Row Operations as Invertible Linear Operators
There are three elementary row operations on a matrix $A \in \mathbb{F}^{m \times n}$:
1. **Row Swap ($R_i \leftrightarrow R_j$):** Exchanging two rows.
2. **Row Scaling ($R_i \leftarrow k R_i, \; k \neq 0$):** Multiplying a row by a non-zero scalar.
3. **Row Addition ($R_i \leftarrow R_i + k R_j$):** Adding a scalar multiple of row $j$ to row $i$.

Every elementary row operation corresponds to multiplying $A$ on the left by an **elementary matrix** $E$:
$$A \xrightarrow{\text{row op}} E A$$
Because $k \neq 0$, every elementary matrix is strictly invertible ($E^{-1}$ exists and is of the exact same type). Consequently:
$$\ker(E A) = \ker(A) \quad \text{and} \quad \operatorname{row}(E A) = \operatorname{row}(A) \tag{5}$$
**Elementary row operations leave the null space and row space strictly invariant.**

### 6.2 Row Echelon Form (REF) vs. Reduced Row Echelon Form (RREF)
A matrix is in **Row Echelon Form (REF)** if:
1. All zero rows are at the bottom.
2. The leading entry (pivot) of each non-zero row is strictly to the right of the leading entry of the row above it.
3. All entries below a pivot are zero.

A matrix is in **Reduced Row Echelon Form (RREF)** if, additionally:
4. The leading entry in each non-zero row is equal to $1$.
5. Each pivot $1$ is the *only* non-zero entry in its entire column.

> **Theorem (Uniqueness of RREF):**  
> While a matrix has infinitely many row echelon forms, its **Reduced Row Echelon Form $\operatorname{rref}(A)$ is strictly unique**.

### 7.2 The Rank-Nullity Theorem

> **The Rank-Nullity Theorem (Dimension Theorem):**  
> Let $V$ and $W$ be vector spaces over field $\mathbb{F}$ with $\dim(V) = n$.  
> For any linear transformation $T: V \to W$:
> $$\operatorname{dim}(\ker(T)) + \operatorname{dim}(\operatorname{im}(T)) = \dim(V) \iff \operatorname{nullity}(T) + \operatorname{rank}(T) = n \tag{6}$$

#### Rigorous Proof

1. Let $\operatorname{nullity}(T) = k = \dim(\ker(T))$.
2. Choose a basis for the kernel: $(\mathbf{u}_1, \dots, \mathbf{u}_k)$ of $\ker(T)$.
3. By the Basis Extension Theorem, extend this basis to a complete basis of $V$:
   $$\mathcal{B}_V = (\mathbf{u}_1, \dots, \mathbf{u}_k, \mathbf{w}_1, \dots, \mathbf{w}_{n-k})$$
4. We claim that the image set $S = (T(\mathbf{w}_1), \dots, T(\mathbf{w}_{n-k}))$ forms a basis for $\operatorname{im}(T)$:
   * **Span:** Any $\mathbf{v} \in V$ can be written as $\mathbf{v} = \sum_{i=1}^k c_i \mathbf{u}_i + \sum_{j=1}^{n-k} d_j \mathbf{w}_j$.  
     Applying $T$:
     $$T(\mathbf{v}) = \sum_{i=1}^k c_i T(\mathbf{u}_i) + \sum_{j=1}^{n-k} d_j T(\mathbf{w}_j) = \mathbf{0} + \sum_{j=1}^{n-k} d_j T(\mathbf{w}_j)$$
     Hence $S$ spans $\operatorname{im}(T)$.
   * **Linear Independence:** Suppose $\sum_{j=1}^{n-k} d_j T(\mathbf{w}_j) = \mathbf{0}$.  
     By linearity: $T\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n-k} d_j \mathbf{w}_j\right) = \mathbf{0}$.  
     This implies $\sum_{j=1}^{n-k} d_j \mathbf{w}_j \in \ker(T)$.  
     Therefore, it must be expressible in terms of the kernel basis:
     $$\sum_{j=1}^{n-k} d_j \mathbf{w}_j = \sum_{i=1}^k c_i \mathbf{u}_i \implies \sum_{i=1}^k (-c_i) \mathbf{u}_i + \sum_{j=1}^{n-k} d_j \mathbf{w}_j = \mathbf{0}$$
     Because $\mathcal{B}_V$ is linearly independent, all coefficients must vanish: $c_i = 0$ and $d_j = 0$ for all $j$.  
     Thus, $S$ is linearly independent.
5. Since $S$ is a linearly independent spanning set for $\operatorname{im}(T)$, it is a basis.  
   Therefore, $\operatorname{rank}(T) = \dim(\operatorname{im}(T)) = n - k$.  
   Adding the two dimensions:
   $$\operatorname{nullity}(T) + \operatorname{rank}(T) = k + (n - k) = n \quad \blacksquare$$

## 8. Worked Case Study: Coordinate Mappings on Polynomial Function Spaces

To see the coordinate isomorphism in action on an abstract non-Euclidean vector space, consider the space of real polynomials of degree at most 2:
$$\mathcal{P}_2 = \{a_0 + a_1 x + a_2 x^2 \mid a_0, a_1, a_2 \in \mathbb{R}\}$$
equipped with the ordered basis $\mathcal{B} = (1, x, x^2)$.

### Step 1: Mapping Derivatives to Matrices
Consider the derivative operator $D = \frac{d}{dx}: \mathcal{P}_2 \to \mathcal{P}_2$.  
Compute the action of $D$ on the basis vectors:
$$D(1) = 0 = 0 \cdot 1 + 0 \cdot x + 0 \cdot x^2 \implies [D(1)]_\mathcal{B} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$$
$$D(x) = 1 = 1 \cdot 1 + 0 \cdot x + 0 \cdot x^2 \implies [D(x)]_\mathcal{B} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$$
$$D(x^2) = 2x = 0 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot x + 0 \cdot x^2 \implies [D(x^2)]_\mathcal{B} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 2 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$$

The matrix representation of $D$ relative to basis $\mathcal{B}$ is:
$$[D]_\mathcal{B} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \tag{7}$$

### Step 2: Rank-Nullity Verification
* **Matrix Size:** $n = 3$ columns (domain dimension $\dim(\mathcal{P}_2) = 3$).
* **Columns with pivots:** Columns 1 and 2 (indices 1, 2) $\implies \operatorname{rank}([D]_\mathcal{B}) = 2$.  
  The image is $\operatorname{span}(1, x) = \mathcal{P}_1$, which has dimension 2.
* **Nullity:** Free column is Column 0 $\implies \operatorname{nullity}([D]_\mathcal{B}) = 3 - 2 = 1$.  
  The kernel is $\ker(D) = \{p(x) \mid p'(x) = 0\} = \operatorname{span}(1)$ (the constant polynomials), which has dimension 1.
* **Check:** $2 + 1 = 3 \implies \operatorname{rank} + \operatorname{nullity} = \dim(V)$. The theorem holds perfectly!

## 9. Foundational Terminology Glossary

* **Abelian Group:** A set with an associative, commutative binary operation containing an identity element and an inverse for every element.
* **Ring:** An algebraic structure with addition (forming an abelian group) and multiplication (associative and distributive over addition).
* **Ideal:** An additive subgroup of a ring that absorbs multiplication by any element in the ring ($r \cdot x \in I$).
* **Quotient Ring ($R/I$):** The partition of a ring into cosets modulo an ideal, forming a new ring.
* **Field:** A commutative ring with non-zero unity where every non-zero element has a multiplicative inverse.
* **Fraction Field:** The smallest field containing a given integral domain (e.g., $\mathbb{Q} = \operatorname{Frac}(\mathbb{Z})$).
* **Vector Space Isomorphism:** A bijective linear mapping between vector spaces that preserves addition and scalar multiplication ($V \cong \mathbb{F}^n$).
* **Coordinate Mapping:** The canonical isomorphism $\Phi_\mathcal{B}: V \to \mathbb{F}^n$ mapping an abstract vector $\mathbf{v}$ to its unique coordinate tuple relative to basis $\mathcal{B}$.
* **RREF (Reduced Row Echelon Form):** The unique canonical matrix echelon form where pivots are 1 and are the sole non-zero entries in their columns.
* **Rank-Nullity Theorem:** The foundational theorem proving that for any linear map from an $n$-dimensional domain, $\operatorname{rank} + \operatorname{nullity} = n$.
