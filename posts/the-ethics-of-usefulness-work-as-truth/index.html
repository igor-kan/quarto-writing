The Ethics of Usefulness: Work, Deliverables, and the Search for Authentic Grounding

Why tangible output and authentic utility—not solipsistic happiness or performative busywork—anchor human existence in reality.

1. The Miyazaki Dilemma: Earning One’s Space

In the documentary archives documenting Studio Ghibli’s grueling production cycles, an elderly Hayao Miyazaki is frequently captured hunched over a drawing desk, clutching a cigarette, groaning under the weight of an animation deadline. At one point, amidst the exhaustion of drafting thousands of individual frames by hand, he offers an unvarnished self-examination that cuts directly to the core of the human condition:

“If I can entertain people maybe I deserve to exist. I have this repressed need to feel useful.”
— Hayao Miyazaki

Coming from one of the most celebrated creative minds in cinematic history—a director whose films (Spirited Away, Princess Mononoke, My Neighbor Totoro, The Boy and the Heron) have enriched the imaginations of hundreds of millions of people—the quote sounds startling. Modern ears, conditioned by contemporary therapeutic culture, are trained to reflexively pathologize such a statement:

  • “You don’t need to earn your existence!”
  • “You are inherently worthy simply by being!”
  • “Don’t tie your self-worth to productivity!”

Yet Miyazaki’s admission is neither neurosis nor self-flagellation. It is an articulation of an ancient, unyielding ontological truth: the human soul cannot sustain itself on self-referential affirmation alone.

When existence is declared self-justifying without reference to any contribution beyond the boundaries of the ego, meaning rapidly collapses into solipsism, boredom, and existential dread. Miyazaki understood that personal existence feels weightless until it is anchored to something outside oneself—specifically, to the demanding, rigorous labor of being genuinely useful to others.

2. The Bankruptcy of Unanchored “Personal Happiness”

Much of modern discourse encourages people to treat personal happiness as the supreme objective of life:

Goal: Maximize subjective positive emotion → Minimize discomfort → Achieve fulfillment

This formulation is psychologically bankrupt. It collapses under two fundamental structural flaws: the Paradox of Hedonism and the Absence of Reality-Testing.

The Paradox of Hedonism

As Viktor Frankl noted in Man’s Search for Meaning, happiness cannot be pursued directly:

“Happiness must happen, and the same holds for success: you have to let it happen by not caring about it… Success will follow you precisely because you had forgotten to think of it.”

When subjective emotion is made the metric of a life, every transient wave of sadness, boredom, or fatigue is perceived as an existential crisis. The individual is trapped on the Hedonic Treadmill: biological habituation ensures that any pleasant state quickly normalizes, demanding greater stimulation, pampering, and self-absorption to maintain the same baseline.

The Malaise of Modern Self-Care Culture

Modern “wellness” culture often mistakes the retreat from difficulty for healing. When people isolate themselves from the friction of demanding tasks, their tolerance for stress atrophies. In the absence of demanding goals, the mind turns inward, cannibalizing itself through hyper-vigilant rumination.

True self-esteem is not an emotional state you can induce by chanting mantras in a mirror. Self-esteem is a reputation you acquire with your own subconscious mind. Your subconscious is not gullible: it tracks your actual competence, your follow-through, and your tangible utility to the surrounding world. If you produce nothing of value, your nervous system registers your obsolescence regardless of how many soothing affirmations you repeat.

3. Deliverables as Contact with Objective Truth

Why does work—specifically the creation of concrete deliverables—provide such potent psychological grounding?

Because the deliverable is an encounter with objective reality.

In the realm of pure thought, conversation, and social performance, an individual can maintain delusions of grandeur indefinitely. You can believe you are a brilliant strategist, an undiscovered novelist, a master engineer, or a profound thinker without ever testing those hypotheses against the physical world.

The moment you sit down to build a deliverable, however, your ego collides with reality:

Domain The Ego’s Illusion The Deliverable’s Verdict
Software Engineering “My architectural intuition is flawless.” The compiler rejects the code; the distributed cluster deadlocks under load.
Mechanical Design “This structure looks elegant.” The beam buckles under shear stress; the CAD simulation fails the load test.
Mathematics & Logic “This proposition must be true.” A counterexample invalidates line 14 of the proof.
Animation & Art “I have boundless creative vision.” The hand cannot render the dynamic curve of the wind on frame 42.
Writing & Scholarship “I have profound insights on this subject.” The paragraph is vague, redundant, and falls apart upon critical reading. 
The compiler does not care about your intentions.
The laws of thermodynamics do not care about your self-esteem.
The market does not care about your effort.

This friction is not an obstacle to be avoided; it is a mercy. The physical and logical universe provides unyielding, uncorrupted feedback. When a program compiles, passes all regression tests, and handles production traffic without dropping a packet, you have extracted order from chaos. You have moved the state of the universe from \(S_0\) to \(S_1\).

That transition cannot be faked. It grounds the human psyche because it pulls you out of subjective solipsism and drops you firmly into reality.

4. The Pathological Plague of Performative Busywork

If real output anchors the soul, its counterfeit—performative busywork—corrupts it.

In his seminal treatise Bullshit Jobs, anthropologist David Graeber identified a profound spiritual crisis in post-industrial society: millions of professionals spending 40 to 60 hours every week executing tasks that even they privately acknowledge make no difference to the world.

The Pathology of Corporate Theater

Performative work consists of actions designed to signal diligence rather than produce utility: * Endlessly formatting slide decks that will never inform a real decision. * Organizing meetings to plan subsequent meetings. * Drafting voluminous policy documents that exist solely to satisfy bureaucratic compliance checklists. * Participating in circular email threads that generate high activity metrics with zero deliverable footprint.

Activity ≠ Productivity.
Pretending to be useful is physically and spiritually exhausting.

The Evolutionary Alarm of Uselessness

Why does performative labor cause such severe psychic decay?

Humans are obligate cooperative primates. For 99% of human evolutionary history, our survival on the savannah depended entirely on mutual interdependence within bands of 30 to 150 individuals: * The tracker who could not read tracks endangered the hunt. * The toolmaker who chipped brittle flint compromised the defense of the tribe. * The healer who misidentified medicinal plants cost lives.

Our neurobiology evolved an exquisite, hardwired alarm system: if you are not contributing real utility to the collective, your status is precarious and your existence is a burden.

When you spend your days shuffling papers or polishing vanity metrics, your primitive brain perceives the truth: “I am consuming resources without providing value; I am a liability.” This evolutionary mismatch manifests as chronic low-grade anxiety, impostor syndrome, cynicism, and anhedonia.

The antidote to impostor syndrome is not therapy to convince yourself that you are competent; the antidote is to do something genuinely difficult and objectively useful.

5. Alfred Adler and Gemeinschaftsgefühl

In psychological theory, this truth was most rigorously formulated by Alfred Adler, the founder of Individual Psychology. Adler broke with Sigmund Freud’s obsession with libido and drive satisfaction, arguing instead that human motivation is governed by the quest for significance within a social matrix.

Adler identified a single, decisive metric for psychological health: Gemeinschaftsgefühl (frequently translated as social interest, community feeling, or a sense of collective solidarity).

Adler argued that all neuroses stem from an exaggerated focus on the private self—protecting the ego from the possibility of failure while contributing nothing to the surrounding community. Conversely, the individual who redirects their attention outward—asking: “What problem can I solve? What load can I carry? How can my work lighten the burden of another?”—discovers that neurotic anxiety evaporates.

When Miyazaki said, “If I can entertain people maybe I deserve to exist,” he was speaking pure Adlerian psychology. He was subordinating his private ego to the joy and wonder of children watching his films. The grueling physical toll of the animation was bearable because the deliverable served a purpose that transcended himself.

6. Four Concrete Reasons to Work and Build High Competence

For anyone grappling with inertia, burnout, or a lack of motivation, the philosophy of usefulness provides four pragmatic, actionable arguments for rigorous labor and skill acquisition.

1. The Dissolution of Solipsistic Anxiety (Action as Grounding)

Neurotic rumination depends on the continuous activation of the brain’s Default Mode Network (DMN)—the neural circuitry responsible for self-referential thought, autobiographical memory, and social comparison. When you sit idly contemplating your flaws or worrying about your future, the DMN is hyperactive.

The moment you engage in demanding, skilled problem-solving—debugging an intricate race condition, translating a complex text, carving wood, or calculating structural loads—the brain switches to the Task-Positive Network (TPN).

The self recedes. The boundary between the worker and the work dissolves into what Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi called Flow. You cannot simultaneously ruminate on whether you are happy while focusing every ounce of your cognitive capacity on resolving a pointer error in C. Work is the most reliable neurological reset available to human beings.

2. Proof of Causal Efficacy (Agency)

Helplessness is the conviction that your actions cannot alter your environment. Depression frequently takes the form of learned helplessness: the belief that the future is predetermined and that your effort is meaningless.

A deliverable is empirical proof to the contrary. When you write a script that automates a painful manual workflow, you have exerted causal power over the universe. You have proven that your mind possesses the power to reshape the physical or digital environment.

Each completed artifact is a stone laid in the fortress of personal agency.

3. The Compounding Returns of the Craftsman Mindset

In So Good They Can’t Ignore You, Cal Newport dismantles the ubiquitous advice to “follow your passion.” Passion, Newport demonstrates, is rarely a starting point. It is an emergent byproduct of mastery.

Apprenticeship → Competence → Autonomy → Impact → Deep Passion

When you focus on acquiring rare, valuable skills: 1. You gain leverage and autonomy over your time and conditions. 2. You earn the respect of peers who understand the difficulty of the craft. 3. You solve problems that matter, generating profound satisfaction.

The beginner who quits because the early stages of learning to code, write, or design are frustrating never reaches the threshold where competence makes the work exhilarating. Usefulness demands that you endure the awkwardness of the novice stage to earn the power of the master.

4. Trans-Personal Meaning (The Gift to the Commons)

Civilization is an accumulated stockpile of deliverables created by people who came before us: * The sanitation systems that keep typhus at bay were designed by civil engineers. * The operating systems that power our global logistics were built by kernel programmers. * The vaccines that eradicated smallpox were formulated by biochemists. * The stories that sustain our spirits during tragedy were penned by authors.

To demand the benefits of this vast civilizational inheritance while refusing to contribute anything back into the storehouse is a parasitic posture. To produce deliverables—to be useful—is to pay your rent to human history.

7. The Usefulness Matrix: Evaluating Your Daily Output

To diagnose whether your daily labor is anchoring you or draining you, evaluate your work across two fundamental axes: Reality Friction (how brutally your work is tested against objective facts) and True Social Utility (how directly your work improves the lives of others).

  • Quadrant 1 (The Craftsman / Builder): High reality friction combined with direct social utility. This is the sweet spot of human meaning. Work in this quadrant is demanding, humbling, and deeply gratifying.
  • Quadrant 3 (The Bureaucratic Drifter): Low reality friction (nobody checks if the work is right) and low social utility (nobody uses it). This is the breeding ground for depression and cynicism.

If you find yourself stranded in Quadrant 3, no amount of mindfulness or stoic meditation will cure your dissatisfaction. You must pivot your labor toward Quadrant 1: find real problems, pick up real tools, and build things that work.

8. A Daily Operating System for the Useful Life

How do you translate the ethics of usefulness into concrete daily behavior? Adopt four foundational practices:

1. Perform the Daily Deliverable Audit

At the conclusion of each working day, ask yourself a single, uncompromising question:

“What artifact did I bring into existence today that did not exist this morning?”

It does not need to be a completed cathedral. It can be: * A cleanly refactored function that eliminates a memory leak. * A well-structured explanatory note that clarifies a difficult concept for a colleague. * A single page of prose that communicates an essential argument. * A resolved bug ticket.

If your answer for five consecutive days is “I attended eight meetings and replied to forty emails,” you are drifting into performative theater. Reclaim your calendar and produce an artifact.

2. Embrace the Resistance of the Medium

When a task resists your effort—when the code refuses to compile, the writing is muddy, or the mathematical theorem resists resolution—do not interpret that resistance as a personal failure or an excuse to quit.

Recognize resistance as the grain of the wood. The resistance is proof that you have engaged with something real. Lean into it with patience and craftsmanship.

3. Subordinate Ego to Utility

When deciding what to work on, resist the temptation to ask: “What will make me look impressive?” Instead, ask:

“What is broken that I have the competence to fix?”
“What complex topic can I clarify so others do not have to struggle through the same confusion?”

When utility guides your choices, vanity drops away. You stop worrying about whether you are a genius and start focusing on whether the bolt is tightened.

9. Conclusion: The Dignity of the Workbench

Hayao Miyazaki is now well into his eighties. He has announced his retirement multiple times, only to return to his desk at Studio Ghibli months later, picking up his 2B pencil to begin storyboarding yet another film.

He does not return for wealth; he has plenty. He does not return for acclaim; he has won every honor cinema can bestow. He returns because, in the absence of the workbench, existence loses its mooring. He returns because he has a repressed need to be useful, and because creating stories that enchant a child is how he justifies taking up space on this earth.

You do not need to direct an Oscar-winning masterpiece to claim that dignity.

You need only to find an honest workbench: * Open the text editor. * Pick up the tool. * Solve the concrete problem. * Deliver the work.

In the reality of the finished deliverable, you will find a quiet, unshakeable truth that no self-help book can ever manufacture: you were needed, you answered, and you made the world work.

When deciding what to work on, resist the temptation to ask: *"What will make me look impressive?"* Instead, ask:

> *"What is broken that I have the competence to fix?"*  
> *"What complex topic can I clarify so others do not have to struggle through the same confusion?"*

When utility guides your choices, vanity drops away. You stop worrying about whether you are a genius and start focusing on whether the bolt is tightened.

---

## 9. Conclusion: The Dignity of the Workbench

Hayao Miyazaki is now well into his eighties. He has announced his retirement multiple times, only to return to his desk at Studio Ghibli months later, picking up his 2B pencil to begin storyboarding yet another film. 

He does not return for wealth; he has plenty. He does not return for acclaim; he has won every honor cinema can bestow. He returns because, in the absence of the workbench, existence loses its mooring. He returns because he has a repressed need to be useful, and because creating stories that enchant a child is how he justifies taking up space on this earth.

You do not need to direct an Oscar-winning masterpiece to claim that dignity. 

You need only to find an honest workbench:
* Open the text editor.
* Pick up the tool.
* Solve the concrete problem.
* Deliver the work.

In the reality of the finished deliverable, you will find a quiet, unshakeable truth that no self-help book can ever manufacture: **you were needed, you answered, and you made the world work.**
