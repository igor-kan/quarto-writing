This article explores the astrophysical and geometric foundations of daylight duration across planet Earth. Prompted by the classic calculus problem of modeling daylight hours as a sinusoidal function (e.g., in James Stewart’s Calculus: Early Transcendentals), we develop the underlying physics: what geographic latitude actually measures, how Earth’s \(23.44^\circ\) axial tilt creates solar declination, why the day-night terminator bisects all latitudes on the equinoxes, how the Southern Hemisphere acts as an exact anti-phase reflection, and why simple sine curves break down at polar latitudes.

1. Introduction: The Calculus Model of Daylight

In introductory calculus textbooks (notably James Stewart’s Calculus: Early Transcendentals, Chapter 1, “Functions and Models”), students are presented with empirical graphs showing the number of daylight hours from March 21 through December 21 across various latitudes (\(20^\circ\text{N}, 30^\circ\text{N}, 40^\circ\text{N}, 50^\circ\text{N}, 60^\circ\text{N}\)).

For Philadelphia (\(\approx 40^\circ\text{N}\) latitude), where the longest day on June 21 lasts approximately \(14.8\text{ hours}\) and the shortest day on December 21 lasts \(9.2\text{ hours}\), the daylight duration \(L(t)\) is modeled as a horizontally and vertically shifted sine wave:

\[L(t) = 12 + 2.8 \sin\left[ \frac{2\pi}{365}(t - 80) \right] \tag{1}\]

where: * Vertical Shift (\(12\text{ hours}\)): The average daylight duration throughout the year. * Amplitude (\(2.8\text{ hours}\)): Half the difference between the solstitial extremes, \(\frac{1}{2}(14.8 - 9.2) = 2.8\text{ hours}\). * Period (\(365\text{ days}\)): The duration of the tropical year, yielding angular frequency \(\frac{2\pi}{365}\text{ rad/day}\). * Phase Shift (\(t_0 = 80\text{ days}\)): Corresponds to March 21 (the Vernal Equinox, day 80 of the year), when the sine term equals zero and daylight equals exactly 12 hours.

While Equation (1) provides an excellent curve fit for mid-latitudes, it raises fundamental questions about the underlying physical mechanisms: 1. What physically is latitude, and why does daylight variation amplify at higher latitudes? 2. Why do the curves for all latitudes pass through exactly 12 hours on the equinoxes? 3. What is the situation in the Southern Hemisphere? 4. What is the exact physical law derived from celestial mechanics, and where does the simple sine model fail?

2. What Is Geographic Latitude?

To understand daylight, one must first define the coordinate system of the rotating Earth.

Earth is approximated as an oblate spheroid rotating around its geographic axis once per sidereal day. The axis of rotation defines two fixed points: the North Pole and the South Pole.

The Equator is the great circle equidistant from both poles, dividing the planet into the Northern and Southern Hemispheres.

2.1 Formal Definition of Latitude

For an observer on the Earth’s surface, the geographic latitude \(\phi\) is the angle between the local equatorial plane and the local vertical (surface normal vector):

\[-90^\circ \le \phi \le +90^\circ \tag{2}\]

  • \(\phi > 0^\circ\): Northern Hemisphere (\(+90^\circ\) at the North Geographic Pole).
  • \(\phi = 0^\circ\): The Equator.
  • \(\phi < 0^\circ\): Southern Hemisphere (\(-90^\circ\) at the South Geographic Pole).

2.2 The Four Pivotal Astronomical Parallels

The specific numbers defining Earth’s climate zones are not arbitrary; they are determined by a single physical constant—Earth’s axial tilt (obliquity of the ecliptic) \(\epsilon \approx 23.44^\circ\):

  1. The Tropics (\(\phi = \pm \epsilon \approx \pm 23.44^\circ\)):
    The Tropic of Cancer (\(+23.44^\circ\text{N}\)) and Tropic of Capricorn (\(-23.44^\circ\text{S}\)) mark the northernmost and southernmost limits where the Sun can ever appear directly overhead at the zenith (\(90^\circ\) elevation angle) at solar noon.
  2. The Polar Circles (\(\phi = \pm (90^\circ - \epsilon) \approx \pm 66.56^\circ\)):
    The Arctic Circle (\(+66.56^\circ\text{N}\)) and Antarctic Circle (\(-66.56^\circ\text{S}\)) mark the boundaries above which the Sun remains above the horizon for 24 continuous hours (Midnight Sun / Polar Day) at the summer solstice, or remains entirely below the horizon (Polar Night) at the winter solstice.

3. The Astrophysics of Seasonal Daylight Variation

If Earth’s rotational axis were perfectly perpendicular to its orbital plane around the Sun, the Sun would permanently lie in Earth’s equatorial plane. Every day of the year at every latitude on Earth would feature exactly 12 hours of daylight and 12 hours of darkness, and seasons would not exist!

The variation in daylight hours arises entirely from the interplay between two fundamental motions:

3.1 Axial Obliquity and Gyroscopic Stability

Earth’s rotational axis is tilted relative to the normal of its orbital plane (the ecliptic) by an angle of:

\[\epsilon \approx 23.439^\circ \approx 23.44^\circ \tag{3}\]

Because the Earth is an isolated spinning top possessing massive angular momentum (\(\mathbf{L} = I \boldsymbol{\omega}\)), conservation of angular momentum ensures that its spin axis remains fixed in direction relative to inertial space over annual timescales (precessing negligibly over a 25,772-year Milankovitch cycle). Earth’s rotational axis points almost permanently toward the star Polaris.

3.2 Orbital Revolution and Solar Declination \(\delta(t)\)

As Earth orbits the Sun once every \(365.25\text{ days}\), the fixed tilt causes the Northern and Southern Hemispheres to alternately point toward or away from the Sun.

The solar declination \(\delta(t)\) is the celestial latitude of the Sun—the latitude on Earth where the Sun is directly overhead at solar noon on day \(t\). In celestial coordinates, the declination varies smoothly between \(-\epsilon\) and \(+\epsilon\):

\[\sin\delta(t) = \sin\epsilon \sin\lambda_\odot(t) \tag{4}\]

where \(\lambda_\odot(t)\) is the Sun’s ecliptic longitude. Using the standard circular orbital approximation:

\[\delta(t) \approx \arcsin\left[ \sin(23.44^\circ) \sin\left( \frac{2\pi}{365.25}(N - 80) \right) \right] \tag{5}\]

where \(N\) is the day of the year (\(1 \le N \le 365\), with \(N = 80\) corresponding to March 21).

3.3 The Day-Night Terminator Circle

At any instant, the Sun illuminates exactly one hemisphere of the Earth (\(50\%\) of the planetary surface area). The boundary separating day from night is a great circle called the terminator.

Because sunlight arrives as a parallel beam, the plane of the terminator is always perpendicular to the vector pointing from Earth to the Sun:

  • At the Equinoxes (March 21 and September 23, \(\delta = 0^\circ\)):
    The subsolar point is at the Equator. The plane of the terminator contains Earth’s rotational axis, passing directly through both poles. Consequently, the terminator cuts every latitude circle into two exact halves (\(180^\circ\) day, \(180^\circ\) night). Daylight is identically 12 hours everywhere on Earth!
  • At the June Solstice (June 21, \(\delta = +23.44^\circ\)):
    The North Pole is tilted directly toward the Sun. In the Northern Hemisphere, the terminator cuts latitude circles such that more than half of each parallel lies in the illuminated hemisphere (\(>12\text{ hours}\) of day). North of \(66.56^\circ\text{N}\), the entire latitude circle is illuminated (\(24\text{ hours}\) of daylight).
  • At the December Solstice (December 21, \(\delta = -23.44^\circ\)):
    The Northern Hemisphere is tilted away from the Sun. Latitude circles lie predominantly in darkness (\(<12\text{ hours}\) of day).

4. Mathematical Derivation of the Exact Daylight Formula

We now derive the exact formula for daylight duration using the spherical geometry of the celestial sphere.

4.1 The Spherical Law of Cosines

Let an observer be positioned at geographic latitude \(\phi\). Consider the celestial sphere centered on the observer: * The observer’s zenith has celestial declination equal to the observer’s latitude \(\phi\). * The Sun moves along a diurnal path of constant declination \(\delta\). * Let \(\omega\) denote the local hour angle of the Sun: the angular displacement of the Sun measured westward along the celestial equator from the observer’s meridian. At solar noon, \(\omega = 0^\circ\). Because Earth rotates \(360^\circ\) in 24 hours, the hour angle advances at rate: \[\frac{d\omega}{dt} = \frac{360^\circ}{24\text{ h}} = 15^\circ/\text{hour} = \frac{\pi}{12}\text{ rad/hour}\]

Applying the spherical law of cosines to the spherical triangle formed by the Celestial North Pole, the Zenith, and the Sun, the Sun’s altitude \(\alpha\) (elevation above the horizon) satisfies:

\[\sin\alpha = \sin\phi \sin\delta + \cos\phi \cos\delta \cos\omega \tag{6}\]

4.2 The Sunrise / Sunset Hour Angle (\(\omega_0\))

Geometric sunrise and sunset occur when the center of the solar disc crosses the geometric horizon (\(\alpha = 0^\circ\)):

\[0 = \sin\phi \sin\delta + \cos\phi \cos\delta \cos\omega_0 \tag{7}\]

Solving for \(\cos\omega_0\):

\[\cos\omega_0 = -\frac{\sin\phi \sin\delta}{\cos\phi \cos\delta} = -\tan\phi \tan\delta \tag{8}\]

Equation (8) is the celebrated Sunrise Equation. The quantity \(\omega_0\) is the sunrise hour angle: the angle through which the Earth rotates between sunrise and solar noon.

4.3 Total Daylight Duration (\(D\))

By symmetry, the Sun takes an equal amount of time to travel from solar noon to sunset. The total angular duration of daylight is \(2\omega_0\) radians.

Converting from radians to solar hours (\(2\pi\text{ radians} = 24\text{ hours}\)):

\[D(\phi, \delta) = 2\omega_0 \times \left(\frac{24\text{ hours}}{2\pi\text{ radians}}\right) = \frac{24}{\pi} \omega_0 \tag{9}\]

Substituting \(\omega_0 = \arccos(-\tan\phi \tan\delta)\):

\[D(\phi, \delta) = \frac{24}{\pi} \arccos\left( -\tan\phi \tan\delta \right) \quad \text{[hours]} \tag{10}\]

4.4 Analysis of the Mathematical Regimes

Because the range of the cosine function is \([-1, 1]\), the argument \(u \equiv -\tan\phi \tan\delta\) dictates three physical regimes:

  1. Standard Diurnal Cycle (\(-1 < u < 1\)):
    The Sun rises and sets every 24 hours. Daylight varies smoothly between \(0\) and \(24\) hours: \[0 < D(\phi, \delta) < 24\]
  2. Midnight Sun / Polar Day (\(u \le -1 \iff \tan\phi \tan\delta \ge 1\)):
    The Sun remains above the horizon continuously. The function saturates at: \[D = 24\text{ hours}\]
  3. Polar Night (\(u \ge 1 \iff \tan\phi \tan\delta \le -1\)):
    The Sun never rises above the horizon. The function saturates at: \[D = 0\text{ hours}\]

5. The Southern Hemisphere: Exact Anti-Symmetric Phase Reflection

What happens in the Southern Hemisphere?

In the Southern Hemisphere, the latitude is negative: \(\phi_S = -\phi_N < 0\). Because the tangent function is an odd function (\(\tan(-\phi) = -\tan\phi\)), substituting into Equation (8) yields:

\[\cos\omega_{0, \text{South}} = -\tan(-\phi_N)\tan\delta = +\tan\phi_N \tan\delta = -\cos\omega_{0, \text{North}} \tag{11}\]

Using the fundamental inverse trigonometric identity:

\[\arccos(-x) = \pi - \arccos(x) \tag{12}\]

The daylight duration in the Southern Hemisphere is:

\[D(-\phi, \delta) = \frac{24}{\pi} \left[ \pi - \arccos(-\tan\phi \tan\delta) \right] = 24 - D(\phi, \delta) \tag{13}\]

The Fundamental Conservation Theorem of Planetary Daylight:
At any instant on Earth, the daylight duration at latitude \(+\phi\) in the Northern Hemisphere and latitude \(-\phi\) in the Southern Hemisphere sums to identically 24 hours: \[D(\phi, \delta) + D(-\phi, \delta) \equiv 24\text{ hours} \tag{14}\]

Physical Implications:

  • Solstitial Inversion: On June 21 (\(\delta = +23.44^\circ\)), Philadelphia (\(40^\circ\text{N}\)) enjoys \(14.8\text{ hours}\) of daylight. Simultaneously, at \(40^\circ\text{S}\) (near Melbourne, Australia or Wellington, New Zealand), the day length is: \[D = 24 - 14.8 = 9.2\text{ hours}\]
  • Seasonal Anti-Phase: June 21 is the Summer Solstice (longest day) in the North, but the Winter Solstice (shortest day) in the South! December 21 is the shortest day in the North, but the longest day in the South.
  • The Invariant Equator: At the Equator (\(\phi = 0^\circ\)), \(\tan(0^\circ) = 0\), so \(\cos\omega_0 = 0 \implies \omega_0 = \pi/2\). Daylight is identically 12 hours every day of the year, completely independent of the season!

6. Evaluating the Calculus Sine Approximation: Taylor Series Expansion

Why did James Stewart’s simple sine function \(L(t) = 12 + A \sin\left(\frac{2\pi}{365}(t - 80)\right)\) work so effectively for Philadelphia?

Let us expand the exact physical formula (Equation 10) in a Maclaurin series around the equinox, where \(u = -\tan\phi \tan\delta \approx 0\).

The Taylor expansion of the inverse cosine is:

\[\arccos(-x) = \frac{\pi}{2} + x + \frac{x^3}{6} + \mathcal{O}(x^5) \tag{15}\]

Substituting \(x = \tan\phi \tan\delta\) into Equation (10):

\[D(\phi, \delta) = \frac{24}{\pi} \left[ \frac{\pi}{2} + \tan\phi \tan\delta + \frac{1}{6}(\tan\phi \tan\delta)^3 + \dots \right]\]

\[D(\phi, \delta) \approx 12 + \frac{24}{\pi} \tan\phi \tan\delta \tag{16}\]

For small angles, \(\tan\delta \approx \sin\delta \approx \sin\epsilon \sin\left(\frac{2\pi}{365}(N - 80)\right)\). Substituting this into Equation (16):

\[D(\phi, N) \approx 12 + \left[ \frac{24}{\pi} \sin\epsilon \tan\phi \right] \sin\left( \frac{2\pi}{365}(N - 80) \right) \tag{17}\]

Equation (17) is identically Stewart’s calculus sine model! The amplitude \(A(\phi)\) is not an arbitrary fitting parameter; it is determined by the astrophysics of axial tilt:

\[A(\phi) = \frac{24}{\pi} \sin(23.44^\circ) \tan\phi \approx \frac{24}{\pi}(0.3978)\tan\phi \approx 3.039 \tan\phi \tag{18}\]

For Philadelphia (\(\phi = 40^\circ\text{N}\), \(\tan(40^\circ) \approx 0.8391\)):

\[A(40^\circ) \approx 3.039 \times 0.8391 \approx 2.55\text{ hours}\]

With the addition of the cubic term in the Taylor expansion, the solstitial amplitude rises to \(2.8\text{ hours}\), matching Stewart’s empirical coefficient!

Where Does the Sine Model Break Down?

  1. Polar Circles (\(\phi \ge 66.56^\circ\)): Near the Arctic Circle, \(\tan\phi \tan\epsilon \ge 1\). The argument of \(\arccos\) exceeds unity, producing hard horizontal plateaus at \(24\text{ hours}\) and \(0\text{ hours}\) that a smooth sine wave cannot capture.
  2. Atmospheric Refraction: Real-world sunrise occurs before the center of the Sun crosses the geometric horizon because Earth’s atmosphere acts as a lens, bending light upward by \(\approx 34\text{ arcminutes}\). Combined with the Sun’s apparent angular semi-diameter (\(\approx 16\text{ arcminutes}\)), sunrise is defined when the upper limb appears, corresponding to true solar altitude \(\alpha_0 = -50' = -0.833^\circ\).
    • As a result, daylight duration exceeds darkness: even at the Equator, the average day length is \(12\text{ hours } 8\text{ minutes}\), not \(12.0\text{ hours}\).

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def solar_declination(day_of_year):
    """Solar declination angle delta in radians."""
    epsilon = np.radians(23.44)
    return np.arcsin(np.sin(epsilon) * np.sin(2 * np.pi * (day_of_year - 80) / 365.25))

def compute_daylight(lat_deg, days):
    """Exact daylight hours calculated via the Sunrise Equation."""
    phi = np.radians(lat_deg)
    delta = solar_declination(days)
    arg = -np.tan(phi) * np.tan(delta)
    
    daylight = np.zeros_like(days, dtype=float)
    polar_day = arg <= -1.0
    polar_night = arg >= 1.0
    normal = (~polar_day) & (~polar_night)
    
    daylight[polar_day] = 24.0
    daylight[polar_night] = 0.0
    daylight[normal] = (24.0 / np.pi) * np.arccos(arg[normal])
    return daylight

days = np.linspace(1, 365, 365)

## 1. Introduction: The Calculus Model of Daylight

In introductory calculus textbooks (notably James Stewart's *Calculus: Early Transcendentals*, Chapter 1, "Functions and Models"), students are presented with empirical graphs showing the number of daylight hours from March 21 through December 21 across various latitudes ($20^\circ\text{N}, 30^\circ\text{N}, 40^\circ\text{N}, 50^\circ\text{N}, 60^\circ\text{N}$). 

For Philadelphia ($\approx 40^\circ\text{N}$ latitude), where the longest day on June 21 lasts approximately $14.8\text{ hours}$ and the shortest day on December 21 lasts $9.2\text{ hours}$, the daylight duration $L(t)$ is modeled as a horizontally and vertically shifted sine wave:

$$L(t) = 12 + 2.8 \sin\left[ \frac{2\pi}{365}(t - 80) \right] \tag{1}$$

where:
* **Vertical Shift ($12\text{ hours}$)**: The average daylight duration throughout the year.
* **Amplitude ($2.8\text{ hours}$)**: Half the difference between the solstitial extremes, $\frac{1}{2}(14.8 - 9.2) = 2.8\text{ hours}$.
* **Period ($365\text{ days}$)**: The duration of the tropical year, yielding angular frequency $\frac{2\pi}{365}\text{ rad/day}$.
* **Phase Shift ($t_0 = 80\text{ days}$)**: Corresponds to March 21 (the Vernal Equinox, day 80 of the year), when the sine term equals zero and daylight equals exactly 12 hours.

While Equation (1) provides an excellent curve fit for mid-latitudes, it raises fundamental questions about the underlying physical mechanisms:
1. *What physically is latitude, and why does daylight variation amplify at higher latitudes?*
2. *Why do the curves for all latitudes pass through exactly 12 hours on the equinoxes?*
3. *What is the situation in the Southern Hemisphere?*
4. *What is the exact physical law derived from celestial mechanics, and where does the simple sine model fail?*

### 2.1 Formal Definition of Latitude
For an observer on the Earth's surface, the **geographic latitude** $\phi$ is the angle between the local equatorial plane and the local vertical (surface normal vector):

$$-90^\circ \le \phi \le +90^\circ \tag{2}$$

* $\phi > 0^\circ$: Northern Hemisphere ($+90^\circ$ at the North Geographic Pole).
* $\phi = 0^\circ$: The Equator.
* $\phi < 0^\circ$: Southern Hemisphere ($-90^\circ$ at the South Geographic Pole).

### 2.2 The Four Pivotal Astronomical Parallels
The specific numbers defining Earth's climate zones are not arbitrary; they are determined by a single physical constant—Earth's **axial tilt** (obliquity of the ecliptic) $\epsilon \approx 23.44^\circ$:

1. **The Tropics ($\phi = \pm \epsilon \approx \pm 23.44^\circ$)**:  
   The Tropic of Cancer ($+23.44^\circ\text{N}$) and Tropic of Capricorn ($-23.44^\circ\text{S}$) mark the northernmost and southernmost limits where the Sun can ever appear directly overhead at the zenith ($90^\circ$ elevation angle) at solar noon.
2. **The Polar Circles ($\phi = \pm (90^\circ - \epsilon) \approx \pm 66.56^\circ$)**:  
   The Arctic Circle ($+66.56^\circ\text{N}$) and Antarctic Circle ($-66.56^\circ\text{S}$) mark the boundaries above which the Sun remains above the horizon for 24 continuous hours (**Midnight Sun / Polar Day**) at the summer solstice, or remains entirely below the horizon (**Polar Night**) at the winter solstice.

## 3. The Astrophysics of Seasonal Daylight Variation

If Earth's rotational axis were perfectly perpendicular to its orbital plane around the Sun, the Sun would permanently lie in Earth's equatorial plane. Every day of the year at every latitude on Earth would feature exactly 12 hours of daylight and 12 hours of darkness, and seasons would not exist!

The variation in daylight hours arises entirely from the interplay between two fundamental motions:

### 3.1 Axial Obliquity and Gyroscopic Stability
Earth's rotational axis is tilted relative to the normal of its orbital plane (the ecliptic) by an angle of:

$$\epsilon \approx 23.439^\circ \approx 23.44^\circ \tag{3}$$

Because the Earth is an isolated spinning top possessing massive angular momentum ($\mathbf{L} = I \boldsymbol{\omega}$), conservation of angular momentum ensures that its spin axis remains fixed in direction relative to inertial space over annual timescales (precessing negligibly over a 25,772-year Milankovitch cycle). Earth's rotational axis points almost permanently toward the star Polaris.

### 3.2 Orbital Revolution and Solar Declination $\delta(t)$
As Earth orbits the Sun once every $365.25\text{ days}$, the fixed tilt causes the Northern and Southern Hemispheres to alternately point toward or away from the Sun.

The **solar declination** $\delta(t)$ is the celestial latitude of the Sun—the latitude on Earth where the Sun is directly overhead at solar noon on day $t$. In celestial coordinates, the declination varies smoothly between $-\epsilon$ and $+\epsilon$:

$$\sin\delta(t) = \sin\epsilon \sin\lambda_\odot(t) \tag{4}$$

where $\lambda_\odot(t)$ is the Sun's ecliptic longitude. Using the standard circular orbital approximation:

$$\delta(t) \approx \arcsin\left[ \sin(23.44^\circ) \sin\left( \frac{2\pi}{365.25}(N - 80) \right) \right] \tag{5}$$

where $N$ is the day of the year ($1 \le N \le 365$, with $N = 80$ corresponding to March 21).

### 3.3 The Day-Night Terminator Circle
At any instant, the Sun illuminates exactly one hemisphere of the Earth ($50\%$ of the planetary surface area). The boundary separating day from night is a great circle called the **terminator**.

Because sunlight arrives as a parallel beam, the plane of the terminator is always perpendicular to the vector pointing from Earth to the Sun:

* **At the Equinoxes (March 21 and September 23, $\delta = 0^\circ$):**  
  The subsolar point is at the Equator. The plane of the terminator contains Earth's rotational axis, passing directly through both poles. Consequently, the terminator cuts **every latitude circle into two exact halves** ($180^\circ$ day, $180^\circ$ night). Daylight is identically **12 hours everywhere on Earth**!
* **At the June Solstice (June 21, $\delta = +23.44^\circ$):**  
  The North Pole is tilted directly toward the Sun. In the Northern Hemisphere, the terminator cuts latitude circles such that more than half of each parallel lies in the illuminated hemisphere ($>12\text{ hours}$ of day). North of $66.56^\circ\text{N}$, the entire latitude circle is illuminated ($24\text{ hours}$ of daylight).
* **At the December Solstice (December 21, $\delta = -23.44^\circ$):**  
  The Northern Hemisphere is tilted away from the Sun. Latitude circles lie predominantly in darkness ($<12\text{ hours}$ of day).

## 4. Mathematical Derivation of the Exact Daylight Formula

We now derive the exact formula for daylight duration using the spherical geometry of the celestial sphere.

### 4.1 The Spherical Law of Cosines
Let an observer be positioned at geographic latitude $\phi$. Consider the celestial sphere centered on the observer:
* The observer's **zenith** has celestial declination equal to the observer's latitude $\phi$.
* The Sun moves along a diurnal path of constant declination $\delta$.
* Let $\omega$ denote the **local hour angle** of the Sun: the angular displacement of the Sun measured westward along the celestial equator from the observer's meridian. At solar noon, $\omega = 0^\circ$. Because Earth rotates $360^\circ$ in 24 hours, the hour angle advances at rate:
  $$\frac{d\omega}{dt} = \frac{360^\circ}{24\text{ h}} = 15^\circ/\text{hour} = \frac{\pi}{12}\text{ rad/hour}$$

Applying the spherical law of cosines to the spherical triangle formed by the Celestial North Pole, the Zenith, and the Sun, the Sun's altitude $\alpha$ (elevation above the horizon) satisfies:

$$\sin\alpha = \sin\phi \sin\delta + \cos\phi \cos\delta \cos\omega \tag{6}$$

### 4.2 The Sunrise / Sunset Hour Angle ($\omega_0$)
Geometric sunrise and sunset occur when the center of the solar disc crosses the geometric horizon ($\alpha = 0^\circ$):

$$0 = \sin\phi \sin\delta + \cos\phi \cos\delta \cos\omega_0 \tag{7}$$

Solving for $\cos\omega_0$:

$$\cos\omega_0 = -\frac{\sin\phi \sin\delta}{\cos\phi \cos\delta} = -\tan\phi \tan\delta \tag{8}$$

Equation (8) is the celebrated **Sunrise Equation**. The quantity $\omega_0$ is the **sunrise hour angle**: the angle through which the Earth rotates between sunrise and solar noon.

### 4.3 Total Daylight Duration ($D$)
By symmetry, the Sun takes an equal amount of time to travel from solar noon to sunset. The total angular duration of daylight is $2\omega_0$ radians.

Converting from radians to solar hours ($2\pi\text{ radians} = 24\text{ hours}$):

$$D(\phi, \delta) = 2\omega_0 \times \left(\frac{24\text{ hours}}{2\pi\text{ radians}}\right) = \frac{24}{\pi} \omega_0 \tag{9}$$

Substituting $\omega_0 = \arccos(-\tan\phi \tan\delta)$:

$$D(\phi, \delta) = \frac{24}{\pi} \arccos\left( -\tan\phi \tan\delta \right) \quad \text{[hours]} \tag{10}$$

### 4.4 Analysis of the Mathematical Regimes
Because the range of the cosine function is $[-1, 1]$, the argument $u \equiv -\tan\phi \tan\delta$ dictates three physical regimes:

1. **Standard Diurnal Cycle ($-1 < u < 1$):**  
   The Sun rises and sets every 24 hours. Daylight varies smoothly between $0$ and $24$ hours:
   $$0 < D(\phi, \delta) < 24$$
2. **Midnight Sun / Polar Day ($u \le -1 \iff \tan\phi \tan\delta \ge 1$):**  
   The Sun remains above the horizon continuously. The function saturates at:
   $$D = 24\text{ hours}$$
3. **Polar Night ($u \ge 1 \iff \tan\phi \tan\delta \le -1$):**  
   The Sun never rises above the horizon. The function saturates at:
   $$D = 0\text{ hours}$$

## 5. The Southern Hemisphere: Exact Anti-Symmetric Phase Reflection

What happens in the Southern Hemisphere?

In the Southern Hemisphere, the latitude is negative: $\phi_S = -\phi_N < 0$. Because the tangent function is an odd function ($\tan(-\phi) = -\tan\phi$), substituting into Equation (8) yields:

$$\cos\omega_{0, \text{South}} = -\tan(-\phi_N)\tan\delta = +\tan\phi_N \tan\delta = -\cos\omega_{0, \text{North}} \tag{11}$$

Using the fundamental inverse trigonometric identity:

$$\arccos(-x) = \pi - \arccos(x) \tag{12}$$

The daylight duration in the Southern Hemisphere is:

$$D(-\phi, \delta) = \frac{24}{\pi} \left[ \pi - \arccos(-\tan\phi \tan\delta) \right] = 24 - D(\phi, \delta) \tag{13}$$

> **The Fundamental Conservation Theorem of Planetary Daylight:**  
> At any instant on Earth, the daylight duration at latitude $+\phi$ in the Northern Hemisphere and latitude $-\phi$ in the Southern Hemisphere sums to **identically 24 hours**:
> $$D(\phi, \delta) + D(-\phi, \delta) \equiv 24\text{ hours} \tag{14}$$

### Physical Implications:
* **Solstitial Inversion**: On June 21 ($\delta = +23.44^\circ$), Philadelphia ($40^\circ\text{N}$) enjoys $14.8\text{ hours}$ of daylight. Simultaneously, at $40^\circ\text{S}$ (near Melbourne, Australia or Wellington, New Zealand), the day length is:
  $$D = 24 - 14.8 = 9.2\text{ hours}$$
* **Seasonal Anti-Phase**: June 21 is the **Summer Solstice** (longest day) in the North, but the **Winter Solstice** (shortest day) in the South! December 21 is the shortest day in the North, but the longest day in the South.
* **The Invariant Equator**: At the Equator ($\phi = 0^\circ$), $\tan(0^\circ) = 0$, so $\cos\omega_0 = 0 \implies \omega_0 = \pi/2$. Daylight is **identically 12 hours every day of the year**, completely independent of the season!

## 6. Evaluating the Calculus Sine Approximation: Taylor Series Expansion

Why did James Stewart's simple sine function $L(t) = 12 + A \sin\left(\frac{2\pi}{365}(t - 80)\right)$ work so effectively for Philadelphia?

Let us expand the exact physical formula (Equation 10) in a Maclaurin series around the equinox, where $u = -\tan\phi \tan\delta \approx 0$. 

The Taylor expansion of the inverse cosine is:

$$\arccos(-x) = \frac{\pi}{2} + x + \frac{x^3}{6} + \mathcal{O}(x^5) \tag{15}$$

Substituting $x = \tan\phi \tan\delta$ into Equation (10):

$$D(\phi, \delta) = \frac{24}{\pi} \left[ \frac{\pi}{2} + \tan\phi \tan\delta + \frac{1}{6}(\tan\phi \tan\delta)^3 + \dots \right]$$

$$D(\phi, \delta) \approx 12 + \frac{24}{\pi} \tan\phi \tan\delta \tag{16}$$

For small angles, $\tan\delta \approx \sin\delta \approx \sin\epsilon \sin\left(\frac{2\pi}{365}(N - 80)\right)$. Substituting this into Equation (16):

$$D(\phi, N) \approx 12 + \left[ \frac{24}{\pi} \sin\epsilon \tan\phi \right] \sin\left( \frac{2\pi}{365}(N - 80) \right) \tag{17}$$

Equation (17) is **identically Stewart's calculus sine model**! The amplitude $A(\phi)$ is not an arbitrary fitting parameter; it is determined by the astrophysics of axial tilt:

$$A(\phi) = \frac{24}{\pi} \sin(23.44^\circ) \tan\phi \approx \frac{24}{\pi}(0.3978)\tan\phi \approx 3.039 \tan\phi \tag{18}$$

For Philadelphia ($\phi = 40^\circ\text{N}$, $\tan(40^\circ) \approx 0.8391$):

$$A(40^\circ) \approx 3.039 \times 0.8391 \approx 2.55\text{ hours}$$

With the addition of the cubic term in the Taylor expansion, the solstitial amplitude rises to **$2.8\text{ hours}$**, matching Stewart's empirical coefficient!

### Where Does the Sine Model Break Down?
1. **Polar Circles ($\phi \ge 66.56^\circ$)**: Near the Arctic Circle, $\tan\phi \tan\epsilon \ge 1$. The argument of $\arccos$ exceeds unity, producing hard horizontal plateaus at $24\text{ hours}$ and $0\text{ hours}$ that a smooth sine wave cannot capture.
2. **Atmospheric Refraction**: Real-world sunrise occurs before the center of the Sun crosses the geometric horizon because Earth's atmosphere acts as a lens, bending light upward by $\approx 34\text{ arcminutes}$. Combined with the Sun's apparent angular semi-diameter ($\approx 16\text{ arcminutes}$), sunrise is defined when the **upper limb** appears, corresponding to true solar altitude $\alpha_0 = -50' = -0.833^\circ$.
   * As a result, daylight duration exceeds darkness: even at the Equator, the average day length is **$12\text{ hours } 8\text{ minutes}$**, not $12.0\text{ hours}$.

### 7.1 Multi-Latitude Daylight Curves (Northern vs. Southern Hemispheres)

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def solar_declination(day_of_year):
    """Solar declination angle delta in radians."""
    epsilon = np.radians(23.44)
    return np.arcsin(np.sin(epsilon) * np.sin(2 * np.pi * (day_of_year - 80) / 365.25))

def compute_daylight(lat_deg, days):
    """Exact daylight hours calculated via the Sunrise Equation."""
    phi = np.radians(lat_deg)
    delta = solar_declination(days)
    arg = -np.tan(phi) * np.tan(delta)
    
    daylight = np.zeros_like(days, dtype=float)
    polar_day = arg <= -1.0
    polar_night = arg >= 1.0
    normal = (~polar_day) & (~polar_night)
    
    daylight[polar_day] = 24.0
    daylight[polar_night] = 0.0
    daylight[normal] = (24.0 / np.pi) * np.arccos(arg[normal])
    return daylight

days = np.linspace(1, 365, 365)

fig, (ax1, ax2) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(12, 5.2), dpi=180)

# Panel 1: Northern and Southern Hemispheres

for lat, c in zip(lats_north, colors_n):
    d_val = compute_daylight(lat, days)
    lbl = f"{lat}°N" if lat > 0 else "Equator (0°)"
    ax1.plot(days, d_val, label=lbl, color=c, lw=2.0)

lats_south = [20, 40, 60]
colors_s = ['#3b82f6', '#10b981', '#f59e0b']
for lat, c in zip(lats_south, colors_s):
    d_val = compute_daylight(-lat, days)

## 8. Summary of Physical Principles

1. **Latitude ($\phi$)**: The angle measured between the terrestrial equatorial plane and the local observer's zenith.
2. **Axial Tilt ($\epsilon = 23.44^\circ$)**: Earth's gyroscopic spin axis maintains a fixed orientation in space while orbiting the Sun, causing the subsolar point (declination $\delta$) to oscillate seasonally between $-23.44^\circ$ and $+23.44^\circ$.
3. **The Equinox Pivot**: When $\delta = 0^\circ$, the day-night terminator plane passes directly through both rotational poles, bisecting all latitude circles equally and providing **exactly 12 hours of daylight at every point on Earth**.
4. **The Exact Law**: Daylight duration is governed by the Sunrise Equation:
   $$D = \frac{24}{\pi} \arccos(-\tan\phi \tan\delta)$$
5. **Southern Hemisphere Inversion**: Because $\tan(-\phi) = -\tan\phi$, daylight hours in the Southern Hemisphere are exact anti-phase reflections satisfying:
   $$D(\phi) + D(-\phi) \equiv 24\text{ hours}$$
   When the Northern Hemisphere experiences summer, the Southern Hemisphere experiences winter.
6. **Calculus Model Context**: James Stewart's sine curve $L(t) = 12 + A \sin\left(\frac{2\pi}{365}(t - 80)\right)$ is the first-order Taylor expansion of the exact spherical law. It succeeds brilliantly at temperate mid-latitudes, but gives way to hard saturation at 0h and 24h across polar circles.
