The Ultimate Open University Curriculum

A curated self-study guide featuring the best free lectures from Yale and Stanford, spanning Philosophy, Physics, and Biology.

The democratization of education has made it possible to receive an Ivy League education from the comfort of your own home, entirely for free. Through initiatives like Open Yale Courses and Stanford’s YouTube archives, some of the greatest professors of our generation have made their entire semester-long lectures publicly available.

Below is a curated self-study curriculum combining the best of these courses, organized by discipline. Whether you are interested in the physics of black holes, the biological basis of human behavior, or the ethical dilemmas of ancient philosophers, this guide provides the definitive syllabus for the autodidact.

1. Philosophy and Ethics

The study of philosophy is not just about abstract logic; it is about understanding how to live. This track blends classical political philosophy with modern cognitive science and the ultimate existential question: death.

  • PHIL 181: Philosophy and the Science of Human Nature (Prof. Tamar Gendler - Yale): This brilliant course bridges the gap between ancient philosophical texts (like Plato and Aristotle) and modern cognitive psychology. A standout lecture is her breakdown of Plato’s Ring of Gyges (see the specific lecture here), which asks the fundamental ethical question: If you had a ring of invisibility and could get away with any crime, would you still act justly? Are we moral only because we fear the consequences of getting caught?
  • PHIL 176: Death (Prof. Shelly Kagan - Yale): One of Yale’s most famous courses. Prof. Kagan famously sits cross-legged on his desk and analytically deconstructs the nature of death. It covers arguments for the existence of the soul, what it means to die, the badness of death, immortality, and the ethics of suicide.
  • PLSC 114: Introduction to Political Philosophy (Prof. Steven Smith - Yale): An exploration of the major texts of Western political thought. It traces the evolution of political philosophy from Socrates and Aristotle through Machiavelli, Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau, and Tocqueville, asking what makes a government legitimate and what makes a citizen “good.”

2. Biology and Psychology

Understanding the human animal requires looking at both the mind (psychology) and the biological mechanics (neuroscience and genetics) that govern it.

  • Human Behavioral Biology (Prof. Robert Sapolsky - Stanford): Arguably one of the greatest lecture series on the internet. Sapolsky rigorously destroys the “nature vs. nurture” false dichotomy, explaining human behavior through the lenses of evolutionary biology, molecular genetics, endocrinology, and neurology. It is a masterpiece in scientific communication.
  • PSYC 110: Introduction to Psychology (Prof. Paul Bloom - Yale): A comprehensive overview of the scientific study of the mind and behavior, covering perception, memory, learning, language, emotions, and social interactions, as well as Freud and Skinner’s legacies.
  • MCDB 150: Global Problems of Population Growth (Prof. Robert Wyman - Yale): A macro-level look at biology, focusing on the environmental, demographic, and biological consequences of human population expansion.

3. Physics and Astronomy

For those mathematically inclined, this track builds from the ground up, starting with classical mechanics and extending out into the cosmos.

  • PHYS 200: Fundamentals of Physics I (Prof. Ramamurti Shankar - Yale): A legendary introduction to classical mechanics, including Newton’s laws, oscillations, waves, and thermodynamics. Prof. Shankar’s humor and clarity make this notoriously difficult subject approachable.
  • PHYS 201: Fundamentals of Physics II (Prof. Ramamurti Shankar - Yale): The continuation of PHYS 200, diving into electromagnetism, optics, and an introduction to quantum mechanics and special relativity.
  • ASTR 160: Frontiers and Controversies in Astrophysics (Prof. Charles Bailyn - Yale): Instead of a dry textbook overview, this course focuses on three active areas of astrophysical research: extra-solar planets, black holes, and the accelerating expansion of the universe (dark energy).

4. Religious Studies in Historical Context

Rather than studying these texts from a purely theological or devotional perspective, these courses analyze the Bible as profound historical and literary documents that shaped Western civilization.

  • RLST 145: Introduction to the Old Testament (Hebrew Bible) (Prof. Christine Hayes - Yale): An academic study of the Hebrew Bible, examining its texts against the backdrop of the ancient Near Eastern world.
  • RLST 152: Introduction to the New Testament History and Literature (Prof. Dale B. Martin - Yale): An exploration of early Christianity, analyzing the New Testament writings within their first-century Greco-Roman and Jewish contexts.

A Note on Other Tools: Building a self-study habit requires a good ecosystem. You can organize your coursework using tools like Google Keep for quick notes, Google Tasks for assignment deadlines, and Goodreads for tracking course reading lists. If you need a mental break from heavy academic study, music theory sites like PianoChord.org or All-Guitar-Chords provide excellent creative outlets!
