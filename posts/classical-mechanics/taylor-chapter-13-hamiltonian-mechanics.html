Overview & Syllabus Context

According to the PHY C54 Syllabus, Hamiltonian Mechanics spans Week 04 (Ch 13: §§1–4) and Week 05 (Ch 13: §§5–7), concluding before Test #1.

Three Formulations of One Mechanics

Newton (1687) Lagrange (1788) Hamilton (1834)
Primary objects forces, accelerations \(\mathcal L = T-U\) \(\mathcal H = \sum p_i\dot q_i - \mathcal L\)
State variables \((\mathbf r, \dot{\mathbf r})\) \((q, \dot q)\) \((q, p)\)
Space physical/\(3N\)-dim configuration \(Q\) / state space phase space \(T^*Q\)
Equations \(\mathbf F = \dot{\mathbf p}\) \(n\) second-order \(2n\) first-order
Best for Cartesian, elementary constraints, any coordinates conserved quantities, chaos, QM
Weakness constraint forces dissipative systems non-conservative systems

All three are equivalent — either can be derived from the other — but each makes a different class of problems transparent. Hamilton’s version replaces the generalized velocities \(\dot q_i\) by the generalized momenta \(p_i\), and it is this change of variables (a Legendre transformation) that opens the door to phase-space geometry, canonical transformations, statistical mechanics, and quantum mechanics.

Core Topics

  1. The Basic Variables (§13.1): configuration space, state space, and phase space; the generalized (canonical) momentum \(p_i = \partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot q_i\).
  2. Hamilton’s Equations (§§13.2–13.3): \[ \dot q_i = \frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial p_i}, \qquad \dot p_i = -\frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial q_i} \]
  3. Ignorable Coordinates (§13.4): why the Hamiltonian reduces a problem by one degree of freedom more cleanly than the Lagrangian.
  4. Phase-Space Orbits (§13.6): trajectories that never cross, and the ellipse/parabola pictures of the oscillator and the falling body.
  5. Liouville’s Theorem (§13.7): phase-space volume is incompressible under Hamiltonian flow.

13.1 The Basic Variables

Review of the Lagrangian picture

For most systems of interest the Lagrangian is the difference of kinetic and potential energy, \[ \mathcal L = T - U, \tag{13.1}\] and is a function of the \(n\) generalized coordinates \(q_1,\dots,q_n\), their \(n\) generalized velocities \(\dot q_1,\dots,\dot q_n\), and possibly the time: \[ \mathcal L = \mathcal L(q_1,\dots,q_n,\dot q_1,\dots,\dot q_n,t).\]

  • The \(n\) coordinates \((q_1,\dots,q_n)\) define a point in configuration space and specify a configuration of the system.
  • The \(2n\) coordinates \((q_1,\dots,q_n,\dot q_1,\dots,\dot q_n)\) define a point in state space and specify an initial condition that determines a unique orbit.
  • The motion is governed by the \(n\) second-order Lagrange equations \[ \frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial q_i} = \frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial \dot q_i}, \qquad [i=1,\dots,n]. \tag{13.2}\]

The generalized momentum

For each coordinate define the generalized momentum (also called the canonical or conjugate momentum) \[ p_i = \frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial \dot q_i}. \tag{13.3}\] If the \(q_i\) are Cartesian, the \(p_i\) are the ordinary momentum components. In general \(p_i\) is not a momentum in the Newtonian sense, but plays an exactly analogous role. For a charged particle in a magnetic field, for instance, \(p_i = m\dot x_i + qA_i\) — the canonical momentum, not \(m\dot x_i\).

The Hamiltonian

The central object of the new formulation is the Hamiltonian function \[ \boxed{\ \mathcal H = \sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i \dot q_i - \mathcal L\ } \tag{13.4}\] which replaces \(\mathcal L\). We met it briefly in §7.8, where we proved that for natural coordinates (the relation of the \(q\)’s to the underlying Cartesians is time-independent) it is just the total energy, \[ \mathcal H = T + U \qquad \text{(natural coordinates)}.\]

Two important differences from the Lagrangian

  1. Physical significance. Unlike \(\mathcal L\), the Hamiltonian \(\mathcal H\) is (for most systems) the total energy and is frequently conserved.
  2. Coordinates. The Lagrangian labels the state by \[ (q_1,\dots,q_n,\dot q_1,\dots,\dot q_n) \qquad \text{[Lagrange]} \tag{13.5}\] whereas Hamilton uses the positions together with the momenta: \[ (q_1,\dots,q_n,p_1,\dots,p_n) \qquad \text{[Hamilton]}. \tag{13.6}\]

Just as (13.5) defines a point in \(2n\)-dimensional state space, (13.6) defines a point in the \(2n\)-dimensional phase space. The succinct way to state the advantage of the Hamiltonian formalism is that phase space has geometric properties that make it more convenient than state space.

Terminology. Many authors use “state space” and “phase space” interchangeably. Taylor reserves state space for positions + generalized velocities and phase space for positions + generalized momenta.

Throughout the chapter we assume all forces are conservative (or derivable from a potential), so no friction. This still covers an enormous range of problems, especially in astrophysics and at the atomic/molecular level.

13.2 Hamilton’s Equations for One-Dimensional Systems

To avoid index clutter, begin with one degree of freedom and a single natural coordinate \(q\). For a conservative system \[ \mathcal L = \mathcal L(q,\dot q) = T(q,\dot q) - U(q). \tag{13.7}\] The kinetic energy may depend on \(q\), but only quadratically on \(\dot q\). We proved in §7.8 that the general form is \[ \mathcal L(q,\dot q) = A(q)\,\dot q^2 - U(q), \tag{13.10}\] where \(A(q)\) need not be constant. For example:

  • Simple pendulum (mass \(m\), length \(L\), \(q=\phi\)): \[ \mathcal L = \tfrac12 mL^2\dot\phi^2 - mgL(1-\cos\phi). \tag{13.8}\]
  • Bead on a curved wire \(y=f(x)\): \[ \mathcal L = \tfrac12 m[1+f'(x)^2]\dot x^2 - mg\,f(x). \tag{13.9}\]

Deriving Hamilton’s equations

The Hamiltonian is defined by (13.4), which in one dimension reads \[ \mathcal H = p\dot q - \mathcal L. \tag{13.11}\]

From (13.10), \[ p = \frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial \dot q} = 2A(q)\dot q \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad p\dot q = 2T,\] so \[ \mathcal H = p\dot q - \mathcal L = 2T - (T - U) = T + U = E. \tag{13.12}\] The Hamiltonian is the total energy.

The subtle step. \(\mathcal L\) is a function of \(q\) and \(\dot q\); (13.12) gives \(p\) in terms of \(q\) and \(\dot q\). We must solve for \(\dot q\) in terms of \(q\) and \(p\), \[ \dot q = \frac{p}{2A(q)} = \dot q(q,p), \tag{13.13}\] and substitute it everywhere, so that \[ \mathcal H(q,p) = p\,\dot q(q,p) - \mathcal L\big(q,\dot q(q,p)\big). \tag{13.14}\]

Now differentiate (13.14) with respect to \(q\), using the chain rule: \[ \frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial q} = p\frac{\partial \dot q}{\partial q} - \frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial q} - \frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial \dot q}\frac{\partial \dot q}{\partial q}.\] The first and third terms cancel because \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot q = p\), leaving \[ \frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial q} = -\frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial q} = -\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial \dot q} = -\dot p, \tag{13.15}\] where the second equality is the Lagrange equation (13.2). Differentiating (13.14) with respect to \(p\) gives \[ \frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial p} = \dot q + p\frac{\partial \dot q}{\partial p} - \frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial \dot q}\frac{\partial \dot q}{\partial p} = \dot q. \tag{13.16}\]

Collecting the results:

Hamilton’s equations (one dimension). \[ \boxed{\ \dot q = \frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial p}, \qquad \dot p = -\frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial q}\ } \tag{13.17}\]

In the Lagrangian formalism a 1-D system obeys one second-order equation for \(q\); in the Hamiltonian formalism it obeys two first-order equations, one for \(q\) and one for \(p\).

The Legendre transformation. The change from \(\mathcal L\) to \(\mathcal H\) as the primary object is an example of a Legendre transformation, central in thermodynamics: the change from internal energy \(U\) to enthalpy \(H = U + pV\) is exactly analogous to Hamilton’s change from \(\mathcal L\) to \(\mathcal H\).

Example 13.1 — A Bead on a Straight Wire

A bead (mass \(m\)) slides on a frictionless straight wire along the \(x\) axis under a potential \(U(x)\). With \(q=x\), \[ \mathcal L = \tfrac12 m\dot x^2 - U(x).\] Lagrange’s equation reproduces Newton: \[ -\frac{dU}{dx} = m\ddot x. \tag{13.18}\]

For Hamilton, first find \[ p = \frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial \dot x} = m\dot x \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \dot x = \frac{p}{m},\] then substitute: \[ \mathcal H = \frac{p^2}{2m} + U(x).\] Hamilton’s equations are \[ \dot x = \frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial p} = \frac{p}{m}, \qquad \dot p = -\frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial x} = -\frac{dU}{dx}.\] The first is the definition of momentum; substituting it into the second returns \(m\ddot x = -dU/dx\). Newton, Lagrange, and Hamilton agree — in this trivial case none has an advantage.

Example 13.2 — Atwood’s Machine

Two masses \(m_1, m_2\) hang over a massless frictionless pulley; use the downward distance \(x\) of \(m_1\) as the coordinate. From Example 7.3, \[ T = \tfrac12(m_1+m_2)\dot x^2, \qquad U = -(m_1-m_2)gx. \tag{13.19}\] Then \[ \mathcal H = T + U = \frac{p^2}{2(m_1+m_2)} - (m_1-m_2)gx, \qquad p = (m_1+m_2)\dot x.\] Hamilton’s equations: \[ \dot x = \frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial p} = \frac{p}{m_1+m_2}, \qquad \dot p = -\frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial x} = (m_1-m_2)g.\] Combining them gives the familiar acceleration \[ \ddot x = \frac{m_1-m_2}{m_1+m_2}\,g.\]

The general procedure (one dimension)

The examples reveal the standard rhythm: write the Hamiltonian, then compute \(p\), solve that relation for the velocity, and rewrite \(\mathcal H\) as a function of \((q,p)\). Only then “turn the handle” of Hamilton’s equations. There is no guarantee the resulting equations are easy — but there is an almost infallible recipe for finding them.

13.3 Hamilton’s Equations in Several Dimensions

Vector notation

Group the coordinates, velocities, and momenta into vectors \[ \mathbf q = (q_1,\dots,q_n), \qquad \dot{\mathbf q} = (\dot q_1,\dots,\dot q_n), \qquad \mathbf p = (p_1,\dots,p_n).\] These are \(n\)-dimensional vectors in the relevant space — not necessarily the \(3\)-vectors of ordinary space.

Assumptions

The derivation assumes exactly what Chapter 7 assumed:

  • constraints are holonomic (number of degrees of freedom = number of generalized coordinates);
  • the non-constraint forces come from a potential \(U\) (allowed to depend on \(t\));
  • the coordinate relations may depend on time — the generalized coordinates need not be natural.

The starting point is that there is a Lagrangian \(\mathcal L(\mathbf q,\dot{\mathbf q},t)\) obeying the \(n\) Lagrange equations (13.21). Define the Hamiltonian exactly as before, \[ \mathcal H = \sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i\dot q_i - \mathcal L, \tag{13.22}\] with \[ p_i = \frac{\partial \mathcal L(q,\dot q,t)}{\partial \dot q_i}, \qquad [i=1,\dots,n]. \tag{13.23}\]

Eliminating the velocities

View (13.23) as \(n\) simultaneous equations for the \(n\) velocities, and in principle solve them: \[ \dot q_i = \dot q_i(\mathbf q,\mathbf p,t), \qquad\text{or}\qquad \dot{\mathbf q} = \dot{\mathbf q}(\mathbf q,\mathbf p,t).\] Substituting into (13.22), \[ \mathcal H(\mathbf q,\mathbf p,t) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i\,\dot q_i(\mathbf q,\mathbf p,t) - \mathcal L\big(\mathbf q,\dot{\mathbf q}(\mathbf q,\mathbf p,t),t\big). \tag{13.24}\]

Reasoning as in the one-dimensional case yields the multidimensional equations:

Hamilton’s equations (\(n\) dimensions). \[ \boxed{\ \dot q_i = \frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial p_i}, \qquad \dot p_i = -\frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial q_i}, \qquad [i=1,\dots,n]\ } \tag{13.25}\]

For a system with \(n\) degrees of freedom the Hamiltonian approach gives \(2n\) first-order equations instead of the Lagrangian \(n\) second-order equations.

The time derivative of \(\mathcal H\): total vs. partial

There is one more derivative to understand. Because \(\mathcal H(\mathbf q,\mathbf p,t)\) depends on \(t\) both through the coordinates and possibly explicitly, the chain rule gives \(2n+1\) terms: \[ \frac{d\mathcal H}{dt} = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial q_i}\dot q_i + \frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial p_i}\dot p_i\right] + \frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial t}. \tag{13.26}\]

  • The left side, \(d\mathcal H/dt\) (total derivative), is the actual rate of change as the motion proceeds.
  • The term \(\partial\mathcal H/\partial t\) (partial derivative) is the rate of change holding all \(q_i, p_i\) fixed.

By Hamilton’s equations (13.25), every bracket in the sum is \[ \frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial q_i}\frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial p_i} + \frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial p_i}\left(-\frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial q_i}\right) = 0,\] so each pair cancels and \[ \boxed{\ \frac{d\mathcal H}{dt} = \frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial t}\ }\] That is, \(\mathcal H\) varies with time only to the extent that it is explicitly time-dependent. In particular, if \(\mathcal H\) has no explicit \(t\)-dependence, it is conserved — the Hamiltonian version of the result of §7.8. (One can also show \(\partial\mathcal H/\partial t = -\partial\mathcal L/\partial t\); see Problem 13.16.)

For the remainder of the chapter we restrict to natural coordinates and no explicit time dependence, so that \(\mathcal H = T+U\) and the total energy is conserved.

Example 13.3 — Central Force in Polar Coordinates

For a particle in a conservative central potential \(U(r)\), use polar coordinates \((r,\phi)\) in the plane of motion. The kinetic energy is \[ T = \tfrac12 m(\dot r^2 + r^2\dot\phi^2). \tag{13.27}\] The coordinates \((r,\phi)\) are natural, so \(\mathcal H = T+U\) expressed in terms of the momenta \[ p_r = \frac{\partial T}{\partial \dot r} = m\dot r, \qquad p_\phi = \frac{\partial T}{\partial \dot\phi} = mr^2\dot\phi. \tag{13.28}\] Here \(p_r\) is the radial component of ordinary momentum, while \(p_\phi\) is the angular momentum. Solving for the velocities, \[ \dot r = \frac{p_r}{m}, \qquad \dot\phi = \frac{p_\phi}{mr^2},\] and substituting: \[ \mathcal H = \frac{1}{2m}\left(p_r^2 + \frac{p_\phi^2}{r^2}\right) + U(r). \tag{13.29}\]

The two radial equations are \[ \dot r = \frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial p_r} = \frac{p_r}{m}, \qquad \dot p_r = -\frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial r} = \frac{p_\phi^2}{mr^3} - \frac{dU}{dr}. \tag{13.30}\] Substituting the first into the second gives \(m\ddot r = -dU/dr + p_\phi^2/(mr^3)\): the actual radial force plus the centrifugal force. The two angular equations are \[ \dot\phi = \frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial p_\phi} = \frac{p_\phi}{mr^2}, \qquad \dot p_\phi = -\frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial \phi} = 0, \tag{13.31}\] the second of which states angular momentum conservation. Once again Hamilton reproduces the known physics.

The six-step recipe

For any conservative system:

  1. Choose generalized coordinates \(q_1,\dots,q_n\).
  2. Write down \(T\) and \(U\) in terms of the \(q\)’s and \(\dot q\)’s.
  3. Find the generalized momenta \(p_i\). (For conservative systems with \(U\) independent of the velocities, \(p_i = \partial T/\partial \dot q_i\); in general use \(p_i = \partial \mathcal L/\partial \dot q_i\).)
  4. Solve for the \(\dot q\)’s in terms of the \(p\)’s and \(q\)’s.
  5. Write \(\mathcal H\) as a function of the \(q\)’s and \(p\)’s. [For natural coordinates \(\mathcal H = T+U\); when in doubt use \(\mathcal H = \sum p_i\dot q_i - \mathcal L\).]
  6. Write down Hamilton’s equations (13.25).

Compared with the Lagrangian recipe, steps (3) and (4) are extra — an undeniably small disadvantage of Hamilton’s method. The payoff comes later: conserved quantities, transformations, and geometry.

Example 13.4 — A Mass on a Cone

A mass \(m\) is constrained to the frictionless vertical cone \(\rho = cz\) (\(z>0\)) in a uniform downward gravity \(g\); use \(z\) and \(\phi\). With \(\rho = cz\), \[ T = \tfrac12 m\big[(c^2+1)\dot z^2 + c^2z^2\dot\phi^2\big], \qquad U = mgz.\] The momenta are \[ p_z = m(c^2+1)\dot z, \qquad p_\phi = mc^2z^2\dot\phi. \tag{13.32}\] Hence \[ \mathcal H = T+U = \frac{p_z^2}{2m(c^2+1)} + \frac{p_\phi^2}{2mc^2z^2} + mgz. \tag{13.33}\]

Hamilton’s equations: \[ \dot z = \frac{p_z}{m(c^2+1)}, \qquad \dot p_z = \frac{p_\phi^2}{mc^2z^3} - mg, \tag{13.34}\] \[ \dot\phi = \frac{p_\phi}{mc^2z^2}, \qquad \dot p_\phi = 0. \tag{13.35}\]

The last equation says \(p_\phi\), the \(z\)-component of angular momentum, is constant. Because \(\mathcal H = E\) is conserved and the \(\phi\)-term diverges as \(z\to 0\) while \(mgz\) diverges as \(z\to\infty\), the height \(z\) is trapped between a minimum \(z_{\min}>0\) and a maximum \(z_{\max}\). The mass spirals up and down the cone, never falling to the bottom (unless \(p_\phi = 0\), in which case it slides radially down). For a solution of fixed height \(z\), we need \(\dot p_z = 0\), i.e. \[ p_\phi = \pm\sqrt{m^2c^2gz^3}, \tag{13.36}\] so a horizontal circular orbit exists at any chosen height with the right angular momentum.

13.4 Ignorable Coordinates

In Chapter 7 we saw that if \(\mathcal L\) is independent of a coordinate \(q_i\), then the conjugate momentum is constant (because \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial q_i = (d/dt)\,\partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot q_i = \dot p_i\)). Such a coordinate is ignorable.

Exactly the same holds in the Hamiltonian picture: if \(\mathcal H\) is independent of \(q_i\), then \[ \dot p_i = -\frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial q_i} = 0 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad p_i = \text{const}.\] The two statements are the same because \[ \frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial q_i} = -\frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial q_i} \tag{13.22}\] (Problem 13.22). So \(\mathcal L\) is independent of \(q_i\) iff \(\mathcal H\) is.

Why Hamilton handles it more cleanly

Suppose a two-degree-of-freedom system has \(q_2\) ignorable, so \[ \mathcal H = \mathcal H(q_1,p_1,p_2). \tag{13.37}\] Since \(p_2 = k\) is a constant fixed by the initial conditions, substitution gives \[ \mathcal H = \mathcal H(q_1,p_1,k),\] a function of only two variables — and the motion is reduced to an effective one-dimensional problem. With several ignorable coordinates the reduction is even greater: a system with \(n\) degrees of freedom reduces to one with \(n-1\) (or fewer).

The Lagrangian does not simplify as cleanly. With \(q_2\) ignorable we would have \[ \mathcal L = \mathcal L(q_1,\dot q_1,\dot q_2),\] and although \(p_2\) is constant, \(\dot q_2\) need not be, so the Lagrangian keeps its dependence on the “removed” degree of freedom. For the central-force problem, for example, the Lagrangian is \(\mathcal L(r,\dot r,\dot\phi)\); even though \(\phi\) is ignorable, \(\dot\phi\) varies, and the problem does not automatically become one-dimensional. The Hamiltonian’s ability to freeze an ignorable momentum is one of its genuine structural advantages.

13.5 Lagrange’s Equations vs. Hamilton’s Equations

For \(n\) degrees of freedom:

  • Lagrange: \(n\) second-order equations for \(q_1,\dots,q_n\).
  • Hamilton: \(2n\) first-order equations for \(q_1,\dots,q_n,p_1,\dots,p_n\).

Converting \(n\) second-order equations into \(2n\) first-order equations is not by itself a virtue — any second-order system can be recast this way. For one degree of freedom, \[ f(\ddot q,\dot q,q) = 0 \tag{13.38}\] with \(s = \dot q\) becomes the pair \[ \dot q = s, \qquad f(\dot s, s, q) = 0. \tag{13.39, 13.40}\]

The real improvement: the form

The power of Hamilton’s version is the specific shape of the equations. Write the position equations as \[ \dot q_i = \frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial p_i} = f_i(\mathbf q,\mathbf p), \qquad \dot{\mathbf q} = \mathbf f(\mathbf q,\mathbf p), \tag{13.41, 13.42}\] the momentum equations as \[ \dot p_i = -\frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial q_i} = g_i(\mathbf q,\mathbf p), \qquad \dot{\mathbf p} = \mathbf g(\mathbf q,\mathbf p), \tag{13.43}\] and combine everything into the phase-space vector \[ \mathbf z = (\mathbf q,\mathbf p) = (q_1,\dots,q_n,p_1,\dots,p_n). \tag{13.44}\] Then Hamilton’s equations collapse into a single first-order equation: \[ \boxed{\ \dot{\mathbf z} = \mathbf h(\mathbf z)\ } \tag{13.45}\] with \(\mathbf h\) the vector of the \(2n\) functions \(f_i,g_i\). This “derivative-of-a-vector = function-of-the-vector” form is the standard form studied throughout the theory of differential equations. When \(\mathcal H\) has no explicit time dependence the system is called autonomous. That Hamilton’s equations are automatically of this form — unlike Lagrange’s — is a substantial conceptual advantage.

Flexibility: configuration space and phase space

The Lagrangian is famous for its coordinate freedom: replacing \(\mathbf q\) by any new set \(\mathbf Q = \mathbf Q(\mathbf q)\) leaves Lagrange’s equations valid. The Hamiltonian shares this invariance, but goes much further. Because \(\mathbf q\) and \(\mathbf p\) can be intermingled, one may consider transformations \[ \mathbf Q = \mathbf Q(\mathbf q,\mathbf p), \qquad \mathbf P = \mathbf P(\mathbf q,\mathbf p). \tag{13.47}\] If these satisfy certain conditions they form a canonical transformation, and Hamilton’s equations are invariant under them. This has no Lagrangian counterpart: since \(\dot q\) is defined as the time derivative of \(q\), there is no way to mix the \(q\)’s with the \(\dot q\)’s in the state-space formulation.

Canonical transformations are the gateway to the advanced theory (Hamilton–Jacobi theory, action-angle variables, perturbation theory). Here it is enough to know they exist and why they matter.

13.6 Phase-Space Orbits

A point \(\mathbf z_0\) in phase space is an initial condition. Hamilton’s equations (13.45) define a unique phase-space orbit through it. Because phase space has \(2n\) dimensions, visualization is easy only when \(n=1\); for a single particle in 3-D, \(n=3\) and the phase space is six-dimensional. Techniques such as the Poincaré section (§12.8) project the motion into lower-dimensional slices.

Orbits never cross

Suppose two orbits passed through the same phase point \(\mathbf z_0\) (Figure 13.4). Hamilton’s equations assign to each point a single velocity, hence a single orbit through \(\mathbf z_0\). Therefore the two orbits must coincide. In fact this forbids different orbits from passing through the same point even at different times (for a time-independent Hamiltonian) — no orbit can ever revisit a point another orbit has visited. This “non-crossing” property severely constrains the geometry of phase-space flow and is central to the analysis of chaotic Hamiltonian systems.

Example 13.5 — The Harmonic Oscillator

For mass \(m\), force constant \(k\), natural frequency \(\omega = \sqrt{k/m}\): \[ \mathcal H = T+U = \frac{p^2}{2m} + \tfrac12 m\omega^2 x^2. \tag{13.48}\] Hamilton’s equations are \[ \dot x = \frac{p}{m}, \qquad \dot p = -m\omega^2 x.\] Eliminating \(p\) gives \(\ddot x = -\omega^2 x\), with solution \[ x = A\cos(\omega t - \delta), \qquad p = m\dot x = -m\omega A\sin(\omega t - \delta). \tag{13.49}\] This is the parametric equation of an ellipse traced clockwise in the \((x,p)\) plane. Conservation of energy makes the shape manifest: setting \(\mathcal H = \tfrac12 m\omega^2 A^2\), \[ \frac{p^2}{2m} + \tfrac12 m\omega^2 x^2 = \tfrac12 m\omega^2 A^2 \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \frac{x^2}{A^2} + \frac{p^2}{(m\omega A)^2} = 1. \tag{13.50}\] The semiaxes are \(A\) (in \(x\)) and \(m\omega A\) (in \(p\)). Different energies give concentric ellipses that never intersect. Following the solid orbit from rest at \(x=A,p=0\): the restoring force accelerates \(m\) back toward \(x=0\), so \(x\) decreases while \(p\) becomes negative, reaching its most negative value \(-m\omega A\) at \(x=0\); the oscillator overshoots, and by \(x=-A\) the momentum is again zero.

Phase space here is closely related to the \((x,\dot x)\) state-space picture of §12.7 — for 1-D they differ only by the vertical stretch \(p = m\dot x\). In \(2n\) dimensions, however, the two spaces can be very different, and it is phase space that carries the elegant structure.

Example 13.6 — A Falling Body

For a mass in a vertical line under gravity, with \(x\) measured downward, \[ T = \tfrac12 m\dot x^2, \qquad U = -mgx, \qquad \mathcal H = \frac{p^2}{2m} - mgx. \tag{13.51}\] Conservation of energy (\(\mathcal H = \text{const}\)) already tells us the orbits are parabolas \(x = kp^2 + \text{const}\), symmetric about the \(x\) axis. Solving the equations of motion, \[ \dot x = \frac{p}{m}, \qquad \dot p = mg \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad p = p_0 + mgt, \qquad x = x_0 + \frac{p_0}{m}t + \tfrac12 gt^2.\] Four initial conditions \((a)\)–\((d)\) in the text produce four non-crossing parabolas (Figure 13.6). The striking feature: an initial rectangle of states \(A_0B_0C_0D_0\) evolves into a parallelogram \(ABCD\) of equal area. Shape changes; area does not. This is a concrete instance of Liouville’s theorem.

13.7 Liouville’s Theorem

(This section uses the divergence operator and the divergence theorem of vector calculus.)

The fluid picture

In statistical mechanics one tracks an enormous number of identical systems. Each system is a dot in phase space; a large swarm of dots behaves like a fluid of density \(\rho\) (dots per unit phase-space volume). For an ideal gas, \(\sim 10^{23}\) molecules each evolve in the same six-dimensional phase space \((x,y,z,p_x,p_y,p_z)\).

Each dot moves with the phase-space velocity \[ (\dot q, \dot p) = \left(\frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial p},\, -\frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial q}\right). \tag{13.52}\] Different dots move differently, so the cloud can change shape and orientation — but, as we now prove, not its volume.

Points cannot leave a comoving surface

Consider a closed surface \(S\) in phase space (Figure 13.8). Every point on it defines an orbit, so the whole surface is carried along by the flow. A point initially inside \(S\) can never leave: to do so it would have to cross the moving surface, which would mean two orbits crossing — impossible. Likewise an outside point stays outside. Hence the number of dots inside is constant.

Rate of change of volume

The volume \(V\) enclosed by a comoving surface changes at a rate given by the flux through the surface: \[ \frac{dV}{dt} = \oint_S \mathbf n\cdot\mathbf v\,dA, \tag{13.54}\] where \(\mathbf n\) is the outward unit normal. (Derived by tracking a small cylinder of base \(dA\) and height \(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf v\,\delta t\).) If the flow is incompressible, \(dV/dt = 0\) because the outward and inward contributions cancel.

The divergence theorem

The surface integral is converted to a volume integral by Gauss’s (divergence) theorem, \[ \oint_S \mathbf n\cdot\mathbf v\,dA = \int_V \nabla\cdot\mathbf v\,dV, \tag{13.56}\] with the divergence \[ \nabla\cdot\mathbf v = \frac{\partial v_x}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial v_y}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial v_z}{\partial z}. \tag{13.55}\] Physically, \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf v\) is the outward flow per unit volume: positive means local expansion, negative local contraction, zero incompressibility.

Example 13.7 — A Shearing Flow

A fluid with velocity \[ \mathbf v = ky\,\hat{\mathbf x}, \qquad v_x = ky,\quad v_y = v_z = 0 \tag{13.57}\] is a laminar shear: planes \(y=\text{const}\) slide rigidly at speed \(\propto y\). A spherical blob is stretched into an ever-thinner ellipsoid. Its divergence is \[ \nabla\cdot\mathbf v = \frac{\partial (ky)}{\partial x} + 0 + 0 = 0, \tag{13.58}\] so \(dV/dt = 0\): the flow is incompressible, and any comoving volume is constant.

Proof for Hamiltonian flow

In the 2-D case (\(n=1\), \(\mathbf z = (q,p)\)), the phase-space velocity is \(\mathbf v = (\dot q,\dot p) = (\partial\mathcal H/\partial p,\, -\partial\mathcal H/\partial q)\), so \[ \nabla\cdot\mathbf v = \frac{\partial \dot q}{\partial q} + \frac{\partial \dot p}{\partial p} = \frac{\partial^2\mathcal H}{\partial q\,\partial p} - \frac{\partial^2\mathcal H}{\partial p\,\partial q} = 0, \tag{13.61}\] because mixed partial derivatives commute. Since the divergence vanishes identically, \(dV/dt = 0\):

Liouville’s theorem. The volume occupied by any group of phase-space points — equivalently, the phase-space density \(\rho = N/V\) along an orbit — is constant in time. The swarm moves like an incompressible fluid.

Equivalently, since \(N\) and \(V\) are both constant, the density at each moving phase point is unchanged, even though \(\rho\) can differ from point to point: \[ \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \{\rho,\mathcal H\} = 0. \qquad \text{(see the Poisson section below)}\]

Consequences and caveats

  • Chaos. When motion is chaotic, nearby phase points separate rapidly. Liouville says the total volume cannot grow, so the blob must stretch and fold: it grows long and thin and wraps intricately around itself within the bounded energy surface. This is the geometric face of chaos.
  • Validity is very general. The proof uses only Hamilton’s equations. It does not require \(\mathcal H = T+U\), natural coordinates, or time-independent forces. A charged particle in an electromagnetic field obeys Hamilton’s equations and hence Liouville’s theorem, even though \(\mathcal H \neq T+U\) and may depend explicitly on time.
  • No Lagrangian analogue. Liouville’s theorem applies to the Hamiltonian phase space \((q,p)\). There is no corresponding theorem for the Lagrangian state space \((q,\dot q)\). This is among the most important advantages of Hamilton’s formulation, and the reason statistical mechanics is built on phase space.

Principal Definitions and Equations of Chapter 13

The Hamiltonian. For coordinates \(\mathbf q = (q_1,\dots,q_n)\) and momenta \(p_i = \partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot q_i\), \[ \mathcal H = \sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i\dot q_i - \mathcal L, \tag{13.22}\] always regarded as a function of \(\mathbf q,\mathbf p\) (and possibly \(t\)).

Hamilton’s equations. \[ \dot q_i = \frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial p_i}, \qquad \dot p_i = -\frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial q_i}, \qquad [i=1,\dots,n]. \tag{13.25}\]

Phase space and orbits. Phase space is the \(2n\)-dimensional space of points \((\mathbf q,\mathbf p)\). A phase-space orbit is the path traced as time evolves. Orbits never cross.

Liouville’s theorem. The density of a swarm of identical systems in phase space is constant in time — equivalently, the volume occupied by any group of points is constant.

Supplement: Poisson Brackets, Canonical Transformations, and Noether

The textbook stops at Liouville’s theorem, but the same machinery is usually packaged in a notation that makes the structure transparent.

Poisson brackets

For any two functions \(F,G\) on phase space define the Poisson bracket \[ \{F,G\} = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial q_i}\frac{\partial G}{\partial p_i} - \frac{\partial F}{\partial p_i}\frac{\partial G}{\partial q_i}\right).\] It is bilinear, antisymmetric (\(\{F,G\}=-\{G,F\}\)), and satisfies the Jacobi identity \[ \{F,\{G,H\}\} + \{G,\{H,F\}\} + \{H,\{F,G\}\} = 0,\] which makes the phase-space functions a Lie algebra. The fundamental brackets are \[ \{q_i,q_j\} = 0, \qquad \{p_i,p_j\} = 0, \qquad \{q_i,p_j\} = \delta_{ij}.\]

Hamilton’s equations become the single elegant statement \[ \dot F = \{F,\mathcal H\} + \frac{\partial F}{\partial t},\] so that \(\{F,\mathcal H\}=0\) (and \(F\) not explicitly time-dependent) characterizes a conserved quantity. Liouville’s equation for the phase-space density is \(\partial_t\rho + \{\rho,\mathcal H\} = 0\). A canonical transformation is precisely a change of coordinates that preserves the fundamental brackets.

Noether’s theorem in Hamiltonian form

A continuous symmetry whose generator \(G\) satisfies \(\{G,\mathcal H\}=0\) has \(G\) as a conserved charge, and the symmetry acts by bracket flow. This is the Hamiltonian face of the Lagrangian Noether theorem from Chapter 7:

Symmetry Generator / conserved quantity
time translation \(\mathcal H\) (energy)
spatial translation total momentum \(\mathbf p\)
rotation total angular momentum \(\mathbf L\)
phase (gauge on \(p\)) charge

Bridge to quantum mechanics

Dirac’s correspondence replaces the Poisson bracket by a commutator, \[ \{F,G\} \;\longrightarrow\; \frac{1}{i\hbar}\,[\hat F,\hat G],\] so that \(\{q_i,p_j\}=\delta_{ij}\) becomes \([\hat q_i,\hat p_j]=i\hbar\,\delta_{ij}\). Hamilton’s phase-space formulation is thus the classical skeleton on which canonical quantization is built — one of the clearest reasons it matters.

Computational Companion

Symplectic integrators preserve phase-space area exactly (up to round-off), which is why they are preferred in Hamiltonian simulation. The first block integrates the harmonic oscillator of Example 13.5 and exposes the ellipses and the tiny energy drift.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# --- Example 13.5: harmonic oscillator, H = p^2/(2m) + (1/2) m w^2 x^2 ---
m, w = 2.0, 1.0
dt, nsteps = 0.01, 6000

def stormer_verlet(x0, p0, dt=dt, n=nsteps):
    """Symplectic (kick-drift-kick) integration: preserves phase-space area."""
    x, p = x0, p0
    xs, ps = [x], [p]
    for _ in range(n):
        p -= 0.5 * dt * m * w**2 * x   # half kick
        x += dt * p / m                # full drift
        p -= 0.5 * dt * m * w**2 * x   # half kick
        xs.append(x)
        ps.append(p)
    return np.array(xs), np.array(ps)

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6.2, 5.0))
for x0, p0, c in [(1.0, 0.0, "tab:blue"),
                  (0.5, 0.0, "tab:orange"),
                  (0.0, 0.0, "tab:green")]:
    xs, ps = stormer_verlet(x0, p0)
    E = 0.5 * ps**2 / m + 0.5 * m * w**2 * xs**2
    drift = float(np.abs(E - E[0]).max())
    ax.plot(xs, ps, color=c, lw=1.2,
            label=fr"$E_0$={E[0]:.2f}, max drift={drift:.1e}")

ax.set_ylabel("momentum $p$")
ax.set_title("Phase-space orbits: concentric, non-crossing ellipses")

# --- Example 13.6: falling body, H = p^2/(2m) - m g x  (x downward) ---
m, g, t = 1.0, 9.8, 0.7

def evolve(x0, p0):
    """Exact flow: xdot = p/m, pdot = m g."""
    return x0 + p0 * t / m + 0.5 * g * t**2, p0 + m * g * t

# corners of an initial rectangle (ordered so the shoelace formula is valid)
rectangle = [(0.0, 0.0), (1.0, 0.0), (1.0, 2.0), (0.0, 2.0)]

Lean 4 Formalization of Hamiltonian Mechanics

The Hamiltonian structure is algebraic, so it formalizes cleanly: phase space is a type with a symplectic form, and Hamilton’s equations are a predicate on curves.

import Mathlib

-- phase space (cotangent bundle) as a normed vector space M with a Hamiltonian
variable {M : Type*} [NormedAddCommGroup M] [NormedSpace ℝ M]
variable (H : M → ℝ) (hH : ContDiff ℝ 2 H)

-- a trajectory in phase space and Hamilton's equations (schematic):
--   qdot = ∂H/∂p, pdot = -∂H/∂q  become  z' = X_H(z)  for the Hamiltonian field
variable (z : ℝ → M)
def IsHamiltonianFlow : Prop :=
  ∀ t, deriv z t = fderiv ℝ H (z t) 1         -- schematic: X_H from the symplectic form

-- a Poisson bracket: bilinear, antisymmetric, satisfying Jacobi
variable (br : M → M → ℝ)
def IsPoisson : Prop :=
  (∀ x, br x x = 0) ∧                            -- antisymmetry
  (∀ x y, br x y = - br y x) ∧
  (∀ x y z, br x (br y z) + br y (br z x) + br z (br x y) = 0)  -- Jacobi

-- conserved quantity = vanishing bracket with the Hamiltonian
def IsConserved (F : M → ℝ) : Prop := ∀ x, br x (gradient H x) = 0

What the formalization makes precise:

  • Legendre transform as a type-level operation. \(p = \partial\mathcal L/\partial v\) must be invertible; in Lean this is the hypothesis that the velocity-Hessian is positive definite (PosDef), i.e. convexity in the velocities.
  • Hamilton’s equations are first-order. The state is a single term z : ℝ → M, not a curve with its derivative carried along.
  • Phase space is TangentBundle/CotangentBundle. Coordinates \((q,p)\) are a chart on \(T^*Q\); the symplectic form is a 2-form ω : M → M → ℝ (antisymmetric, closed, nondegenerate).
  • Conservation is an algebraic identity. \(\{F,\mathcal H\} = 0\) is checkable from the bracket axioms — no analysis required once the bracket is set up.
  • Liouville is a volume identity. Incompressibility is the statement that the Hamiltonian vector field is divergence-free, ∀ z, fderiv ℝ (fun z => X H z) z = 0 combined with the divergence theorem.
  • Gaps. Mathlib contains manifolds, TangentSpace, and Lie theory, but symplectic geometry and the Legendre transform are only partially developed, so a full formalization of Hamiltonian mechanics currently requires building the symplectic and Poisson machinery.

## Three Formulations of One Mechanics

| | Newton (1687) | Lagrange (1788) | Hamilton (1834) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Primary objects | forces, accelerations | $\mathcal L = T-U$ | $\mathcal H = \sum p_i\dot q_i - \mathcal L$ |
| State variables | $(\mathbf r, \dot{\mathbf r})$ | $(q, \dot q)$ | $(q, p)$ |
| Space | physical/$3N$-dim | configuration $Q$ / state space | **phase space** $T^*Q$ |
| Equations | $\mathbf F = \dot{\mathbf p}$ | $n$ second-order | $2n$ first-order |
| Best for | Cartesian, elementary | constraints, any coordinates | conserved quantities, chaos, QM |
| Weakness | constraint forces | dissipative systems | non-conservative systems |

All three are **equivalent** — either can be derived from the other — but each makes a different class of problems transparent. ## 13.1 The Basic Variables

### Review of the Lagrangian picture

For most systems of interest the Lagrangian is the difference of kinetic and potential energy,
$$ \mathcal L = T - U, \tag{13.1}$$
and is a function of the $n$ generalized coordinates $q_1,\dots,q_n$, their $n$ generalized velocities $\dot q_1,\dots,\dot q_n$, and possibly the time:
$$ \mathcal L = \mathcal L(q_1,\dots,q_n,\dot q_1,\dots,\dot q_n,t).$$

- The $n$ coordinates $(q_1,\dots,q_n)$ define a point in **configuration space** and specify a *configuration* of the system.
- The $2n$ coordinates $(q_1,\dots,q_n,\dot q_1,\dots,\dot q_n)$ define a point in **state space** and specify an initial condition that determines a unique **orbit**.
- The motion is governed by the $n$ second-order **Lagrange equations**
$$ \frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial q_i} = \frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial \dot q_i}, \qquad [i=1,\dots,n]. \tag{13.2}$$

### The generalized momentum

For each coordinate define the **generalized momentum** (also called the *canonical* or *conjugate* momentum)
$$ p_i = \frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial \dot q_i}. \tag{13.3}$$
If the $q_i$ are Cartesian, the $p_i$ are the ordinary momentum components. In general $p_i$ is *not* a momentum in the Newtonian sense, but plays an exactly analogous role. For a charged particle in a magnetic field, for instance, $p_i = m\dot x_i + qA_i$ — the canonical momentum, not $m\dot x_i$.

### The Hamiltonian

The central object of the new formulation is the **Hamiltonian function**
$$ \boxed{\ \mathcal H = \sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i \dot q_i - \mathcal L\ } \tag{13.4}$$
which replaces $\mathcal L$. We met it briefly in §7.8, where we proved that for **natural** coordinates (the relation of the $q$'s to the underlying Cartesians is time-independent) it is just the total energy,
$$ \mathcal H = T + U \qquad \text{(natural coordinates)}.$$

### Two important differences from the Lagrangian

1. **Physical significance.** Unlike $\mathcal L$, the Hamiltonian $\mathcal H$ is (for most systems) the total energy and is frequently conserved.
2. **Coordinates.** The Lagrangian labels the state by
$$ (q_1,\dots,q_n,\dot q_1,\dots,\dot q_n) \qquad \text{[Lagrange]} \tag{13.5}$$
whereas Hamilton uses the *positions together with the momenta*:
$$ (q_1,\dots,q_n,p_1,\dots,p_n) \qquad \text{[Hamilton]}. \tag{13.6}$$

Just as (13.5) defines a point in $2n$-dimensional **state space**, (13.6) defines a point in the $2n$-dimensional **phase space**. The succinct way to state the advantage of the Hamiltonian formalism is that **phase space has geometric properties that make it more convenient than state space**.

> **Terminology.** Many authors use "state space" and "phase space" interchangeably. Taylor reserves **state space** for positions + generalized *velocities* and **phase space** for positions + generalized *momenta*.

Throughout the chapter we assume all forces are conservative (or derivable from a potential), so no friction. This still covers an enormous range of problems, especially in astrophysics and at the atomic/molecular level.

## 13.2 Hamilton's Equations for One-Dimensional Systems

To avoid index clutter, begin with one degree of freedom and a single natural coordinate $q$. For a conservative system
$$ \mathcal L = \mathcal L(q,\dot q) = T(q,\dot q) - U(q). \tag{13.7}$$
**The kinetic energy may depend on $q$, but only quadratically on $\dot q$.** We proved in §7.8 that the general form is
$$ \mathcal L(q,\dot q) = A(q)\,\dot q^2 - U(q), \tag{13.10}$$
where $A(q)$ need not be constant. For example:

- **Simple pendulum** (mass $m$, length $L$, $q=\phi$):
$$ \mathcal L = \tfrac12 mL^2\dot\phi^2 - mgL(1-\cos\phi). \tag{13.8}$$
- **Bead on a curved wire** $y=f(x)$:
$$ \mathcal L = \tfrac12 m[1+f'(x)^2]\dot x^2 - mg\,f(x). \tag{13.9}$$

### Deriving Hamilton's equations

The Hamiltonian is defined by (13.4), which in one dimension reads
$$ \mathcal H = p\dot q - \mathcal L. \tag{13.11}$$

From (13.10),
$$ p = \frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial \dot q} = 2A(q)\dot q \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad p\dot q = 2T,$$
so
$$ \mathcal H = p\dot q - \mathcal L = 2T - (T - U) = T + U = E. \tag{13.12}$$
The Hamiltonian is the total energy.

**The subtle step.** $\mathcal L$ is a function of $q$ and $\dot q$; (13.12) gives $p$ in terms of $q$ and $\dot q$. We must **solve for $\dot q$ in terms of $q$ and $p$**,
$$ \dot q = \frac{p}{2A(q)} = \dot q(q,p), \tag{13.13}$$
and substitute it everywhere, so that
$$ \mathcal H(q,p) = p\,\dot q(q,p) - \mathcal L\big(q,\dot q(q,p)\big). \tag{13.14}$$

Now differentiate (13.14) with respect to $q$, using the chain rule:
$$ \frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial q} = p\frac{\partial \dot q}{\partial q} - \frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial q} - \frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial \dot q}\frac{\partial \dot q}{\partial q}.$$
The first and third terms cancel because $\partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot q = p$, leaving
$$ \frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial q} = -\frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial q} = -\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial \dot q} = -\dot p, \tag{13.15}$$
where the second equality is the Lagrange equation (13.2). Differentiating (13.14) with respect to $p$ gives
$$ \frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial p} = \dot q + p\frac{\partial \dot q}{\partial p} - \frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial \dot q}\frac{\partial \dot q}{\partial p} = \dot q. \tag{13.16}$$

Collecting the results:

> **Hamilton's equations (one dimension).**
> $$ \boxed{\ \dot q = \frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial p}, \qquad \dot p = -\frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial q}\ } \tag{13.17}$$

In the Lagrangian formalism a 1-D system obeys **one second-order** equation for $q$; in the Hamiltonian formalism it obeys **two first-order** equations, one for $q$ and one for $p$.

> **The Legendre transformation.** The change from $\mathcal L$ to $\mathcal H$ as the primary object is an example of a **Legendre transformation**, central in thermodynamics: the change from internal energy $U$ to enthalpy $H = U + pV$ is exactly analogous to Hamilton's change from $\mathcal L$ to $\mathcal H$.

### Example 13.1 — A Bead on a Straight Wire

A bead (mass $m$) slides on a frictionless straight wire along the $x$ axis under a potential $U(x)$. With $q=x$,
$$ \mathcal L = \tfrac12 m\dot x^2 - U(x).$$
Lagrange's equation reproduces Newton:
$$ -\frac{dU}{dx} = m\ddot x. \tag{13.18}$$

For Hamilton, first find
$$ p = \frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial \dot x} = m\dot x \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \dot x = \frac{p}{m},$$
then substitute:
$$ \mathcal H = \frac{p^2}{2m} + U(x).$$
Hamilton's equations are
$$ \dot x = \frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial p} = \frac{p}{m}, \qquad \dot p = -\frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial x} = -\frac{dU}{dx}.$$
The first is the definition of momentum; substituting it into the second returns $m\ddot x = -dU/dx$. Newton, Lagrange, and Hamilton agree — in this trivial case none has an advantage.

### Example 13.2 — Atwood's Machine

Two masses $m_1, m_2$ hang over a massless frictionless pulley; use the downward distance $x$ of $m_1$ as the coordinate. From Example 7.3,
$$ T = \tfrac12(m_1+m_2)\dot x^2, \qquad U = -(m_1-m_2)gx. \tag{13.19}$$
Then
$$ \mathcal H = T + U = \frac{p^2}{2(m_1+m_2)} - (m_1-m_2)gx, \qquad p = (m_1+m_2)\dot x.$$
Hamilton's equations:
$$ \dot x = \frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial p} = \frac{p}{m_1+m_2}, \qquad \dot p = -\frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial x} = (m_1-m_2)g.$$
Combining them gives the familiar acceleration
$$ \ddot x = \frac{m_1-m_2}{m_1+m_2}\,g.$$

### The general procedure (one dimension)

The examples reveal the standard rhythm: write the Hamiltonian, then compute $p$, solve that relation for the velocity, and rewrite $\mathcal H$ as a function of $(q,p)$. Only then "turn the handle" of Hamilton's equations. There is no guarantee the resulting equations are easy — but there *is* an almost infallible recipe for finding them.

## 13.3 Hamilton's Equations in Several Dimensions

### Vector notation

Group the coordinates, velocities, and momenta into vectors
$$ \mathbf q = (q_1,\dots,q_n), \qquad \dot{\mathbf q} = (\dot q_1,\dots,\dot q_n), \qquad \mathbf p = (p_1,\dots,p_n).$$
These are $n$-dimensional vectors in the relevant space — *not* necessarily the $3$-vectors of ordinary space.

### Assumptions

The derivation assumes exactly what Chapter 7 assumed:

- constraints are **holonomic** (number of degrees of freedom = number of generalized coordinates);
- the non-constraint forces come from a potential $U$ (allowed to depend on $t$);
- the coordinate relations may depend on time — the generalized coordinates need *not* be natural.

The starting point is that there is a Lagrangian $\mathcal L(\mathbf q,\dot{\mathbf q},t)$ obeying the $n$ Lagrange equations (13.21). Define the Hamiltonian exactly as before,
$$ \mathcal H = \sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i\dot q_i - \mathcal L, \tag{13.22}$$
with
$$ p_i = \frac{\partial \mathcal L(q,\dot q,t)}{\partial \dot q_i}, \qquad [i=1,\dots,n]. \tag{13.23}$$

### Eliminating the velocities

View (13.23) as $n$ simultaneous equations for the $n$ velocities, and **in principle** solve them:
$$ \dot q_i = \dot q_i(\mathbf q,\mathbf p,t), \qquad\text{or}\qquad \dot{\mathbf q} = \dot{\mathbf q}(\mathbf q,\mathbf p,t).$$
Substituting into (13.22),
$$ \mathcal H(\mathbf q,\mathbf p,t) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i\,\dot q_i(\mathbf q,\mathbf p,t) - \mathcal L\big(\mathbf q,\dot{\mathbf q}(\mathbf q,\mathbf p,t),t\big). \tag{13.24}$$

Reasoning as in the one-dimensional case yields the multidimensional equations:

> **Hamilton's equations ($n$ dimensions).**
> $$ \boxed{\ \dot q_i = \frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial p_i}, \qquad \dot p_i = -\frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial q_i}, \qquad [i=1,\dots,n]\ } \tag{13.25}$$

For a system with $n$ degrees of freedom the Hamiltonian approach gives **$2n$ first-order** equations instead of the Lagrangian **$n$ second-order** equations.

### The time derivative of $\mathcal H$: total vs. partial

There is one more derivative to understand. Because $\mathcal H(\mathbf q,\mathbf p,t)$ depends on $t$ both *through the coordinates* and possibly *explicitly*, the chain rule gives $2n+1$ terms:
$$ \frac{d\mathcal H}{dt} = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial q_i}\dot q_i + \frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial p_i}\dot p_i\right] + \frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial t}. \tag{13.26}$$

- The left side, $d\mathcal H/dt$ (**total derivative**), is the actual rate of change as the motion proceeds.
- The term $\partial\mathcal H/\partial t$ (**partial derivative**) is the rate of change holding all $q_i, p_i$ fixed.

By Hamilton's equations (13.25), every bracket in the sum is
$$ \frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial q_i}\frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial p_i} + \frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial p_i}\left(-\frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial q_i}\right) = 0,$$
so each pair cancels and
$$ \boxed{\ \frac{d\mathcal H}{dt} = \frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial t}\ }$$
That is, **$\mathcal H$ varies with time only to the extent that it is explicitly time-dependent.** In particular, if $\mathcal H$ has no explicit $t$-dependence, it is **conserved** — the Hamiltonian version of the result of §7.8. (One can also show $\partial\mathcal H/\partial t = -\partial\mathcal L/\partial t$; see Problem 13.16.)

For the remainder of the chapter we restrict to natural coordinates and no explicit time dependence, so that $\mathcal H = T+U$ *and* the total energy is conserved.

### Example 13.3 — Central Force in Polar Coordinates

For a particle in a conservative central potential $U(r)$, use polar coordinates $(r,\phi)$ in the plane of motion. The kinetic energy is
$$ T = \tfrac12 m(\dot r^2 + r^2\dot\phi^2). \tag{13.27}$$
The coordinates $(r,\phi)$ are natural, so $\mathcal H = T+U$ expressed in terms of the momenta
$$ p_r = \frac{\partial T}{\partial \dot r} = m\dot r, \qquad p_\phi = \frac{\partial T}{\partial \dot\phi} = mr^2\dot\phi. \tag{13.28}$$
Here $p_r$ is the radial component of ordinary momentum, while $p_\phi$ is the **angular momentum**. Solving for the velocities,
$$ \dot r = \frac{p_r}{m}, \qquad \dot\phi = \frac{p_\phi}{mr^2},$$
and substituting:
$$ \mathcal H = \frac{1}{2m}\left(p_r^2 + \frac{p_\phi^2}{r^2}\right) + U(r). \tag{13.29}$$

The two radial equations are
$$ \dot r = \frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial p_r} = \frac{p_r}{m}, \qquad \dot p_r = -\frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial r} = \frac{p_\phi^2}{mr^3} - \frac{dU}{dr}. \tag{13.30}$$
Substituting the first into the second gives $m\ddot r = -dU/dr + p_\phi^2/(mr^3)$: the actual radial force **plus the centrifugal force**. The two angular equations are
$$ \dot\phi = \frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial p_\phi} = \frac{p_\phi}{mr^2}, \qquad \dot p_\phi = -\frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial \phi} = 0, \tag{13.31}$$
the second of which states **angular momentum conservation**. Once again Hamilton reproduces the known physics.

### The six-step recipe

For any conservative system:

1. **Choose** generalized coordinates $q_1,\dots,q_n$.
2. **Write down** $T$ and $U$ in terms of the $q$'s and $\dot q$'s.
3. **Find** the generalized momenta $p_i$. (For conservative systems with $U$ independent of the velocities, $p_i = \partial T/\partial \dot q_i$; in general use $p_i = \partial \mathcal L/\partial \dot q_i$.)
4. **Solve** for the $\dot q$'s in terms of the $p$'s and $q$'s.
5. **Write** $\mathcal H$ as a function of the $q$'s and $p$'s. [For natural coordinates $\mathcal H = T+U$; when in doubt use $\mathcal H = \sum p_i\dot q_i - \mathcal L$.]
6. **Write down** Hamilton's equations (13.25).

Compared with the Lagrangian recipe, steps (3) and (4) are *extra* — an undeniably small disadvantage of Hamilton's method. The payoff comes later: conserved quantities, transformations, and geometry.

### Example 13.4 — A Mass on a Cone

A mass $m$ is constrained to the frictionless vertical cone $\rho = cz$ ($z>0$) in a uniform downward gravity $g$; use $z$ and $\phi$. With $\rho = cz$,
$$ T = \tfrac12 m\big[(c^2+1)\dot z^2 + c^2z^2\dot\phi^2\big], \qquad U = mgz.$$
The momenta are
$$ p_z = m(c^2+1)\dot z, \qquad p_\phi = mc^2z^2\dot\phi. \tag{13.32}$$
Hence
$$ \mathcal H = T+U = \frac{p_z^2}{2m(c^2+1)} + \frac{p_\phi^2}{2mc^2z^2} + mgz. \tag{13.33}$$

Hamilton's equations:
$$ \dot z = \frac{p_z}{m(c^2+1)}, \qquad \dot p_z = \frac{p_\phi^2}{mc^2z^3} - mg, \tag{13.34}$$
$$ \dot\phi = \frac{p_\phi}{mc^2z^2}, \qquad \dot p_\phi = 0. \tag{13.35}$$

The last equation says $p_\phi$, the $z$-component of angular momentum, is **constant**. Because $\mathcal H = E$ is conserved and the $\phi$-term diverges as $z\to 0$ while $mgz$ diverges as $z\to\infty$, the height $z$ is trapped between a minimum $z_{\min}>0$ and a maximum $z_{\max}$. The mass spirals up and down the cone, **never falling to the bottom** (unless $p_\phi = 0$, in which case it slides radially down). For a solution of fixed height $z$, we need $\dot p_z = 0$, i.e.
$$ p_\phi = \pm\sqrt{m^2c^2gz^3}, \tag{13.36}$$
so a horizontal circular orbit exists at any chosen height with the right angular momentum.

## 13.4 Ignorable Coordinates

In Chapter 7 we saw that if $\mathcal L$ is independent of a coordinate $q_i$, then the conjugate momentum is constant (because $\partial\mathcal L/\partial q_i = (d/dt)\,\partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot q_i = \dot p_i$). Such a coordinate is **ignorable**.

Exactly the same holds in the Hamiltonian picture: if $\mathcal H$ is independent of $q_i$, then
$$ \dot p_i = -\frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial q_i} = 0 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad p_i = \text{const}.$$
The two statements are the same because
$$ \frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial q_i} = -\frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial q_i} \tag{13.22}$$
(Problem 13.22). So **$\mathcal L$ is independent of $q_i$ iff $\mathcal H$ is.**

### Why Hamilton handles it more cleanly

Suppose a two-degree-of-freedom system has $q_2$ ignorable, so
$$ \mathcal H = \mathcal H(q_1,p_1,p_2). \tag{13.37}$$
Since $p_2 = k$ is a constant fixed by the initial conditions, substitution gives
$$ \mathcal H = \mathcal H(q_1,p_1,k),$$
a function of only two variables — and the motion is reduced to an **effective one-dimensional problem**. With several ignorable coordinates the reduction is even greater: a system with $n$ degrees of freedom reduces to one with $n-1$ (or fewer).

The Lagrangian does **not** simplify as cleanly. With $q_2$ ignorable we would have
$$ \mathcal L = \mathcal L(q_1,\dot q_1,\dot q_2),$$
and although $p_2$ is constant, $\dot q_2$ need not be, so the Lagrangian keeps its dependence on the "removed" degree of freedom. For the central-force problem, for example, the Lagrangian is $\mathcal L(r,\dot r,\dot\phi)$; even though $\phi$ is ignorable, $\dot\phi$ varies, and the problem does not automatically become one-dimensional. The Hamiltonian's ability to *freeze* an ignorable momentum is one of its genuine structural advantages.

## 13.5 Lagrange's Equations vs. Hamilton's Equations

For $n$ degrees of freedom:

- **Lagrange:** $n$ second-order equations for $q_1,\dots,q_n$.
- **Hamilton:** $2n$ first-order equations for $q_1,\dots,q_n,p_1,\dots,p_n$.

Converting $n$ second-order equations into $2n$ first-order equations is not by itself a virtue — any second-order system can be recast this way. For one degree of freedom,
$$ f(\ddot q,\dot q,q) = 0 \tag{13.38}$$
with $s = \dot q$ becomes the pair
$$ \dot q = s, \qquad f(\dot s, s, q) = 0. \tag{13.39, 13.40}$$

### The real improvement: the *form*

The power of Hamilton's version is the *specific shape* of the equations. Write the position equations as
$$ \dot q_i = \frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial p_i} = f_i(\mathbf q,\mathbf p), \qquad \dot{\mathbf q} = \mathbf f(\mathbf q,\mathbf p), \tag{13.41, 13.42}$$
the momentum equations as
$$ \dot p_i = -\frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial q_i} = g_i(\mathbf q,\mathbf p), \qquad \dot{\mathbf p} = \mathbf g(\mathbf q,\mathbf p), \tag{13.43}$$
and combine everything into the **phase-space vector**
$$ \mathbf z = (\mathbf q,\mathbf p) = (q_1,\dots,q_n,p_1,\dots,p_n). \tag{13.44}$$
Then Hamilton's equations collapse into a single first-order equation:
$$ \boxed{\ \dot{\mathbf z} = \mathbf h(\mathbf z)\ } \tag{13.45}$$
with $\mathbf h$ the vector of the $2n$ functions $f_i,g_i$. This "derivative-of-a-vector = function-of-the-vector" form is the standard form studied throughout the theory of differential equations. When $\mathcal H$ has no explicit time dependence the system is called **autonomous**. That Hamilton's equations are *automatically* of this form — unlike Lagrange's — is a substantial conceptual advantage.

### Flexibility: configuration space and phase space

The Lagrangian is famous for its coordinate freedom: replacing $\mathbf q$ by any new set $\mathbf Q = \mathbf Q(\mathbf q)$ leaves Lagrange's equations valid. The Hamiltonian shares this invariance, but goes **much further**. Because $\mathbf q$ and $\mathbf p$ can be *intermingled*, one may consider transformations
$$ \mathbf Q = \mathbf Q(\mathbf q,\mathbf p), \qquad \mathbf P = \mathbf P(\mathbf q,\mathbf p). \tag{13.47}$$
If these satisfy certain conditions they form a **canonical transformation**, and Hamilton's equations are *invariant* under them. This has no Lagrangian counterpart: since $\dot q$ is defined as the time derivative of $q$, there is no way to mix the $q$'s with the $\dot q$'s in the state-space formulation.

Canonical transformations are the gateway to the advanced theory (Hamilton–Jacobi theory, action-angle variables, perturbation theory). Here it is enough to know they exist and why they matter.

## 13.6 Phase-Space Orbits

A point $\mathbf z_0$ in phase space is an initial condition. Hamilton's equations (13.45) define a unique **phase-space orbit** through it. Because phase space has $2n$ dimensions, visualization is easy only when $n=1$; for a single particle in 3-D, $n=3$ and the phase space is six-dimensional. Techniques such as the **Poincaré section** (§12.8) project the motion into lower-dimensional slices.

### Orbits never cross

Suppose two orbits passed through the same phase point $\mathbf z_0$ (Figure 13.4). Hamilton's equations assign to each point a *single* velocity, hence a single orbit through $\mathbf z_0$. Therefore the two orbits must coincide. In fact this forbids different orbits from passing through the same point *even at different times* (for a time-independent Hamiltonian) — no orbit can ever revisit a point another orbit has visited. This "non-crossing" property severely constrains the geometry of phase-space flow and is central to the analysis of **chaotic Hamiltonian systems**.

### Example 13.5 — The Harmonic Oscillator

For mass $m$, force constant $k$, natural frequency $\omega = \sqrt{k/m}$:
$$ \mathcal H = T+U = \frac{p^2}{2m} + \tfrac12 m\omega^2 x^2. \tag{13.48}$$
Hamilton's equations are
$$ \dot x = \frac{p}{m}, \qquad \dot p = -m\omega^2 x.$$
Eliminating $p$ gives $\ddot x = -\omega^2 x$, with solution
$$ x = A\cos(\omega t - \delta), \qquad p = m\dot x = -m\omega A\sin(\omega t - \delta). \tag{13.49}$$
This is the parametric equation of an **ellipse traced clockwise** in the $(x,p)$ plane. Conservation of energy makes the shape manifest: setting $\mathcal H = \tfrac12 m\omega^2 A^2$,
$$ \frac{p^2}{2m} + \tfrac12 m\omega^2 x^2 = \tfrac12 m\omega^2 A^2 \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \frac{x^2}{A^2} + \frac{p^2}{(m\omega A)^2} = 1. \tag{13.50}$$
The semiaxes are $A$ (in $x$) and $m\omega A$ (in $p$). Different energies give concentric ellipses that never intersect. Following the solid orbit from rest at $x=A,p=0$: the restoring force accelerates $m$ back toward $x=0$, so $x$ decreases while $p$ becomes negative, reaching its most negative value $-m\omega A$ at $x=0$; the oscillator overshoots, and by $x=-A$ the momentum is again zero.

Phase space here is closely related to the $(x,\dot x)$ state-space picture of §12.7 — for 1-D they differ only by the vertical stretch $p = m\dot x$. In $2n$ dimensions, however, the two spaces can be very different, and it is phase space that carries the elegant structure.

### Example 13.6 — A Falling Body

For a mass in a vertical line under gravity, with $x$ measured *downward*,
$$ T = \tfrac12 m\dot x^2, \qquad U = -mgx, \qquad \mathcal H = \frac{p^2}{2m} - mgx. \tag{13.51}$$
Conservation of energy ($\mathcal H = \text{const}$) already tells us the orbits are **parabolas** $x = kp^2 + \text{const}$, symmetric about the $x$ axis. Solving the equations of motion,
$$ \dot x = \frac{p}{m}, \qquad \dot p = mg \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad p = p_0 + mgt, \qquad x = x_0 + \frac{p_0}{m}t + \tfrac12 gt^2.$$
Four initial conditions $(a)$–$(d)$ in the text produce four non-crossing parabolas (Figure 13.6). The striking feature: an initial **rectangle** of states $A_0B_0C_0D_0$ evolves into a **parallelogram** $ABCD$ of *equal area*. Shape changes; area does not. This is a concrete instance of **Liouville's theorem**.

## 13.7 Liouville's Theorem

*(This section uses the divergence operator and the divergence theorem of vector calculus.)*

### The fluid picture

In statistical mechanics one tracks an enormous number of identical systems. Each system is a **dot** in phase space; a large swarm of dots behaves like a **fluid** of density $\rho$ (dots per unit phase-space volume). For an ideal gas, $\sim 10^{23}$ molecules each evolve in the same six-dimensional phase space $(x,y,z,p_x,p_y,p_z)$.

Each dot moves with the **phase-space velocity**
$$ (\dot q, \dot p) = \left(\frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial p},\, -\frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial q}\right). \tag{13.52}$$
Different dots move differently, so the cloud can change **shape** and orientation — but, as we now prove, **not its volume**.

### Points cannot leave a comoving surface

Consider a closed surface $S$ in phase space (Figure 13.8). Every point on it defines an orbit, so the whole surface is carried along by the flow. A point initially inside $S$ can never leave: to do so it would have to cross the moving surface, which would mean two orbits crossing — impossible. Likewise an outside point stays outside. **Hence the number of dots inside is constant.**

### Rate of change of volume

The volume $V$ enclosed by a comoving surface changes at a rate given by the flux through the surface:
$$ \frac{dV}{dt} = \oint_S \mathbf n\cdot\mathbf v\,dA, \tag{13.54}$$
where $\mathbf n$ is the outward unit normal. (Derived by tracking a small cylinder of base $dA$ and height $\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf v\,\delta t$.) If the flow is incompressible, $dV/dt = 0$ because the outward and inward contributions cancel.

### The divergence theorem

The surface integral is converted to a volume integral by **Gauss's (divergence) theorem**,
$$ \oint_S \mathbf n\cdot\mathbf v\,dA = \int_V \nabla\cdot\mathbf v\,dV, \tag{13.56}$$
with the divergence
$$ \nabla\cdot\mathbf v = \frac{\partial v_x}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial v_y}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial v_z}{\partial z}. \tag{13.55}$$
Physically, $\nabla\cdot\mathbf v$ is the **outward flow per unit volume**: positive means local expansion, negative local contraction, zero incompressibility.

### Example 13.7 — A Shearing Flow

A fluid with velocity
$$ \mathbf v = ky\,\hat{\mathbf x}, \qquad v_x = ky,\quad v_y = v_z = 0 \tag{13.57}$$
is a laminar **shear**: planes $y=\text{const}$ slide rigidly at speed $\propto y$. A spherical blob is stretched into an ever-thinner ellipsoid. Its divergence is
$$ \nabla\cdot\mathbf v = \frac{\partial (ky)}{\partial x} + 0 + 0 = 0, \tag{13.58}$$
so $dV/dt = 0$: the flow is **incompressible**, and any comoving volume is constant.

### Proof for Hamiltonian flow

In the 2-D case ($n=1$, $\mathbf z = (q,p)$), the phase-space velocity is $\mathbf v = (\dot q,\dot p) = (\partial\mathcal H/\partial p,\, -\partial\mathcal H/\partial q)$, so
$$ \nabla\cdot\mathbf v = \frac{\partial \dot q}{\partial q} + \frac{\partial \dot p}{\partial p} = \frac{\partial^2\mathcal H}{\partial q\,\partial p} - \frac{\partial^2\mathcal H}{\partial p\,\partial q} = 0, \tag{13.61}$$
because mixed partial derivatives commute. Since the divergence vanishes identically, $dV/dt = 0$:

> **Liouville's theorem.** The volume occupied by any group of phase-space points — equivalently, the phase-space density $\rho = N/V$ along an orbit — is **constant in time**. The swarm moves like an incompressible fluid.

Equivalently, since $N$ and $V$ are both constant, the density **at each moving phase point** is unchanged, even though $\rho$ can differ from point to point:
$$ \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \{\rho,\mathcal H\} = 0. \qquad \text{(see the Poisson section below)}$$

### Consequences and caveats

- **Chaos.** When motion is chaotic, nearby phase points separate rapidly. Liouville says the *total* volume cannot grow, so the blob must **stretch and fold**: it grows long and thin and wraps intricately around itself within the bounded energy surface. This is the geometric face of chaos.
- **Validity is very general.** The proof uses *only* Hamilton's equations. It does **not** require $\mathcal H = T+U$, natural coordinates, or time-independent forces. A charged particle in an electromagnetic field obeys Hamilton's equations and hence Liouville's theorem, even though $\mathcal H \neq T+U$ and may depend explicitly on time.
- **No Lagrangian analogue.** Liouville's theorem applies to the Hamiltonian phase space $(q,p)$. There is **no** corresponding theorem for the Lagrangian state space $(q,\dot q)$. This is among the most important advantages of Hamilton's formulation, and the reason statistical mechanics is built on phase space.

## Principal Definitions and Equations of Chapter 13

> **The Hamiltonian.** For coordinates $\mathbf q = (q_1,\dots,q_n)$ and momenta $p_i = \partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot q_i$,
> $$ \mathcal H = \sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i\dot q_i - \mathcal L, \tag{13.22}$$
> always regarded as a function of $\mathbf q,\mathbf p$ (and possibly $t$).
>
> **Hamilton's equations.**
> $$ \dot q_i = \frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial p_i}, \qquad \dot p_i = -\frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial q_i}, \qquad [i=1,\dots,n]. \tag{13.25}$$
>
> **Phase space and orbits.** Phase space is the $2n$-dimensional space of points $(\mathbf q,\mathbf p)$. A phase-space orbit is the path traced as time evolves. Orbits never cross.
>
> **Liouville's theorem.** The density of a swarm of identical systems in phase space is constant in time — equivalently, the volume occupied by any group of points is constant.

## Supplement: Poisson Brackets, Canonical Transformations, and Noether

The textbook stops at Liouville's theorem, but the same machinery is usually packaged in a notation that makes the structure transparent.

### Poisson brackets

For any two functions $F,G$ on phase space define the **Poisson bracket**
$$ \{F,G\} = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial q_i}\frac{\partial G}{\partial p_i} - \frac{\partial F}{\partial p_i}\frac{\partial G}{\partial q_i}\right).$$
It is **bilinear**, **antisymmetric** ($\{F,G\}=-\{G,F\}$), and satisfies the **Jacobi identity**
$$ \{F,\{G,H\}\} + \{G,\{H,F\}\} + \{H,\{F,G\}\} = 0,$$
which makes the phase-space functions a **Lie algebra**. The **fundamental brackets** are
$$ \{q_i,q_j\} = 0, \qquad \{p_i,p_j\} = 0, \qquad \{q_i,p_j\} = \delta_{ij}.$$

Hamilton's equations become the single elegant statement
$$ \dot F = \{F,\mathcal H\} + \frac{\partial F}{\partial t},$$
so that $\{F,\mathcal H\}=0$ (and $F$ not explicitly time-dependent) characterizes a **conserved quantity**. Liouville's equation for the phase-space density is $\partial_t\rho + \{\rho,\mathcal H\} = 0$. A **canonical transformation** is precisely a change of coordinates that preserves the fundamental brackets.

### Noether's theorem in Hamiltonian form

A continuous symmetry whose generator $G$ satisfies $\{G,\mathcal H\}=0$ has $G$ as a conserved charge, and the symmetry acts by bracket flow. This is the Hamiltonian face of the Lagrangian Noether theorem from [Chapter 7](taylor-chapter-7-lagrange-equations.qmd):

| Symmetry | Generator / conserved quantity |
|---|---|
| time translation | $\mathcal H$ (energy) |
| spatial translation | total momentum $\mathbf p$ |
| rotation | total angular momentum $\mathbf L$ |
| phase (gauge on $p$) | charge |

### Bridge to quantum mechanics

Dirac's correspondence replaces the Poisson bracket by a commutator,
$$ \{F,G\} \;\longrightarrow\; \frac{1}{i\hbar}\,[\hat F,\hat G],$$
so that $\{q_i,p_j\}=\delta_{ij}$ becomes $[\hat q_i,\hat p_j]=i\hbar\,\delta_{ij}$. Hamilton's phase-space formulation is thus the classical skeleton on which canonical quantization is built — one of the clearest reasons it matters.

## Computational Companion

Symplectic integrators preserve phase-space area **exactly** (up to round-off), which is why they are preferred in Hamiltonian simulation. The first block integrates the harmonic oscillator of Example 13.5 and exposes the ellipses and the tiny energy drift.

```{python}
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# --- Example 13.5: harmonic oscillator, H = p^2/(2m) + (1/2) m w^2 x^2 ---
m, w = 2.0, 1.0
dt, nsteps = 0.01, 6000

def stormer_verlet(x0, p0, dt=dt, n=nsteps):
    """Symplectic (kick-drift-kick) integration: preserves phase-space area."""
    x, p = x0, p0
    xs, ps = [x], [p]
    for _ in range(n):
        p -= 0.5 * dt * m * w**2 * x   # half kick
        x += dt * p / m                # full drift
        p -= 0.5 * dt * m * w**2 * x   # half kick
        xs.append(x)
        ps.append(p)
    return np.array(xs), np.array(ps)

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6.2, 5.0))
for x0, p0, c in [(1.0, 0.0, "tab:blue"),
                  (0.5, 0.0, "tab:orange"),
                  (0.0, 0.0, "tab:green")]:
    xs, ps = stormer_verlet(x0, p0)
    E = 0.5 * ps**2 / m + 0.5 * m * w**2 * xs**2
    drift = float(np.abs(E - E[0]).max())
    ax.plot(xs, ps, color=c, lw=1.2,
            label=fr"$E_0$={E[0]:.2f}, max drift={drift:.1e}")
ax.set_xlabel("position $q$")
ax.set_ylabel("momentum $p$")
ax.set_title("Phase-space orbits: concentric, non-crossing ellipses")
ax.set_aspect("equal")
ax.legend(fontsize=8)
plt.show()
```

The second block verifies **Example 13.6 / Liouville's theorem**: an initial rectangle of states in the falling-body phase space evolves into a parallelogram of identical area.

```{python}
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# --- Example 13.6: falling body, H = p^2/(2m) - m g x  (x downward) ---
m, g, t = 1.0, 9.8, 0.7

def evolve(x0, p0):
    """Exact flow: xdot = p/m, pdot = m g."""
    return x0 + p0 * t / m + 0.5 * g * t**2, p0 + m * g * t

# corners of an initial rectangle (ordered so the shoelace formula is valid)
rectangle = [(0.0, 0.0), (1.0, 0.0), (1.0, 2.0), (0.0, 2.0)]
mapped = [evolve(x, p) for (x, p) in rectangle]

def shoelace(pts):
    x = np.array([p[0] for p in pts])
    y = np.array([p[1] for p in pts])
    return 0.5 * abs(np.dot(x, np.roll(y, 1)) - np.dot(y, np.roll(x, 1)))

a0, a1 = shoelace(rectangle), shoelace(mapped)
print(f"initial phase-space area = {a0:.6f}")
print(f"evolved phase-space area = {a1:.6f}")
print(f"relative change         = {abs(a1 - a0) / a0:.2e}   (Liouville)")

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6.2, 5.0))
for pts, c, lab in [(rectangle, "tab:blue", "initial rectangle"),
                    (mapped,    "tab:red",  f"evolved (t={t})")]:
    poly = np.array(pts + [pts[0]])
    ax.plot(poly[:, 0], poly[:, 1], "-o", color=c, label=lab)
ax.set_xlabel("position $x$ (downward)")
ax.set_ylabel("momentum $p$")
ax.set_title("Liouville: shape changes, area does not")
ax.legend(fontsize=8)
plt.show()
```

Both plots make the abstract statements concrete: the oscillator's orbits are nested ellipses, and the falling body's phase-space blob shears into a parallelogram without changing its area.

## Lean 4 Formalization of Hamiltonian Mechanics

The Hamiltonian structure is algebraic, so it formalizes cleanly: phase space is a type with a symplectic form, and Hamilton's equations are a predicate on curves.

```lean
import Mathlib

-- phase space (cotangent bundle) as a normed vector space M with a Hamiltonian
variable {M : Type*} [NormedAddCommGroup M] [NormedSpace ℝ M]
variable (H : M → ℝ) (hH : ContDiff ℝ 2 H)

-- a trajectory in phase space and Hamilton's equations (schematic):
--   qdot = ∂H/∂p, pdot = -∂H/∂q  become  z' = X_H(z)  for the Hamiltonian field
variable (z : ℝ → M)
def IsHamiltonianFlow : Prop :=
  ∀ t, deriv z t = fderiv ℝ H (z t) 1         -- schematic: X_H from the symplectic form

-- a Poisson bracket: bilinear, antisymmetric, satisfying Jacobi
variable (br : M → M → ℝ)
def IsPoisson : Prop :=
  (∀ x, br x x = 0) ∧                            -- antisymmetry
  (∀ x y, br x y = - br y x) ∧
  (∀ x y z, br x (br y z) + br y (br z x) + br z (br x y) = 0)  -- Jacobi

-- conserved quantity = vanishing bracket with the Hamiltonian
def IsConserved (F : M → ℝ) : Prop := ∀ x, br x (gradient H x) = 0
```

What the formalization makes precise:

- **Legendre transform as a type-level operation.** $p = \partial\mathcal L/\partial v$ must be *invertible*; in Lean this is the hypothesis that the velocity-Hessian is positive definite (`PosDef`), i.e. convexity in the velocities.
- **Hamilton's equations are first-order.** The state is a single term `z : ℝ → M`, not a curve with its derivative carried along.
- **Phase space is `TangentBundle`/`CotangentBundle`.** Coordinates $(q,p)$ are a chart on $T^*Q$; the symplectic form is a 2-form `ω : M → M → ℝ` (antisymmetric, closed, nondegenerate).
- **Conservation is an algebraic identity.** $\{F,\mathcal H\} = 0$ is checkable from the bracket axioms — no analysis required once the bracket is set up.
- **Liouville is a volume identity.** Incompressibility is the statement that the Hamiltonian vector field is divergence-free, `∀ z, fderiv ℝ (fun z => X H z) z = 0` combined with the divergence theorem.
- **Gaps.** Mathlib contains manifolds, `TangentSpace`, and Lie theory, but symplectic geometry and the Legendre transform are only partially developed, so a full formalization of Hamiltonian mechanics currently requires building the symplectic and Poisson machinery.

*As elsewhere, the names above are schematic and illustrative; the Mathlib API evolves.*
