1. Introduction: The Geometry of Pure Motion

Kinematics is the branch of classical mechanics that describes the motion of points, bodies, and systems of bodies without consideration of the forces that cause the motion. While dynamics asks why an object moves (relating forces to accelerations via Newton’s laws or Lagrange’s equations), kinematics asks how an object moves through space and time.

Kinematics provides the fundamental mathematical language of physics: the mapping between continuous temporal intervals and spatial trajectories.

  │  Position r(t)                                                          │
  │    │                                                                    │
  │    ▼ d/dt                                                               │
  │  Velocity v(t) = ṙ(t) = dr/dt                                           │
  │    │                                                                    │

  └─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

In modern high-speed railway design, elevator engineering, and aerospace trajectory synthesis, higher derivatives such as jerk (\(j = \dot{a}\)) and snap (\(s = \ddot{a}\)) are critical: discontinuous acceleration produces infinite jerk, inducing severe mechanical fatigue and biological whiplash in human passengers.

2. Mathematical Foundations: The Calculus of Motion

Let \(\mathbf{r}(t) \in \mathbb{R}^3\) denote the position vector of a particle relative to a chosen inertial Cartesian coordinate origin.

Differential Definitions

  1. Displacement Vector: The net vector change in position over a discrete time interval \([t_1, t_2]\): \[\Delta \mathbf{r} = \mathbf{r}(t_2) - \mathbf{r}(t_1) \tag{1}\] (Note the distinction between vector displacement \(|\Delta \mathbf{r}|\) and total scalar path length \(s = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} |\mathbf{v}(t)|\,dt\).)
  2. Instantaneous Velocity: The time derivative of position: \[\mathbf{v}(t) = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{\mathbf{r}(t + \Delta t) - \mathbf{r}(t)}{\Delta t} = \frac{d\mathbf{r}}{dt} = \dot{\mathbf{r}}(t) \tag{2}\] Velocity is always strictly tangent to the particle’s spatial trajectory.
  3. Instantaneous Acceleration: The time derivative of velocity: \[\mathbf{a}(t) = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{\mathbf{v}(t + \Delta t) - \mathbf{v}(t)}{\Delta t} = \frac{d\mathbf{v}}{dt} = \frac{d^2\mathbf{r}}{dt^2} = \ddot{\mathbf{r}}(t) \tag{3}\]

In curvilinear 3D motion, acceleration decomposes into tangential and normal components:

\[\mathbf{a}(t) = a_t \hat{\mathbf{T}} + a_n \hat{\mathbf{N}} = \frac{dv}{dt} \hat{\mathbf{T}} + \frac{v^2}{\rho_c} \hat{\mathbf{N}} \tag{4}\]

Where \(\hat{\mathbf{T}}\) is the unit tangent vector, \(\hat{\mathbf{N}}\) is the principal unit normal vector, \(v = |\mathbf{v}|\) is scalar speed, and \(\rho_c\) is the local radius of curvature of the path.

3. Constant Acceleration Kinematics (The SUVAT Equations)

When the acceleration vector is constant in magnitude and direction (\(\mathbf{a}(t) = \mathbf{a}_0 = \text{const}\)), the equations of motion can be integrated in closed form.

Systematic Derivation via Definite Integration

  1. Velocity from Acceleration: \[\int_{\mathbf{v}_0}^{\mathbf{v}(t)} d\mathbf{v}' = \int_0^t \mathbf{a}_0 \, dt' \implies \mathbf{v}(t) = \mathbf{v}_0 + \mathbf{a}_0 t \tag{5}\]
  2. Position from Velocity: \[\int_{\mathbf{r}_0}^{\mathbf{r}(t)} d\mathbf{r}' = \int_0^t \left( \mathbf{v}_0 + \mathbf{a}_0 t' \right) dt' \implies \mathbf{r}(t) = \mathbf{r}_0 + \mathbf{v}_0 t + \frac{1}{2} \mathbf{a}_0 t^2 \tag{6}\]
  3. Eliminating Time (The Work-Energy Kinematic Relation): In 1D along coordinate \(x\): \(a = \frac{dv}{dt} = \frac{dv}{dx} \frac{dx}{dt} = v \frac{dv}{dx}\). \[\int_{x_0}^{x} a_0 \, dx' = \int_{v_0}^{v} v' \, dv' \implies a_0 (x - x_0) = \frac{1}{2} v^2 - \frac{1}{2} v_0^2\] \[v^2 = v_0^2 + 2 a_0 (x - x_0) \tag{7}\]
  4. Average Velocity Representation: Because velocity changes linearly with time under constant acceleration: \[\bar{v} = \frac{v_0 + v(t)}{2} \implies x(t) - x_0 = \left( \frac{v_0 + v(t)}{2} \right) t \tag{8}\]
                   THE SUVAT SYSTEM AT A GLANCE
  ┌────────────────────────────────┬──────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │ Equation                       │ Missing Variable                         │
  ├────────────────────────────────┼──────────────────────────────────────────┤
  │ v = v₀ + a t                   │ Displacement (x - x₀)                    │
  │ x = x₀ + v₀ t + ½ a t²         │ Final Velocity v                         │
  │ v² = v₀² + 2 a (x - x₀)        │ Time t                                   │
  │ x = x₀ + ½ (v₀ + v) t          │ Acceleration a                           │
  │ x = x₀ + v t - ½ a t²          │ Initial Velocity v₀                      │
  └────────────────────────────────┴──────────────────────────────────────────┘

4. One-Dimensional Free Fall Under Gravity

Consider vertical motion near the surface of the Earth. We erect a coordinate system where the vertical axis \(y\) points upward (\(\hat{\mathbf{j}}\)), so gravitational acceleration points downward:

\[\mathbf{a} = -g \hat{\mathbf{j}}, \quad g \approx 9.80665\text{ m/s}^2 \tag{9}\]

The scalar equations of motion become:

\[\begin{aligned} v(t) &= v_0 - g t \\ y(t) &= y_0 + v_0 t - \frac{1}{2} g t^2 \\ v^2(y) &= v_0^2 - 2 g (y - y_0) \end{aligned} \tag{10}\]

  1. Time to Reach Apogee (Peak Height): Setting \(v(t_{\text{apex}}) = 0\): \[0 = v_0 - g t_{\text{apex}} \implies t_{\text{apex}} = \frac{v_0}{g} \tag{11}\]
  2. Maximum Altitude (\(y_{\text{max}}\)): Substituting \(t_{\text{apex}}\) into position equation (10): \[y_{\text{max}} = y_0 + v_0 \left( \frac{v_0}{g} \right) - \frac{1}{2} g \left( \frac{v_0}{g} \right)^2 = y_0 + \frac{v_0^2}{2 g} \tag{12}\]
  3. Flight Symmetry for Ground-to-Ground (\(y_0 = 0\)): Setting \(y(t) = 0 \implies t(v_0 - \frac{1}{2} g t) = 0\). The landing time is exactly double the ascent time: \[t_{\text{land}} = 2 t_{\text{apex}} = \frac{2 v_0}{g} \tag{13}\] The impact velocity is \(v(t_{\text{land}}) = v_0 - g(2v_0/g) = -v_0\). The ascent and descent trajectories are exact mirror images in time.

5. Deconstructing the Negative Time Axis Intercept

Now consider the general case: an experimenter stands atop a cliff of height \(h > 0\) (\(y_0 = h\)) and throws a stone vertically upward with initial velocity \(v_0 > 0\) at time \(t = 0\).

We seek the time \(t\) when the stone strikes the ground at the base of the cliff (\(y = 0\)):

\[0 = h + v_0 t - \frac{1}{2} g t^2 \iff \frac{1}{2} g t^2 - v_0 t - h = 0 \tag{14}\]

Applying the quadratic formula:

\[t = \frac{v_0 \pm \sqrt{v_0^2 - 4\left(\frac{1}{2}g\right)(-h)}}{2\left(\frac{1}{2}g\right)} = \frac{v_0 \pm \sqrt{v_0^2 + 2 g h}}{g} \tag{15}\]

Because \(h > 0\) and \(g > 0\), the discriminant \(\sqrt{v_0^2 + 2 g h}\) is strictly greater than \(v_0\). This guarantees two real roots of opposite algebraic sign:

\[\begin{aligned} t_+ &= \frac{v_0 + \sqrt{v_0^2 + 2 g h}}{g} > 0 \quad (\text{Future landing time}) \\ t_- &= \frac{v_0 - \sqrt{v_0^2 + 2 g h}}{g} < 0 \quad (\text{Past negative time intercept}) \end{aligned} \tag{16}\]

In elementary textbooks, students are almost universally instructed:

“Discard \(t_-\) because time cannot be negative; it is an unphysical extraneous mathematical root.”

This dismissal is fundamentally mistaken. Mathematics does not produce “meaningless artifacts” by accident. The quadratic equation (14) does not know that a human hand released the stone at \(t = 0\). The differential equation \(\ddot{y} = -g\) governs a smooth, unbroken parabola extending across the entire real number line \(t \in (-\infty, +\infty)\).

Physical Interpretation 1: The Virtual Past Launch (Counterfactual Trajectory)

What does \(t_- < 0\) represent?

Physical Reality: \(t_-\) is the precise time in the past at which an observer at ground level (\(y = 0\)) would have had to launch an identical stone upward so that it would arrive at height \(y = h\) at exactly \(t = 0\) with velocity exactly equal to \(+v_0\).

Let us verify this mathematically:

  1. Suppose at time \(t = t_- < 0\), a stone is launched from \(y = 0\) with unknown initial velocity \(v_{\text{ground}}\).
  2. Its trajectory equation for all \(t\) is: \[y_{\text{virtual}}(t) = 0 + v_{\text{ground}} (t - t_-) - \frac{1}{2} g (t - t_-)^2 \tag{17}\]
  3. What is the velocity of this stone at \(t = t_-\)? By conservation of energy between \(y = 0\) and \(y = h\): \[\frac{1}{2} m v_{\text{ground}}^2 = \frac{1}{2} m v_0^2 + m g h \implies v_{\text{ground}} = \sqrt{v_0^2 + 2 g h} \tag{18}\]
  4. Now evaluate its state at \(t = 0\): \[v_{\text{virtual}}(0) = v_{\text{ground}} - g(0 - t_-) = \sqrt{v_0^2 + 2 g h} + g t_-\] Substituting \(t_- = \frac{v_0 - \sqrt{v_0^2 + 2gh}}{g}\): \[v_{\text{virtual}}(0) = \sqrt{v_0^2 + 2 g h} + g \left( \frac{v_0 - \sqrt{v_0^2 + 2 g h}}{g} \right) = v_0 \tag{19}\] The velocity at \(t = 0\) matches \(v_0\) identically.
  5. Evaluate its position at \(t = 0\): \[y_{\text{virtual}}(0) = v_{\text{ground}} (-t_-) - \frac{1}{2} g (-t_-)^2 = -v_{\text{ground}} t_- - \frac{1}{2} g t_-^2\] From the quadratic equation (14), since \(t_-\) is an exact root of \(\frac{1}{2} g t^2 - v_0 t - h = 0\): \[-\frac{1}{2} g t_-^2 + v_0 t_- + h = 0 \implies y_{\text{virtual}}(0) = h \tag{20}\]

Conclusion: The physical trajectory of the stone for \(t \ge 0\) is completely indistinguishable from the trajectory of a stone launched from the ground at \(t = t_-\). The negative root \(t_-\) is the origin time of the smooth kinematic history that produces the initial state \((y_0, v_0)\) at \(t = 0\).

Physical Interpretation 2: Time-Reversal Invariance (\(T\)-Symmetry)

Classical Newtonian gravitation without dissipation is invariant under time reversal:

\[t \to -t, \quad \frac{d^2 y}{d(-t)^2} = \frac{d^2 y}{dt^2} = -g \tag{21}\]

If you record a movie of the stone moving along its trajectory and run the film backward in time:

  • An object landing at \(t = t_+\) with downward velocity \(-v_{\text{impact}}\) becomes an object launched upward from the ground at \(t = -t_+\) with velocity \(+v_{\text{impact}}\).
  • The actual launch at \(t = 0\) from height \(h\) with upward velocity \(+v_0\) becomes, in reverse, an object passing through \(y = h\) at \(t = 0\) with downward velocity \(-v_0\).
  • Where did this backward-in-time trajectory originate? It originated by hitting the ground at \(t = |t_-| = -t_- > 0\).

The negative root \(t_-\) is the time-reversal mirror of the complementary kinematic branch.

Physical Interpretation 3: The Interceptor / Matching Velocity Tactic

In engineering ballistics, artillery fire control, and aerospace rendezvous, the negative root answers a fundamental practical question:

“If a launcher is located at ground level (\(y=0\)), at what time \(t_{\text{launch}}\) and with what launch speed \(v_L\) must an interceptor projectile be fired to match the state \((y=h, v=v_0)\) of a target at \(t = 0\)?”

              INTERCEPTOR TIME-DELAY MATCHING TAXONOMY
  ┌─────────────────────────┬──────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │ Physical Requirement    │ Operational Mathematical Mapping                 │
  ├─────────────────────────┼──────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
  │ Target Position at t=0  │ y(0) = h                                         │
  │ Target Velocity at t=0  │ v(0) = +v₀                                       │
  │ Required Launch Time    │ t_launch = t₋ = (v₀ - √(v₀² + 2gh)) / g < 0      │
  │ Time Delay Before t=0   │ Δt_advance = |t₋| = (√(v₀² + 2gh) - v₀) / g      │
  │ Required Launch Speed   │ v_launch = √(v₀² + 2gh)                          │
  │ Subsequent Landing Time │ t_land = t₊ = (v₀ + √(v₀² + 2gh)) / g > 0        │
  │ Total Flight Duration   │ T_total = t₊ - t₋ = (2 √(v₀² + 2gh)) / g         │
  └─────────────────────────┴──────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

Notice the remarkable elegance of the total flight duration:

\[\Delta T = t_+ - t_- = \frac{v_0 + \sqrt{v_0^2 + 2gh}}{g} - \frac{v_0 - \sqrt{v_0^2 + 2gh}}{g} = \frac{2\sqrt{v_0^2 + 2gh}}{g} = \frac{2 v_{\text{ground}}}{g} \tag{22}\]

The difference between the two roots (\(t_+ - t_-\)) is identically equal to the total round-trip flight time of a projectile launched from ground level with speed \(v_{\text{ground}}\)!

6. Worked Numerical Example: The Cliff-Drop Benchmark

Let a student stand atop a building of height \(h = 45\text{ m}\). At time \(t = 0\), the student throws a ball vertically upward with initial speed \(v_0 = 20\text{ m/s}\). Take \(g = 10\text{ m/s}^2\) for clean analytical arithmetic.

                      WORKED NUMERICAL TRAJECTORY
          y (meters) ▲
                     │                Peak: t = 2 s, y = 65 m
                65 ──┼──────────────────────●
                     │                     / \
                     │                    /   \
                45 ──┼────────● (t=0, v=+20)   \
                     │       /                  \
                     │      /                    \
                     │     /                      \
                 0 ──┴────●────────────────────────●─────► t (seconds)
                       t = -1.61 s               t = +5.61 s
                       v = +36.06 m/s            v = -36.06 m/s
  1. Calculate the Discriminant: \[\sqrt{v_0^2 + 2 g h} = \sqrt{20^2 + 2(10)(45)} = \sqrt{400 + 900} = \sqrt{1300} \approx 36.0555\text{ m/s}\]
  2. Compute Both Roots: \[t_+ = \frac{20 + 36.0555}{10} = \frac{56.0555}{10} \approx +5.61\text{ s}\] \[t_- = \frac{20 - 36.0555}{10} = \frac{-16.0555}{10} \approx -1.61\text{ s}\]
  3. Physical Interpretation:
    • The ball ascends to its peak at \(t_{\text{apex}} = 20/10 = 2.0\text{ s}\), reaching altitude \(y_{\text{max}} = 45 + \frac{20^2}{2(10)} = 65\text{ m}\).
    • It falls back past the roof line (\(y = 45\text{ m}\)) at \(t = 4.0\text{ s}\) with downward velocity \(v = -20\text{ m/s}\).
    • It strikes the ground at \(t = +5.61\text{ s}\) with impact velocity: \[v(5.61) = 20 - 10(5.6055) = -36.06\text{ m/s}\]
    • The Negative Root (\(t_- = -1.61\text{ s}\)): If an archer standing on the ground had fired an identical arrow upward \(1.61\) seconds before the student threw the ball (at \(t = -1.61\text{ s}\)) with an initial speed of \(36.06\text{ m/s}\):
      • At \(t = 0\), the arrow would reach height \(y = 45\text{ m}\).
      • At \(t = 0\), the arrow’s velocity would have decelerated to exactly \(36.06 - 10(1.61) = +20.0\text{ m/s}\).
      • The arrow and the ball would merge into the exact same trajectory, landing at the exact same spot at \(t = +5.61\text{ s}\).

7. Two-Dimensional Kinematic Projectiles and Vector Parabolae

The 1D analysis generalizes directly to 2D projectile motion in a vertical plane \((x, y)\).

Let a projectile be launched from \((x_0, y_0) = (0, h)\) with speed \(v_0\) at angle \(\theta\) above horizontal:

\[\mathbf{v}_0 = v_0 \cos \theta \, \hat{\mathbf{i}} + v_0 \sin \theta \, \hat{\mathbf{j}} \tag{23}\]

Because gravity acts strictly along the vertical axis (\(\mathbf{a} = -g \hat{\mathbf{j}}\)), the horizontal and vertical motions are completely decoupled:

\[\begin{aligned} x(t) &= (v_0 \cos \theta) t \\ y(t) &= h + (v_0 \sin \theta) t - \frac{1}{2} g t^2 \end{aligned} \tag{24}\]

Eliminating \(t = \frac{x}{v_0 \cos \theta}\) yields the spatial Cartesian trajectory:

\[y(x) = h + (\tan \theta) x - \frac{g}{2 v_0^2 \cos^2 \theta} x^2 \tag{25}\]

                   2D PROJECTILE WITH NEGATIVE X-INTERCEPT
         y ▲
           │                     Apex
           │                       ●
           │                    /     \
       h ──┼──────────● (t=0)  /       \
           │         /        /         \
           │        /        /           \
         0 ┼───────●────────┼─────────────●───────► x
                 x₋ < 0     0            x₊ > 0

Just as in the 1D case, setting \(y(x) = 0\) yields two spatial roots: \(x_+ > 0\) (the forward range where the projectile hits the ground) and \(x_- < 0\) (the negative horizontal distance behind the launch platform where the parabola intersects the ground plane).

The negative intercept \(x_-\) marks the virtual ground launch site: if fired from \(x = x_-\) at angle \(\theta_{\text{ground}} = \arctan(v_{y,\text{ground}} / v_{x,0})\), the projectile would seamlessly crest the cliff at \((0, h)\) with angle \(\theta\) and velocity \(v_0\).

## 1. Introduction: The Geometry of Pure Motion

**Kinematics** is the branch of classical mechanics that describes the motion of points, bodies, and systems of bodies without consideration of the forces that cause the motion. While **dynamics** asks *why* an object moves (relating forces to accelerations via Newton's laws or Lagrange's equations), kinematics asks *how* an object moves through space and time.

Kinematics provides the fundamental mathematical language of physics: the mapping between continuous temporal intervals and spatial trajectories.

```
                     THE KINEMATIC DERIVATIVE HIERARCHY
  ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │  Position r(t)                                                          │
  │    │                                                                    │
  │    ▼ d/dt                                                               │
  │  Velocity v(t) = ṙ(t) = dr/dt                                           │
  │    │                                                                    │
  │    ▼ d/dt                                                               │
  │  Acceleration a(t) = v̇(t) = r̈(t) = d²r/dt²                             │
  │    │                                                                    │
  │    ▼ d/dt                                                               │
  │  Jerk j(t) = ȧ(t) = r⃛(t) = d³r/dt³                                     │
  │    │                                                                    │
  │    ▼ d/dt                                                               │
  │  Snap / Jounce s(t) = j̇(t) = r⃜(t) = d⁴r/dt⁴                            │
  └─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

In modern high-speed railway design, elevator engineering, and aerospace trajectory synthesis, higher derivatives such as **jerk** ($j = \dot{a}$) and **snap** ($s = \ddot{a}$) are critical: discontinuous acceleration produces infinite jerk, inducing severe mechanical fatigue and biological whiplash in human passengers.

## 2. Mathematical Foundations: The Calculus of Motion

Let $\mathbf{r}(t) \in \mathbb{R}^3$ denote the position vector of a particle relative to a chosen inertial Cartesian coordinate origin.

### Differential Definitions

1. **Displacement Vector:** The net vector change in position over a discrete time interval $[t_1, t_2]$:
   $$\Delta \mathbf{r} = \mathbf{r}(t_2) - \mathbf{r}(t_1) \tag{1}$$
   *(Note the distinction between vector displacement $|\Delta \mathbf{r}|$ and total scalar path length $s = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} |\mathbf{v}(t)|\,dt$.)*
2. **Instantaneous Velocity:** The time derivative of position:
   $$\mathbf{v}(t) = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{\mathbf{r}(t + \Delta t) - \mathbf{r}(t)}{\Delta t} = \frac{d\mathbf{r}}{dt} = \dot{\mathbf{r}}(t) \tag{2}$$
   Velocity is always strictly tangent to the particle's spatial trajectory.
3. **Instantaneous Acceleration:** The time derivative of velocity:
   $$\mathbf{a}(t) = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{\mathbf{v}(t + \Delta t) - \mathbf{v}(t)}{\Delta t} = \frac{d\mathbf{v}}{dt} = \frac{d^2\mathbf{r}}{dt^2} = \ddot{\mathbf{r}}(t) \tag{3}$$

In curvilinear 3D motion, acceleration decomposes into tangential and normal components:

$$\mathbf{a}(t) = a_t \hat{\mathbf{T}} + a_n \hat{\mathbf{N}} = \frac{dv}{dt} \hat{\mathbf{T}} + \frac{v^2}{\rho_c} \hat{\mathbf{N}} \tag{4}$$

Where $\hat{\mathbf{T}}$ is the unit tangent vector, $\hat{\mathbf{N}}$ is the principal unit normal vector, $v = |\mathbf{v}|$ is scalar speed, and $\rho_c$ is the local radius of curvature of the path.

## 3. Constant Acceleration Kinematics (The SUVAT Equations)

When the acceleration vector is constant in magnitude and direction ($\mathbf{a}(t) = \mathbf{a}_0 = \text{const}$), the equations of motion can be integrated in closed form.

### Systematic Derivation via Definite Integration

1. **Velocity from Acceleration:**
   $$\int_{\mathbf{v}_0}^{\mathbf{v}(t)} d\mathbf{v}' = \int_0^t \mathbf{a}_0 \, dt' \implies \mathbf{v}(t) = \mathbf{v}_0 + \mathbf{a}_0 t \tag{5}$$
2. **Position from Velocity:**
   $$\int_{\mathbf{r}_0}^{\mathbf{r}(t)} d\mathbf{r}' = \int_0^t \left( \mathbf{v}_0 + \mathbf{a}_0 t' \right) dt' \implies \mathbf{r}(t) = \mathbf{r}_0 + \mathbf{v}_0 t + \frac{1}{2} \mathbf{a}_0 t^2 \tag{6}$$
3. **Eliminating Time (The Work-Energy Kinematic Relation):**
   In 1D along coordinate $x$: $a = \frac{dv}{dt} = \frac{dv}{dx} \frac{dx}{dt} = v \frac{dv}{dx}$.
   $$\int_{x_0}^{x} a_0 \, dx' = \int_{v_0}^{v} v' \, dv' \implies a_0 (x - x_0) = \frac{1}{2} v^2 - \frac{1}{2} v_0^2$$
   $$v^2 = v_0^2 + 2 a_0 (x - x_0) \tag{7}$$
4. **Average Velocity Representation:**
   Because velocity changes linearly with time under constant acceleration:
   $$\bar{v} = \frac{v_0 + v(t)}{2} \implies x(t) - x_0 = \left( \frac{v_0 + v(t)}{2} \right) t \tag{8}$$

```
                   THE SUVAT SYSTEM AT A GLANCE
  ┌────────────────────────────────┬──────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │ Equation                       │ Missing Variable                         │
  ├────────────────────────────────┼──────────────────────────────────────────┤
  │ v = v₀ + a t                   │ Displacement (x - x₀)                    │
  │ x = x₀ + v₀ t + ½ a t²         │ Final Velocity v                         │
  │ v² = v₀² + 2 a (x - x₀)        │ Time t                                   │
  │ x = x₀ + ½ (v₀ + v) t          │ Acceleration a                           │
  │ x = x₀ + v t - ½ a t²          │ Initial Velocity v₀                      │
  └────────────────────────────────┴──────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

## 4. One-Dimensional Free Fall Under Gravity

Consider vertical motion near the surface of the Earth. We erect a coordinate system where the vertical axis $y$ points upward ($\hat{\mathbf{j}}$), so gravitational acceleration points downward:

$$\mathbf{a} = -g \hat{\mathbf{j}}, \quad g \approx 9.80665\text{ m/s}^2 \tag{9}$$

The scalar equations of motion become:

$$\begin{aligned}
v(t) &= v_0 - g t \\
y(t) &= y_0 + v_0 t - \frac{1}{2} g t^2 \\
v^2(y) &= v_0^2 - 2 g (y - y_0)
\end{aligned} \tag{10}$$

### Key Kinematic Milestones of Free Fall

```
                         FREE FALL PARABOLIC ARC
           y ▲
             │                     Peak: (t_apex, y_max)
             │                          v = 0
             │                           ●
             │                       /       \
             │                      /         \
        y₀ ──┼───────●             /           \
             │    (t=0, v₀)       /             \
             │                   /               \
             │                  /                 \
         0 ──┴─────────────────●───────────────────●────────► t
                              t_neg < 0           t_land > 0
```

1. **Time to Reach Apogee (Peak Height):**
   Setting $v(t_{\text{apex}}) = 0$:
   $$0 = v_0 - g t_{\text{apex}} \implies t_{\text{apex}} = \frac{v_0}{g} \tag{11}$$
2. **Maximum Altitude ($y_{\text{max}}$):**
   Substituting $t_{\text{apex}}$ into position equation (10):
   $$y_{\text{max}} = y_0 + v_0 \left( \frac{v_0}{g} \right) - \frac{1}{2} g \left( \frac{v_0}{g} \right)^2 = y_0 + \frac{v_0^2}{2 g} \tag{12}$$
3. **Flight Symmetry for Ground-to-Ground ($y_0 = 0$):**
   Setting $y(t) = 0 \implies t(v_0 - \frac{1}{2} g t) = 0$.
   The landing time is exactly double the ascent time:
   $$t_{\text{land}} = 2 t_{\text{apex}} = \frac{2 v_0}{g} \tag{13}$$
   The impact velocity is $v(t_{\text{land}}) = v_0 - g(2v_0/g) = -v_0$. The ascent and descent trajectories are exact mirror images in time.

## 5. Deconstructing the Negative Time Axis Intercept

Now consider the general case: an experimenter stands atop a cliff of height $h > 0$ ($y_0 = h$) and throws a stone vertically upward with initial velocity $v_0 > 0$ at time $t = 0$.

We seek the time $t$ when the stone strikes the ground at the base of the cliff ($y = 0$):

$$0 = h + v_0 t - \frac{1}{2} g t^2 \iff \frac{1}{2} g t^2 - v_0 t - h = 0 \tag{14}$$

Applying the quadratic formula:

$$t = \frac{v_0 \pm \sqrt{v_0^2 - 4\left(\frac{1}{2}g\right)(-h)}}{2\left(\frac{1}{2}g\right)} = \frac{v_0 \pm \sqrt{v_0^2 + 2 g h}}{g} \tag{15}$$

Because $h > 0$ and $g > 0$, the discriminant $\sqrt{v_0^2 + 2 g h}$ is strictly greater than $v_0$. This guarantees **two real roots of opposite algebraic sign**:

$$\begin{aligned}
t_+ &= \frac{v_0 + \sqrt{v_0^2 + 2 g h}}{g} > 0 \quad (\text{Future landing time}) \\
t_- &= \frac{v_0 - \sqrt{v_0^2 + 2 g h}}{g} < 0 \quad (\text{Past negative time intercept})
\end{aligned} \tag{16}$$

In elementary textbooks, students are almost universally instructed:

> *"Discard $t_-$ because time cannot be negative; it is an unphysical extraneous mathematical root."*

**This dismissal is fundamentally mistaken.** Mathematics does not produce "meaningless artifacts" by accident. The quadratic equation (14) does not know that a human hand released the stone at $t = 0$. The differential equation $\ddot{y} = -g$ governs a smooth, unbroken parabola extending across the entire real number line $t \in (-\infty, +\infty)$.

```
                    THE VIRTUAL CONTINUATION PARABOLA
        y ▲
          │                     Apex (t_apex, y_max)
          │                          ●
          │                       /     \
          │                      /       \
     h ───┼───────────●         /         \
          │         (t=0, v₀)  /           \
          │       (Actual Launch)           \  (Actual Descent)
          │                   /               \
          │                  /                 \
        0 ┼─────────────────●───────────────────●────────► t
          │               t₋ < 0               t₊ > 0
          │        (Virtual Past Launch)   (Actual Landing)
          │
          │◄──── Δt_virtual ────►│
```

### Physical Interpretation 1: The Virtual Past Launch (Counterfactual Trajectory)

What does $t_- < 0$ represent?

**Physical Reality:** $t_-$ is the precise time in the past at which an observer at ground level ($y = 0$) would have had to launch an identical stone upward so that it would arrive at height $y = h$ at exactly $t = 0$ with velocity exactly equal to $+v_0$.

Let us verify this mathematically:

1. Suppose at time $t = t_- < 0$, a stone is launched from $y = 0$ with unknown initial velocity $v_{\text{ground}}$.
2. Its trajectory equation for all $t$ is:
   $$y_{\text{virtual}}(t) = 0 + v_{\text{ground}} (t - t_-) - \frac{1}{2} g (t - t_-)^2 \tag{17}$$
3. What is the velocity of this stone at $t = t_-$?
   By conservation of energy between $y = 0$ and $y = h$:
   $$\frac{1}{2} m v_{\text{ground}}^2 = \frac{1}{2} m v_0^2 + m g h \implies v_{\text{ground}} = \sqrt{v_0^2 + 2 g h} \tag{18}$$
4. Now evaluate its state at $t = 0$:
   $$v_{\text{virtual}}(0) = v_{\text{ground}} - g(0 - t_-) = \sqrt{v_0^2 + 2 g h} + g t_-$$
   Substituting $t_- = \frac{v_0 - \sqrt{v_0^2 + 2gh}}{g}$:
   $$v_{\text{virtual}}(0) = \sqrt{v_0^2 + 2 g h} + g \left( \frac{v_0 - \sqrt{v_0^2 + 2 g h}}{g} \right) = v_0 \tag{19}$$
   The velocity at $t = 0$ matches $v_0$ **identically**.
5. Evaluate its position at $t = 0$:
   $$y_{\text{virtual}}(0) = v_{\text{ground}} (-t_-) - \frac{1}{2} g (-t_-)^2 = -v_{\text{ground}} t_- - \frac{1}{2} g t_-^2$$
   From the quadratic equation (14), since $t_-$ is an exact root of $\frac{1}{2} g t^2 - v_0 t - h = 0$:
   $$-\frac{1}{2} g t_-^2 + v_0 t_- + h = 0 \implies y_{\text{virtual}}(0) = h \tag{20}$$

**Conclusion:** The physical trajectory of the stone for $t \ge 0$ is *completely indistinguishable* from the trajectory of a stone launched from the ground at $t = t_-$. The negative root $t_-$ is the **origin time of the smooth kinematic history that produces the initial state $(y_0, v_0)$ at $t = 0$**.

---

### Physical Interpretation 2: Time-Reversal Invariance ($T$-Symmetry)

Classical Newtonian gravitation without dissipation is invariant under time reversal:

$$t \to -t, \quad \frac{d^2 y}{d(-t)^2} = \frac{d^2 y}{dt^2} = -g \tag{21}$$

If you record a movie of the stone moving along its trajectory and run the film backward in time:

* An object landing at $t = t_+$ with downward velocity $-v_{\text{impact}}$ becomes an object launched upward from the ground at $t = -t_+$ with velocity $+v_{\text{impact}}$.
* The actual launch at $t = 0$ from height $h$ with upward velocity $+v_0$ becomes, in reverse, an object passing through $y = h$ at $t = 0$ with downward velocity $-v_0$.
* Where did this backward-in-time trajectory originate? It originated by hitting the ground at $t = |t_-| = -t_- > 0$.

The negative root $t_-$ is the time-reversal mirror of the complementary kinematic branch.

---

### Physical Interpretation 3: The Interceptor / Matching Velocity Tactic

In engineering ballistics, artillery fire control, and aerospace rendezvous, the negative root answers a fundamental practical question:

> *"If a launcher is located at ground level ($y=0$), at what time $t_{\text{launch}}$ and with what launch speed $v_L$ must an interceptor projectile be fired to match the state $(y=h, v=v_0)$ of a target at $t = 0$?"*

```
              INTERCEPTOR TIME-DELAY MATCHING TAXONOMY
  ┌─────────────────────────┬──────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │ Physical Requirement    │ Operational Mathematical Mapping                 │
  ├─────────────────────────┼──────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
  │ Target Position at t=0  │ y(0) = h                                         │
  │ Target Velocity at t=0  │ v(0) = +v₀                                       │
  │ Required Launch Time    │ t_launch = t₋ = (v₀ - √(v₀² + 2gh)) / g < 0      │
  │ Time Delay Before t=0   │ Δt_advance = |t₋| = (√(v₀² + 2gh) - v₀) / g      │
  │ Required Launch Speed   │ v_launch = √(v₀² + 2gh)                          │
  │ Subsequent Landing Time │ t_land = t₊ = (v₀ + √(v₀² + 2gh)) / g > 0        │
  │ Total Flight Duration   │ T_total = t₊ - t₋ = (2 √(v₀² + 2gh)) / g         │
  └─────────────────────────┴──────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

Notice the remarkable elegance of the total flight duration:

$$\Delta T = t_+ - t_- = \frac{v_0 + \sqrt{v_0^2 + 2gh}}{g} - \frac{v_0 - \sqrt{v_0^2 + 2gh}}{g} = \frac{2\sqrt{v_0^2 + 2gh}}{g} = \frac{2 v_{\text{ground}}}{g} \tag{22}$$

The difference between the two roots ($t_+ - t_-$) is **identically equal to the total round-trip flight time of a projectile launched from ground level with speed $v_{\text{ground}}$**!

## 6. Worked Numerical Example: The Cliff-Drop Benchmark

Let a student stand atop a building of height $h = 45\text{ m}$. At time $t = 0$, the student throws a ball vertically upward with initial speed $v_0 = 20\text{ m/s}$. Take $g = 10\text{ m/s}^2$ for clean analytical arithmetic.

```
                      WORKED NUMERICAL TRAJECTORY
          y (meters) ▲
                     │                Peak: t = 2 s, y = 65 m
                65 ──┼──────────────────────●
                     │                     / \
                     │                    /   \
                45 ──┼────────● (t=0, v=+20)   \
                     │       /                  \
                     │      /                    \
                     │     /                      \
                 0 ──┴────●────────────────────────●─────► t (seconds)
                       t = -1.61 s               t = +5.61 s
                       v = +36.06 m/s            v = -36.06 m/s
```

1. **Calculate the Discriminant:**
   $$\sqrt{v_0^2 + 2 g h} = \sqrt{20^2 + 2(10)(45)} = \sqrt{400 + 900} = \sqrt{1300} \approx 36.0555\text{ m/s}$$
2. **Compute Both Roots:**
   $$t_+ = \frac{20 + 36.0555}{10} = \frac{56.0555}{10} \approx +5.61\text{ s}$$
   $$t_- = \frac{20 - 36.0555}{10} = \frac{-16.0555}{10} \approx -1.61\text{ s}$$
3. **Physical Interpretation:**
   * The ball ascends to its peak at $t_{\text{apex}} = 20/10 = 2.0\text{ s}$, reaching altitude $y_{\text{max}} = 45 + \frac{20^2}{2(10)} = 65\text{ m}$.
   * It falls back past the roof line ($y = 45\text{ m}$) at $t = 4.0\text{ s}$ with downward velocity $v = -20\text{ m/s}$.
   * It strikes the ground at $t = +5.61\text{ s}$ with impact velocity:
     $$v(5.61) = 20 - 10(5.6055) = -36.06\text{ m/s}$$
   * **The Negative Root ($t_- = -1.61\text{ s}$):**
     If an archer standing on the ground had fired an identical arrow upward $1.61$ seconds *before* the student threw the ball (at $t = -1.61\text{ s}$) with an initial speed of $36.06\text{ m/s}$:
     - At $t = 0$, the arrow would reach height $y = 45\text{ m}$.
     - At $t = 0$, the arrow's velocity would have decelerated to exactly $36.06 - 10(1.61) = +20.0\text{ m/s}$.
     - The arrow and the ball would merge into the exact same trajectory, landing at the exact same spot at $t = +5.61\text{ s}$.

## 7. Two-Dimensional Kinematic Projectiles and Vector Parabolae

The 1D analysis generalizes directly to 2D projectile motion in a vertical plane $(x, y)$.

Let a projectile be launched from $(x_0, y_0) = (0, h)$ with speed $v_0$ at angle $\theta$ above horizontal:

$$\mathbf{v}_0 = v_0 \cos \theta \, \hat{\mathbf{i}} + v_0 \sin \theta \, \hat{\mathbf{j}} \tag{23}$$

Because gravity acts strictly along the vertical axis ($\mathbf{a} = -g \hat{\mathbf{j}}$), the horizontal and vertical motions are **completely decoupled**:

$$\begin{aligned}
x(t) &= (v_0 \cos \theta) t \\
y(t) &= h + (v_0 \sin \theta) t - \frac{1}{2} g t^2
\end{aligned} \tag{24}$$

Eliminating $t = \frac{x}{v_0 \cos \theta}$ yields the spatial Cartesian trajectory:

$$y(x) = h + (\tan \theta) x - \frac{g}{2 v_0^2 \cos^2 \theta} x^2 \tag{25}$$

```
                   2D PROJECTILE WITH NEGATIVE X-INTERCEPT
         y ▲
           │                     Apex
           │                       ●
           │                    /     \
       h ──┼──────────● (t=0)  /       \
           │         /        /         \
           │        /        /           \
         0 ┼───────●────────┼─────────────●───────► x
                 x₋ < 0     0            x₊ > 0
```

Just as in the 1D case, setting $y(x) = 0$ yields two spatial roots: $x_+ > 0$ (the forward range where the projectile hits the ground) and $x_- < 0$ (the negative horizontal distance behind the launch platform where the parabola intersects the ground plane).

The negative intercept $x_-$ marks the **virtual ground launch site**: if fired from $x = x_-$ at angle $\theta_{\text{ground}} = \arctan(v_{y,\text{ground}} / v_{x,0})$, the projectile would seamlessly crest the cliff at $(0, h)$ with angle $\theta$ and velocity $v_0$.
