Designed to be accessible from first principles, it clarifies the subtle boundaries between abstract spaces, physical coordinates, and moving observers: distinguishing points from displacement vectors, formulating the geometry of planes and orthogonal projection operators, exploring active versus passive rotations in \(\mathrm{SO}(3)\), and analyzing the kinematics of rotating reference frames.

1. Introduction: Disentangling Geometry, Numbers, and Observers

In elementary science and engineering, terms like space, coordinate system, and frame of reference are frequently conflated as mere synonyms for a grid of numbers drawn on a page.

However, in physics and mathematics, these concepts belong to distinctly different levels of abstraction: 1. The Space (\(\mathbb{A}^n\) or \(\mathbb{E}^n\)): The geometric stage itself. A physical room exists whether or not anyone chooses to draw axes across the floor. 2. The Plane or Subspace (\(\mathcal{P}\)): A flat two-dimensional geometric object living inside that space, characterized by normal vectors and linear constraints. 3. The Projection Operator (\(\mathbf{P}\)): The geometric action of collapsing higher-dimensional points onto lower-dimensional flats (casting shadows). 4. The Coordinate System (\(\phi\)): An artificial linguistic tool—a dictionary that translates geometric points into tuples of numbers (addresses). 5. The Orientation and Reference Frame (\(\mathbf{R}(t)\)): An actual physical observer equipped with a clock and an orientation (a set of directional rulers), who may be moving, accelerating, or rotating relative to the space.

Failing to distinguish these concepts causes deep confusion—such as confusing an artificial coordinate singularity (like the North Pole on a latitude-longitude map) with a physical tear in space, or mistaking a fictitious inertia force for a real physical interaction.

2. The Hierarchy of Spaces: Points vs. Vectors

Before drawing coordinate axes, what kind of space do we live in?

2.1 The Affine Space: Positions Are Not Vectors

In elementary physics, instructors often say “the position vector \(\mathbf{r}\) of a particle.” In rigorous mathematics, positions are points, not vectors: * An Affine Space \(\mathbb{A}^n\) is a collection of points with no privileged origin. All locations in empty space are created equal; the universe has no golden center \((0, 0, 0)\) marked by nature. * A Vector Space \(V\), by contrast, requires an absolute zero vector \(\mathbf{0}\). Vectors represent displacements (movements from one place to another), not places.

The Grammar of Affine Arithmetic:

  1. Subtracting two points yields a vector: \[Q - P = \mathbf{v} \in V\] The displacement from Toronto to Montreal is a vector (distance and direction).
  2. Adding a vector to a point yields a new point: \[P + \mathbf{v} = Q \in \mathbb{A}^n\] Starting at Toronto and driving along the displacement vector brings you to Montreal.
  3. Adding two points is mathematically meaningless: \[P + Q = \text{Undefined!}\] What does “Toronto \(+\) Montreal” mean? It has no physical or mathematical sense.
  4. Affine combinations (weighted averages) are meaningful: \[(1 - t)P + tQ \in \mathbb{A}^n \quad (t \in [0, 1])\] This traces the straight line segment connecting point \(P\) to point \(Q\).

2.2 From Affine Space to Euclidean Space

To turn an affine space into familiar Euclidean space \(\mathbb{E}^n\), we equip its associated vector displacement space \(V\) with an inner product (the dot product \(\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}\)): * Length / Norm: \(\|\mathbf{v}\| = \sqrt{\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{v}}\). * Distance: \(d(P, Q) = \|Q - P\|\). * Angle: \(\cos\theta = \frac{\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{u}\| \|\mathbf{v}\|}\).

Only after we arbitrarily select an anchor point \(O \in \mathbb{A}^n\) as our origin does every point \(P\) inherit a unique “position vector” \(\mathbf{r}_P = P - O\).

3. Planes and Hyperplanes in 3D Space

A plane is a flat, two-dimensional affine subspace living inside three-dimensional space \(\mathbb{R}^3\).

3.1 The Point-Normal Form

The cleanest, most powerful way to define a plane does not use \(x, y, z\) coordinates; it uses geometry: 1. A known base point \(\mathbf{p}_0\) on the plane. 2. A non-zero normal vector \(\mathbf{n} = (a, b, c)\) perpendicular to the plane.

A variable point \(\mathbf{r} = (x, y, z)\) lies on the plane if and only if the displacement vector \(\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{p}_0\) is perpendicular to \(\mathbf{n}\):

\[\mathbf{n} \cdot (\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{p}_0) = 0 \tag{1}\]

Expanding the dot product in components:

\[a(x - x_0) + b(y - y_0) + c(z - z_0) = 0 \implies ax + by + cz = d \tag{2}\]

where \(d = \mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{p}_0 = ax_0 + by_0 + cz_0\).

Physical Significance:
The coefficients \((a, b, c)\) in the Cartesian equation \(ax + by + cz = d\) are literally the direction numbers of the stick sticking perpendicular out of the plane!

3.2 The Parametric Form (Two Direction Vectors)

Alternatively, a plane can be generated by starting at point \(\mathbf{p}_0\) and sweeping out all linear combinations of two non-parallel direction vectors \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\) parallel to the surface:

\[\mathbf{r}(s, t) = \mathbf{p}_0 + s\mathbf{u} + t\mathbf{v}, \qquad (s, t \in \mathbb{R}) \tag{3}\]

The normal vector is recovered instantly via the cross product:

\[\mathbf{n} = \mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v} \tag{4}\]

3.3 Distance from an Arbitrary Point to a Plane

Given an arbitrary point \(Q = (x_1, y_1, z_1)\) outside the plane, the perpendicular distance \(D\) from \(Q\) to the plane is the scalar projection of the displacement \(Q - \mathbf{p}_0\) onto the unit normal vector \(\mathbf{\hat{n}} = \frac{\mathbf{n}}{\|\mathbf{n}\|}\):

\[D = |(Q - \mathbf{p}_0) \cdot \mathbf{\hat{n}}| = \frac{|a x_1 + b y_1 + c z_1 - d|}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2 + c^2}} \tag{5}\]

4. Projections: Casting Geometric Shadows

A projection is an idempotent geometric operation that maps points or vectors from a higher-dimensional space into a lower-dimensional subspace.

4.1 Orthogonal Projection of a Vector onto a Line

Given an arbitrary vector \(\mathbf{a}\) and a directional axis defined by non-zero vector \(\mathbf{b}\): * The component of \(\mathbf{a}\) parallel to \(\mathbf{b}\) is its orthogonal projection: \[\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{a}) = \mathbf{a}_\parallel = \left( \frac{\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b}}{\|\mathbf{b}\|^2} \right) \mathbf{b} \tag{6}\] * The component of \(\mathbf{a}\) perpendicular to \(\mathbf{b}\) is the orthogonal rejection: \[\mathbf{a}_\perp = \mathbf{a} - \mathbf{a}_\parallel = \mathbf{a} - \left( \frac{\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b}}{\|\mathbf{b}\|^2} \right) \mathbf{b} \tag{7}\]

By construction, \(\mathbf{a}_\parallel \cdot \mathbf{a}_\perp = 0\), forming a right triangle where \(\|\mathbf{a}\|^2 = \|\mathbf{a}_\parallel\|^2 + \|\mathbf{a}_\perp\|^2\) (the Pythagorean Theorem).

4.2 Orthogonal Projection onto a Plane

If a plane passes through the origin with unit normal \(\mathbf{\hat{n}}\), any vector \(\mathbf{v}\) can be decomposed into a part perpendicular to the plane and a part lying inside the plane:

\[\mathbf{v}_\perp = (\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{\hat{n}})\mathbf{\hat{n}}\]

\[\mathbf{v}_\parallel = \mathbf{v} - \mathbf{v}_\perp = \mathbf{v} - (\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{\hat{n}})\mathbf{\hat{n}} \tag{8}\]

In matrix operator form, using the outer product dyad \(\mathbf{\hat{n}}\mathbf{\hat{n}}^T\):

\[\mathbf{v}_\parallel = (\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{\hat{n}}\mathbf{\hat{n}}^T) \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{P}_{\text{plane}} \mathbf{v} \tag{9}\]

4.3 General Subspace Projection Matrix

Let subspace \(\mathcal{S} \subset \mathbb{R}^n\) be spanned by the linearly independent columns of matrix \(\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times k}\). The orthogonal projection matrix that projects any vector in \(\mathbb{R}^n\) onto \(\mathcal{S}\) is:

\[\mathbf{P} = \mathbf{A}(\mathbf{A}^T \mathbf{A})^{-1} \mathbf{A}^T \tag{10}\]

The Two Universal Axioms of Orthogonal Projections:

  1. Idempotence (Projecting Twice Changes Nothing): \[\mathbf{P}^2 = \mathbf{P}\] Once a vector’s shadow hits the floor, projecting the shadow again leaves it unchanged.
  2. Symmetry (Self-Adjointness): \[\mathbf{P}^T = \mathbf{P}\] Ensures that the error vector \(\mathbf{e} = (\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{P})\mathbf{x}\) is strictly orthogonal to the subspace.
Show Python code generating the 3D Projection Plot 
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

fig = plt.figure(figsize=(8.5, 5.5), dpi=180)
ax = fig.add_subplot(1, 1, 1, projection='3d')

# Plane: 2x + y + 2z = 0 (Normal n = [2, 1, 2])
n = np.array([2.0, 1.0, 2.0])
n_unit = n / np.linalg.norm(n)

# Surface mesh
xx, yy = np.meshgrid(np.linspace(-2.2, 2.2, 25), np.linspace(-2.2, 2.2, 25))
zz = (-n[0]*xx - n[1]*yy) / n[2]

ax.plot_surface(xx, yy, zz, alpha=0.30, color='#38bdf8', edgecolor='none')

# Arbitrary vector v
v = np.array([1.5, 2.2, 2.8])

# Projection calculations
v_perp = np.dot(v, n_unit) * n_unit
v_proj = v - v_perp

# Plot vectors as 3D quivers
ax.quiver(0, 0, 0, n_unit[0]*1.8, n_unit[1]*1.8, n_unit[2]*1.8, 
          color='#dc2626', lw=2.4, arrow_length_ratio=0.15, label=r'Normal Vector $\mathbf{\hat{n}}$')
ax.quiver(0, 0, 0, v[0], v[1], v[2], 
          color='#2563eb', lw=2.6, arrow_length_ratio=0.12, label=r'Original Vector $\mathbf{v}$')
ax.quiver(0, 0, 0, v_proj[0], v_proj[1], v_proj[2], 
          color='#059669', lw=2.6, arrow_length_ratio=0.12, label=r'Projected Vector $\mathbf{v}_\parallel$')

# Dashed orthogonal error line
ax.plot([v[0], v_proj[0]], [v[1], v_proj[1]], [v[2], v_proj[2]], 
        color='#7c3aed', linestyle='--', lw=2.0, label=r'Normal Component $\mathbf{v}_\perp$')

ax.set_title("Orthogonal Projection of a Vector onto a Plane in 3D", fontsize=11, fontweight='bold', pad=12)
ax.set_xlabel("X Axis", fontsize=9.5)
ax.set_ylabel("Y Axis", fontsize=9.5)
ax.set_zlabel("Z Axis", fontsize=9.5)
ax.set_xlim(-2.5, 2.5)
ax.set_ylim(-2.5, 2.5)
ax.set_zlim(-2.5, 3.2)
ax.legend(frameon=True, fontsize=8.8, loc='upper left')

plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

5. Coordinate Systems: How We Map Reality

A coordinate system is a smooth, invertible mathematical function (a chart \(\phi\)) that maps open patches of physical space into tuples of real numbers in \(\mathbb{R}^n\).

5.1 Canonical 3D Systems

Different coordinate grids are chosen to mirror the natural physical symmetry of a problem:

Coordinate System Coordinates Coordinate Transformations Position Vector \(\mathbf{r}\)
Cartesian \((x, y, z)\) \(x, y, z \in (-\infty, \infty)\) \(x\mathbf{e}_x + y\mathbf{e}_y + z\mathbf{e}_z\)
Cylindrical \((\rho, \phi, z)\) \(x = \rho\cos\phi,\; y = \rho\sin\phi,\; z = z\) \(\rho\mathbf{e}_\rho + z\mathbf{e}_z\)
Spherical \((r, \theta, \phi)\) \(x = r\sin\theta\cos\phi,\; y = r\sin\theta\sin\phi,\; z = r\cos\theta\) \(r\mathbf{e}_r\)

5.2 Moving Basis Vectors in Curvilinear Systems

In Cartesian space, the basis vectors \(\mathbf{e}_x, \mathbf{e}_y, \mathbf{e}_z\) are constant everywhere.

In curvilinear systems (like polar or spherical coordinates), the basis vectors depend on position: * At the equator ($ heta = /2, = 0$), the radial unit vector \(\mathbf{e}_r\) points along the \(+X\) axis. * At the North Pole ($ heta = 0$), \(\mathbf{e}_r\) points along \(+Z\).

When differentiating vectors in curvilinear coordinates, one must differentiate both the scalar components and the moving basis vectors:

\[\frac{d\mathbf{r}}{dt} = \frac{d}{dt}(r\mathbf{e}_r) = \dot{r}\mathbf{e}_r + r\dot{\mathbf{e}}_r = \dot{r}\mathbf{e}_r + r\dot{\theta}\mathbf{e}_\theta + r\dot{\phi}\sin\theta\mathbf{e}_\phi \tag{11}\]

This position-dependence of basis vectors is what produces centrifugal and Coriolis kinematic acceleration terms in polar calculus!

6. Orientations, Rotations, and the Special Orthogonal Group \(\mathrm{SO}(3)\)

What is a rotation? Mathematically, a rotation is an orientation-preserving isometric linear transformation: * It leaves the origin fixed. * It preserves lengths: \(\|\mathbf{R}\mathbf{v}\| = \|\mathbf{v}\|\). * It preserves dot products and angles: \((\mathbf{R}\mathbf{u}) \cdot (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{v}) = \mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}\). * It preserves handedness (orientation): \(\det(\mathbf{R}) = +1\) (reflections have \(\det = -1\)).

The set of all such \(3 \times 3\) matrices forms the Special Orthogonal Group \(\mathrm{SO}(3)\), satisfying:

\[\mathbf{R}^T \mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I}, \qquad \det(\mathbf{R}) = +1 \tag{12}\]

6.1 Euler’s Rotation Theorem

Euler’s Theorem (1776):
Every arbitrary rotation \(\mathbf{R} \in \mathrm{SO}(3)\) of a three-dimensional body fixes a unique one-dimensional line passing through the origin. That line is the axis of rotation.

Any 3D rotation can be parameterized by an axis unit vector \(\mathbf{\hat{u}}\) and a rotation angle \(\theta\) around that axis (Rodrigues’ Rotation Formula):

\[\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{\hat{u}}, \theta) = \mathbf{I} + (\sin\theta) \mathbf{K} + (1 - \cos\theta) \mathbf{K}^2 \tag{13}\]

where \(\mathbf{K}\) is the skew-symmetric cross-product matrix of \(\mathbf{\hat{u}}\).

6.2 Active vs. Passive Transformations (Alibi vs. Alias)

One of the most frequent sources of sign errors in physics is confusing active and passive rotations:

  1. Active Rotation (Alibi — “Elsewhere”):
    The coordinate axes remain stationary, while the physical object or vector is rotated to a new physical location: \[\mathbf{v}' = \mathbf{R} \mathbf{v}\]
  2. Passive Rotation (Alias — “By another name”):
    The physical object remains completely stationary in space, but the observer rotates their coordinate measuring axes: \[\mathbf{v}'_{\text{coords}} = \mathbf{R}^T \mathbf{v}_{\text{coords}} = \mathbf{R}^{-1} \mathbf{v}_{\text{coords}}\]

Rule of Thumb:
Rotating an object counterclockwise by \(+45^\circ\) relative to fixed axes produces the exact same coordinate numbers as leaving the object stationary and rotating your coordinate axes clockwise by \(-45^\circ\)!

Show Python code generating the Active vs Passive Diagram 
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

fig, (ax1, ax2) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(10, 4.8), dpi=180)

theta = np.radians(45)
R_act = np.array([[np.cos(theta), -np.sin(theta)],
                  [np.sin(theta),  np.cos(theta)]])

v = np.array([2.0, 0.5])
v_rot = R_act @ v

# --- Panel 1: Active Rotation ---
ax1.axhline(0, color='gray', linestyle=':', lw=1.2)
ax1.axvline(0, color='gray', linestyle=':', lw=1.2)

ax1.quiver(0, 0, v[0], v[1], angles='xy', scale_units='xy', scale=1, 
           color='#2563eb', lw=2.5, label=r'Original Vector $\mathbf{v}$')
ax1.quiver(0, 0, v_rot[0], v_rot[1], angles='xy', scale_units='xy', scale=1, 
           color='#dc2626', lw=2.5, label=r'Active Rotated $\mathbf{v}^\prime = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{v}$')

arc_angles = np.linspace(np.arctan2(v[1], v[0]), np.arctan2(v_rot[1], v_rot[0]), 30)
ax1.plot(1.2 * np.cos(arc_angles), 1.2 * np.sin(arc_angles), color='#7c3aed', lw=1.8, linestyle='--')
ax1.text(1.35 * np.cos(arc_angles[15]), 1.35 * np.sin(arc_angles[15]), r'$+45^\circ$', color='#7c3aed', fontweight='bold')

ax1.set_xlim(-1.5, 2.8)
ax1.set_ylim(-0.5, 2.5)
ax1.set_aspect('equal')
ax1.set_title("Active Rotation (Object Turns in Fixed Space)", fontsize=10.5, fontweight='bold')
ax1.legend(frameon=True, fontsize=8.5, loc='upper left')
ax1.grid(True, linestyle='--', alpha=0.4)

# --- Panel 2: Passive Rotation ---
ax2.axhline(0, color='gray', linestyle=':', lw=1.2, label='Old Frame ($X, Y$)')
ax2.axvline(0, color='gray', linestyle=':', lw=1.2)

# Rotated axes
axis_x = R_act @ np.array([2.5, 0])
axis_y = R_act @ np.array([0, 2.5])
ax2.plot([-axis_x[0], axis_x[0]], [-axis_x[1], axis_x[1]], color='#d97706', lw=1.8, linestyle='--', label=r'Rotated Frame ($X^\prime, Y^\prime$)')
ax2.plot([-axis_y[0], axis_y[0]], [-axis_y[1], axis_y[1]], color='#d97706', lw=1.8, linestyle='--')

ax2.quiver(0, 0, v[0], v[1], angles='xy', scale_units='xy', scale=1, 
           color='#2563eb', lw=2.8, label=r'Fixed Physical Vector $\mathbf{v}$')

ax2.set_xlim(-1.5, 2.8)
ax2.set_ylim(-0.5, 2.5)
ax2.set_aspect('equal')

7. Reference Frames and Moving Observers

What distinguishes a coordinate system from a reference frame? * A coordinate system is a mathematical choice of grid (Cartesian, polar, spherical). * A reference frame is a physical observer equipped with a clock, a state of motion (velocity \(\mathbf{v}\), acceleration \(\mathbf{a}\)), and an orientation triad.

You can use spherical coordinates inside a moving train, or Cartesian coordinates on a spinning merry-go-round.

7.1 Inertial vs. Non-Inertial Reference Frames

  1. Inertial Reference Frame: A frame in which Newton’s First Law holds true without external modifications: free bodies move in straight lines at constant velocity. Any two inertial frames are related by a Galilean transformation: \[\mathbf{r}' = \mathbf{r} - \mathbf{v}_0 t - \mathbf{r}_0, \qquad t' = t\]
  2. Non-Inertial (Accelerating / Rotating) Frame: An observer undergoing acceleration or rotation. In such frames, Newton’s Second Law fails unless we introduce apparent (fictitious) d’Alembert forces.

7.2 The Transport Theorem and Fictitious Forces

Let frame \(\mathcal{S}\) be inertial and frame \(\mathcal{S}'\) rotate with instantaneous angular velocity vector \(\boldsymbol{\omega}(t)\).

For any arbitrary physical vector \(\mathbf{A}(t)\), its time derivative in the inertial frame is related to its time derivative observed in the rotating frame by the fundamental Transport Theorem:

\[\left(\frac{d\mathbf{A}}{dt}\right)_{\text{inertial}} = \left(\frac{d\mathbf{A}}{dt}\right)_{\text{rotating}} + \boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{A} \tag{14}\]

Applying this twice to position vector \(\mathbf{r}(t)\) yields the acceleration observed by a rotating observer:

\[\mathbf{a}_{\text{inertial}} = \mathbf{a}_{\text{rot}} + 2(\boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{v}_{\text{rot}}) + \boldsymbol{\omega} \times (\boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{r}) + \dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}} \times \mathbf{r} \tag{15}\]

Multiplying by mass \(m\) and rearranging:

\[m\mathbf{a}_{\text{rot}} = \mathbf{F}_{\text{real}} + \mathbf{F}_{\text{Coriolis}} + \mathbf{F}_{\text{Centrifugal}} + \mathbf{F}_{\text{Euler}} \tag{16}\]

where the fictitious forces are: * Centrifugal Force: \(\mathbf{F}_{\text{cent}} = -m \boldsymbol{\omega} \times (\boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{r})\) (directed radially outward). * Coriolis Force: \(\mathbf{F}_{\text{cor}} = -2m (\boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{v}_{\text{rot}})\) (acts perpendicular to velocity, curving cyclones on Earth). * Euler Force: \(\mathbf{F}_{\text{euler}} = -m (\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}} \times \mathbf{r})\) (arises only when angular rotation rate changes).

## 2. The Hierarchy of Spaces: Points vs. Vectors

Before drawing coordinate axes, what kind of space do we live in?

### 2.1 The Affine Space: Positions Are Not Vectors
In elementary physics, instructors often say "the position vector $\mathbf{r}$ of a particle." In rigorous mathematics, **positions are points, not vectors**:
* An **Affine Space** $\mathbb{A}^n$ is a collection of points with **no privileged origin**. All locations in empty space are created equal; the universe has no golden center $(0, 0, 0)$ marked by nature.
* A **Vector Space** $V$, by contrast, requires an absolute zero vector $\mathbf{0}$. Vectors represent **displacements** (movements from one place to another), not places.

#### The Grammar of Affine Arithmetic:
1. **Subtracting two points yields a vector:**
   $$Q - P = \mathbf{v} \in V$$
   The displacement from Toronto to Montreal is a vector (distance and direction).
2. **Adding a vector to a point yields a new point:**
   $$P + \mathbf{v} = Q \in \mathbb{A}^n$$
   Starting at Toronto and driving along the displacement vector brings you to Montreal.
3. **Adding two points is mathematically meaningless:**
   $$P + Q = \text{Undefined!}$$
   What does "Toronto $+$ Montreal" mean? It has no physical or mathematical sense.
4. **Affine combinations (weighted averages) are meaningful:**
   $$(1 - t)P + tQ \in \mathbb{A}^n \quad (t \in [0, 1])$$
   This traces the straight line segment connecting point $P$ to point $Q$.

### 2.2 From Affine Space to Euclidean Space
To turn an affine space into familiar **Euclidean space** $\mathbb{E}^n$, we equip its associated vector displacement space $V$ with an **inner product** (the dot product $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}$):
* **Length / Norm:** $\|\mathbf{v}\| = \sqrt{\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{v}}$.
* **Distance:** $d(P, Q) = \|Q - P\|$.
* **Angle:** $\cos\theta = \frac{\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{u}\| \|\mathbf{v}\|}$.

Only after we arbitrarily select an anchor point $O \in \mathbb{A}^n$ as our **origin** does every point $P$ inherit a unique "position vector" $\mathbf{r}_P = P - O$.

## 3. Planes and Hyperplanes in 3D Space

A plane is a flat, two-dimensional affine subspace living inside three-dimensional space $\mathbb{R}^3$.

### 3.1 The Point-Normal Form
The cleanest, most powerful way to define a plane does not use $x, y, z$ coordinates; it uses geometry:
1. A known base point $\mathbf{p}_0$ on the plane.
2. A non-zero **normal vector** $\mathbf{n} = (a, b, c)$ perpendicular to the plane.

A variable point $\mathbf{r} = (x, y, z)$ lies on the plane if and only if the displacement vector $\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{p}_0$ is perpendicular to $\mathbf{n}$:

$$\mathbf{n} \cdot (\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{p}_0) = 0 \tag{1}$$

Expanding the dot product in components:

$$a(x - x_0) + b(y - y_0) + c(z - z_0) = 0 \implies ax + by + cz = d \tag{2}$$

where $d = \mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{p}_0 = ax_0 + by_0 + cz_0$.

> **Physical Significance:**  
> The coefficients $(a, b, c)$ in the Cartesian equation $ax + by + cz = d$ are literally the direction numbers of the stick sticking perpendicular out of the plane!

### 3.2 The Parametric Form (Two Direction Vectors)
Alternatively, a plane can be generated by starting at point $\mathbf{p}_0$ and sweeping out all linear combinations of two non-parallel direction vectors $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ parallel to the surface:

$$\mathbf{r}(s, t) = \mathbf{p}_0 + s\mathbf{u} + t\mathbf{v}, \qquad (s, t \in \mathbb{R}) \tag{3}$$

The normal vector is recovered instantly via the cross product:

$$\mathbf{n} = \mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v} \tag{4}$$

### 3.3 Distance from an Arbitrary Point to a Plane
Given an arbitrary point $Q = (x_1, y_1, z_1)$ outside the plane, the perpendicular distance $D$ from $Q$ to the plane is the scalar projection of the displacement $Q - \mathbf{p}_0$ onto the unit normal vector $\mathbf{\hat{n}} = \frac{\mathbf{n}}{\|\mathbf{n}\|}$:

$$D = |(Q - \mathbf{p}_0) \cdot \mathbf{\hat{n}}| = \frac{|a x_1 + b y_1 + c z_1 - d|}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2 + c^2}} \tag{5}$$

## 4. Projections: Casting Geometric Shadows

A projection is an idempotent geometric operation that maps points or vectors from a higher-dimensional space into a lower-dimensional subspace.

### 4.1 Orthogonal Projection of a Vector onto a Line
Given an arbitrary vector $\mathbf{a}$ and a directional axis defined by non-zero vector $\mathbf{b}$:
* The component of $\mathbf{a}$ parallel to $\mathbf{b}$ is its **orthogonal projection**:
  $$\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{a}) = \mathbf{a}_\parallel = \left( \frac{\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b}}{\|\mathbf{b}\|^2} \right) \mathbf{b} \tag{6}$$
* The component of $\mathbf{a}$ perpendicular to $\mathbf{b}$ is the **orthogonal rejection**:
  $$\mathbf{a}_\perp = \mathbf{a} - \mathbf{a}_\parallel = \mathbf{a} - \left( \frac{\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b}}{\|\mathbf{b}\|^2} \right) \mathbf{b} \tag{7}$$

By construction, $\mathbf{a}_\parallel \cdot \mathbf{a}_\perp = 0$, forming a right triangle where $\|\mathbf{a}\|^2 = \|\mathbf{a}_\parallel\|^2 + \|\mathbf{a}_\perp\|^2$ (the Pythagorean Theorem).

### 4.2 Orthogonal Projection onto a Plane
If a plane passes through the origin with unit normal $\mathbf{\hat{n}}$, any vector $\mathbf{v}$ can be decomposed into a part perpendicular to the plane and a part lying inside the plane:

$$\mathbf{v}_\perp = (\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{\hat{n}})\mathbf{\hat{n}}$$

$$\mathbf{v}_\parallel = \mathbf{v} - \mathbf{v}_\perp = \mathbf{v} - (\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{\hat{n}})\mathbf{\hat{n}} \tag{8}$$

In matrix operator form, using the outer product dyad $\mathbf{\hat{n}}\mathbf{\hat{n}}^T$:

$$\mathbf{v}_\parallel = (\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{\hat{n}}\mathbf{\hat{n}}^T) \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{P}_{\text{plane}} \mathbf{v} \tag{9}$$

### 4.3 General Subspace Projection Matrix
Let subspace $\mathcal{S} \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ be spanned by the linearly independent columns of matrix $\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times k}$. The orthogonal projection matrix that projects any vector in $\mathbb{R}^n$ onto $\mathcal{S}$ is:

$$\mathbf{P} = \mathbf{A}(\mathbf{A}^T \mathbf{A})^{-1} \mathbf{A}^T \tag{10}$$

#### The Two Universal Axioms of Orthogonal Projections:
1. **Idempotence (Projecting Twice Changes Nothing):**
   $$\mathbf{P}^2 = \mathbf{P}$$
   Once a vector's shadow hits the floor, projecting the shadow again leaves it unchanged.
2. **Symmetry (Self-Adjointness):**
   $$\mathbf{P}^T = \mathbf{P}$$
   Ensures that the error vector $\mathbf{e} = (\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{P})\mathbf{x}$ is strictly orthogonal to the subspace.

## 5. Coordinate Systems: How We Map Reality

A **coordinate system** is a smooth, invertible mathematical function (a **chart** $\phi$) that maps open patches of physical space into tuples of real numbers in $\mathbb{R}^n$.

### 5.1 Canonical 3D Systems
Different coordinate grids are chosen to mirror the natural physical symmetry of a problem:

| Coordinate System | Coordinates | Coordinate Transformations | Position Vector $\mathbf{r}$ |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Cartesian** | $(x, y, z)$ | $x, y, z \in (-\infty, \infty)$ | $x\mathbf{e}_x + y\mathbf{e}_y + z\mathbf{e}_z$ |
| **Cylindrical** | $(\rho, \phi, z)$ | $x = \rho\cos\phi,\; y = \rho\sin\phi,\; z = z$ | $\rho\mathbf{e}_\rho + z\mathbf{e}_z$ |
| **Spherical** | $(r, \theta, \phi)$ | $x = r\sin\theta\cos\phi,\; y = r\sin\theta\sin\phi,\; z = r\cos\theta$ | $r\mathbf{e}_r$ |

### 5.2 Moving Basis Vectors in Curvilinear Systems
In Cartesian space, the basis vectors $\mathbf{e}_x, \mathbf{e}_y, \mathbf{e}_z$ are constant everywhere. 

In curvilinear systems (like polar or spherical coordinates), the basis vectors **depend on position**:
* At the equator ($ heta = \pi/2, \phi = 0$), the radial unit vector $\mathbf{e}_r$ points along the $+X$ axis.
* At the North Pole ($  heta = 0$), $\mathbf{e}_r$ points along $+Z$.

When differentiating vectors in curvilinear coordinates, one must differentiate both the scalar components and the moving basis vectors:

$$\frac{d\mathbf{r}}{dt} = \frac{d}{dt}(r\mathbf{e}_r) = \dot{r}\mathbf{e}_r + r\dot{\mathbf{e}}_r = \dot{r}\mathbf{e}_r + r\dot{\theta}\mathbf{e}_\theta + r\dot{\phi}\sin\theta\mathbf{e}_\phi \tag{11}$$

This position-dependence of basis vectors is what produces centrifugal and Coriolis kinematic acceleration terms in polar calculus!

## 6. Orientations, Rotations, and the Special Orthogonal Group $\mathrm{SO}(3)$

What is a rotation? Mathematically, a rotation is an orientation-preserving isometric linear transformation:
* It leaves the origin fixed.
* It preserves lengths: $\|\mathbf{R}\mathbf{v}\| = \|\mathbf{v}\|$.
* It preserves dot products and angles: $(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{u}) \cdot (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{v}) = \mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}$.
* It preserves handedness (orientation): $\det(\mathbf{R}) = +1$ (reflections have $\det = -1$).

The set of all such $3 \times 3$ matrices forms the **Special Orthogonal Group** $\mathrm{SO}(3)$, satisfying:

$$\mathbf{R}^T \mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I}, \qquad \det(\mathbf{R}) = +1 \tag{12}$$

### 6.1 Euler's Rotation Theorem
> **Euler's Theorem (1776):**  
> Every arbitrary rotation $\mathbf{R} \in \mathrm{SO}(3)$ of a three-dimensional body fixes a unique one-dimensional line passing through the origin. That line is the **axis of rotation**.

Any 3D rotation can be parameterized by an axis unit vector $\mathbf{\hat{u}}$ and a rotation angle $\theta$ around that axis (Rodrigues' Rotation Formula):

$$\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{\hat{u}}, \theta) = \mathbf{I} + (\sin\theta) \mathbf{K} + (1 - \cos\theta) \mathbf{K}^2 \tag{13}$$

where $\mathbf{K}$ is the skew-symmetric cross-product matrix of $\mathbf{\hat{u}}$.

### 6.2 Active vs. Passive Transformations (Alibi vs. Alias)
One of the most frequent sources of sign errors in physics is confusing active and passive rotations:

1. **Active Rotation (Alibi — "Elsewhere"):**  
   The coordinate axes remain stationary, while the physical object or vector is rotated to a new physical location:
   $$\mathbf{v}' = \mathbf{R} \mathbf{v}$$
2. **Passive Rotation (Alias — "By another name"):**  
   The physical object remains completely stationary in space, but the observer rotates their coordinate measuring axes:
   $$\mathbf{v}'_{\text{coords}} = \mathbf{R}^T \mathbf{v}_{\text{coords}} = \mathbf{R}^{-1} \mathbf{v}_{\text{coords}}$$

> **Rule of Thumb:**  
> Rotating an object counterclockwise by $+45^\circ$ relative to fixed axes produces the exact same coordinate numbers as leaving the object stationary and rotating your coordinate axes clockwise by $-45^\circ$!

```{python}
#| label: fig-active-passive-rotation
#| fig-cap: "Active vs. Passive Rotation. Left: Active rotation turns a physical vector v (blue) into v' (orange) by +45 degrees while coordinate axes remain fixed. Right: Passive rotation keeps the physical vector stationary while rotating the coordinate axes by +45 degrees (gold), changing the vector's coordinate components."
#| code-fold: true
#| code-summary: "Show Python code generating the Active vs Passive Diagram"

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

fig, (ax1, ax2) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(10, 4.8), dpi=180)

theta = np.radians(45)
R_act = np.array([[np.cos(theta), -np.sin(theta)],
                  [np.sin(theta),  np.cos(theta)]])

v = np.array([2.0, 0.5])
v_rot = R_act @ v

# --- Panel 1: Active Rotation ---
ax1.axhline(0, color='gray', linestyle=':', lw=1.2)
ax1.axvline(0, color='gray', linestyle=':', lw=1.2)

ax1.quiver(0, 0, v[0], v[1], angles='xy', scale_units='xy', scale=1, 
           color='#2563eb', lw=2.5, label=r'Original Vector $\mathbf{v}$')
ax1.quiver(0, 0, v_rot[0], v_rot[1], angles='xy', scale_units='xy', scale=1, 
           color='#dc2626', lw=2.5, label=r'Active Rotated $\mathbf{v}^\prime = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{v}$')

arc_angles = np.linspace(np.arctan2(v[1], v[0]), np.arctan2(v_rot[1], v_rot[0]), 30)
ax1.plot(1.2 * np.cos(arc_angles), 1.2 * np.sin(arc_angles), color='#7c3aed', lw=1.8, linestyle='--')
ax1.text(1.35 * np.cos(arc_angles[15]), 1.35 * np.sin(arc_angles[15]), r'$+45^\circ$', color='#7c3aed', fontweight='bold')

ax1.set_xlim(-1.5, 2.8)
ax1.set_ylim(-0.5, 2.5)
ax1.set_aspect('equal')
ax1.set_title("Active Rotation (Object Turns in Fixed Space)", fontsize=10.5, fontweight='bold')
ax1.legend(frameon=True, fontsize=8.5, loc='upper left')
ax1.grid(True, linestyle='--', alpha=0.4)

# --- Panel 2: Passive Rotation ---
ax2.axhline(0, color='gray', linestyle=':', lw=1.2, label='Old Frame ($X, Y$)')
ax2.axvline(0, color='gray', linestyle=':', lw=1.2)

# Rotated axes
axis_x = R_act @ np.array([2.5, 0])
axis_y = R_act @ np.array([0, 2.5])
ax2.plot([-axis_x[0], axis_x[0]], [-axis_x[1], axis_x[1]], color='#d97706', lw=1.8, linestyle='--', label=r'Rotated Frame ($X^\prime, Y^\prime$)')
ax2.plot([-axis_y[0], axis_y[0]], [-axis_y[1], axis_y[1]], color='#d97706', lw=1.8, linestyle='--')

ax2.quiver(0, 0, v[0], v[1], angles='xy', scale_units='xy', scale=1, 
           color='#2563eb', lw=2.8, label=r'Fixed Physical Vector $\mathbf{v}$')

ax2.set_xlim(-1.5, 2.8)
ax2.set_ylim(-0.5, 2.5)
ax2.set_aspect('equal')
ax2.set_title(r"Passive Rotation (Axes Turn by $+45^\circ$)", fontsize=10.5, fontweight='bold')
ax2.legend(frameon=True, fontsize=8.5, loc='upper left')
ax2.grid(True, linestyle='--', alpha=0.4)

plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

## 7. Reference Frames and Moving Observers

What distinguishes a coordinate system from a **reference frame**?
* A **coordinate system** is a mathematical choice of grid (Cartesian, polar, spherical).
* A **reference frame** is a physical observer equipped with a clock, a state of motion (velocity $\mathbf{v}$, acceleration $\mathbf{a}$), and an orientation triad.

You can use spherical coordinates inside a moving train, or Cartesian coordinates on a spinning merry-go-round.

### 7.1 Inertial vs. Non-Inertial Reference Frames
1. **Inertial Reference Frame:** A frame in which Newton's First Law holds true without external modifications: free bodies move in straight lines at constant velocity. Any two inertial frames are related by a **Galilean transformation**:
   $$\mathbf{r}' = \mathbf{r} - \mathbf{v}_0 t - \mathbf{r}_0, \qquad t' = t$$
2. **Non-Inertial (Accelerating / Rotating) Frame:** An observer undergoing acceleration or rotation. In such frames, Newton's Second Law fails unless we introduce **apparent (fictitious) d'Alembert forces**.

### 7.2 The Transport Theorem and Fictitious Forces
Let frame $\mathcal{S}$ be inertial and frame $\mathcal{S}'$ rotate with instantaneous angular velocity vector $\boldsymbol{\omega}(t)$.

For any arbitrary physical vector $\mathbf{A}(t)$, its time derivative in the inertial frame is related to its time derivative observed in the rotating frame by the fundamental **Transport Theorem**:

$$\left(\frac{d\mathbf{A}}{dt}\right)_{\text{inertial}} = \left(\frac{d\mathbf{A}}{dt}\right)_{\text{rotating}} + \boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{A} \tag{14}$$

Applying this twice to position vector $\mathbf{r}(t)$ yields the acceleration observed by a rotating observer:

$$\mathbf{a}_{\text{inertial}} = \mathbf{a}_{\text{rot}} + 2(\boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{v}_{\text{rot}}) + \boldsymbol{\omega} \times (\boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{r}) + \dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}} \times \mathbf{r} \tag{15}$$

Multiplying by mass $m$ and rearranging:

$$m\mathbf{a}_{\text{rot}} = \mathbf{F}_{\text{real}} + \mathbf{F}_{\text{Coriolis}} + \mathbf{F}_{\text{Centrifugal}} + \mathbf{F}_{\text{Euler}} \tag{16}$$

where the fictitious forces are:
* **Centrifugal Force:** $\mathbf{F}_{\text{cent}} = -m \boldsymbol{\omega} \times (\boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{r})$ (directed radially outward).
* **Coriolis Force:** $\mathbf{F}_{\text{cor}} = -2m (\boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{v}_{\text{rot}})$ (acts perpendicular to velocity, curving cyclones on Earth).
* **Euler Force:** $\mathbf{F}_{\text{euler}} = -m (\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}} \times \mathbf{r})$ (arises only when angular rotation rate changes).
