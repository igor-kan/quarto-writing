1. Introduction: The Universality of Harmonic Oscillations

Why is the harmonic oscillator the single most ubiquitous model in all of theoretical and applied physics?

From vibrating diatomic molecules and acoustic resonators to electrical circuits, structural civil engineering, and inertial seismometers, almost every physical system perturbed slightly from stable equilibrium executes harmonic motion.

The Taylor Expansion of a Generic Potential Well

Consider an arbitrary conservative one-dimensional physical system described by a potential energy function \(V(x)\). Suppose the system has a stable equilibrium point at \(x = x_0\).

By definition of equilibrium, the net generalized force vanishes at \(x_0\):

\[F(x_0) = -\left.\frac{dV}{dx}\right|_{x_0} = 0 \tag{1}\]

Expanding \(V(x)\) in a Taylor series about the equilibrium coordinate \(x_0\):

\[V(x) = V(x_0) + V'(x_0)(x - x_0) + \frac{1}{2} V''(x_0)(x - x_0)^2 + \frac{1}{6} V'''(x_0)(x - x_0)^3 + \dots \tag{2}\]

Substituting the equilibrium condition \(V'(x_0) = 0\), and defining the relative coordinate displacement \(y \equiv x - x_0\):

\[V(y) \approx V(x_0) + \frac{1}{2} V''(x_0) y^2 + \mathcal{O}(y^3) \tag{3}\]

For stable equilibrium, \(V(x)\) must be a local minimum, which requires \(V''(x_0) > 0\). Shifting the zero of potential energy such that \(V(x_0) = 0\) and defining the effective stiffness constant:

\[k \equiv V''(x_0) = \left.\frac{d^2 V}{dx^2}\right|_{x_0} > 0 \tag{4}\]

The restoring force acting on small displacements is:

\[F(y) = -\frac{dV}{dy} \approx -k y \tag{5}\]

Equation (5) is Hooke’s Law. Thus, any smooth physical potential well is locally parabolic near a stable minimum, and sufficiently small perturbations execute simple harmonic motion governed by \(m \ddot{y} + k y = 0\).

2. Setting Up the Physical System: Assumptions, Approximations, and Reference Frames

Before writing down equations of motion, a physicist or engineer must translate physical reality into an idealized mathematical model through explicit assumptions.

2.1 Mechanical Assumptions and Idealizations

  1. Point Mass / Lumped Proof Mass: The oscillating body of mass \(m\) is treated as rigid and lumped at its center of mass, neglecting internal elastic deformation of the mass itself.
  2. Linear Elasticity (Hooke’s Law Limit): The spring exhibits linear elasticity (\(F_s = -k \Delta x\)). This requires that strain remains well within the material’s elastic limit, avoiding plastic yielding or non-linear Duffing stiffness terms (\(\alpha x^3\)).
  3. Linear Viscous Dissipation (Stokes Regime): Damping is modeled as a linear viscous force (\(F_d = -c \dot{x}\)). This approximation holds strictly when fluid flow around the moving mass is laminar with low Reynolds number (\(\text{Re} \ll 1\)). At high velocities, fluid drag transitions to quadratic turbulent drag (\(F_d \propto \dot{x}^2\)), rendering the differential equation non-linear.
  4. Small-Angle Approximation for Pendulums: For gravitational pendulums of length \(L\), the restoring torque is \(\tau = -m g L \sin\theta\). For small angles (\(\theta \ll 1\text{ rad}\)), the first-order Maclaurin series approximation \(\sin\theta \approx \theta\) replaces non-linear elliptic motion with linear harmonic oscillation having natural frequency \(\omega_0 = \sqrt{g/L}\).

2.2 Reference Frames: Inertial vs. Accelerating Non-Inertial Frames

The choice of coordinate reference frame is the central conceptual bridge between abstract mechanics and physical sensors like seismometers and accelerometers.

Let \(\mathcal{S}\) denote an inertial reference frame (at rest or in uniform rectilinear motion). Newton’s second law holds strictly in \(\mathcal{S}\):

\[\mathbf{F}_{\text{real}} = m \mathbf{a}_{\text{inertial}} = m \mathbf{\ddot{r}} \tag{6}\]

Now consider an accelerating reference frame \(\mathcal{S}'\) (such as an instrument casing bolted to vibrating ground). Let \(\mathbf{R}_{\text{frame}}(t)\) denote the position of the origin of \(\mathcal{S}'\) with respect to \(\mathcal{S}\), and let \(\mathbf{r}'(t)\) denote the position of the proof mass measured inside the moving frame.

By vector addition:

\[\mathbf{r}(t) = \mathbf{R}_{\text{frame}}(t) + \mathbf{r}'(t) \tag{7}\]

Differentiating twice with respect to time:

\[\mathbf{\ddot{r}}(t) = \mathbf{\ddot{R}}_{\text{frame}}(t) + \mathbf{\ddot{r}}'(t) \implies \mathbf{\ddot{r}}'(t) = \mathbf{\ddot{r}}(t) - \mathbf{\ddot{R}}_{\text{frame}}(t) \tag{8}\]

Multiplying Equation (8) by mass \(m\) and substituting Newton’s second law (\(m\mathbf{\ddot{r}} = \mathbf{F}_{\text{real}}\)):

\[m \mathbf{\ddot{r}}' = \mathbf{F}_{\text{real}} - m \mathbf{\ddot{R}}_{\text{frame}}(t) \tag{9}\]

Equation (9) reveals the emergence of the d’Alembert fictitious force (inertial force):

\[\mathbf{F}_{\text{inertial}} \equiv -m \mathbf{\ddot{R}}_{\text{frame}}(t) \tag{10}\]

An observer operating entirely within the accelerating frame can apply Newton’s laws as if the frame were inertial, provided they add the apparent inertial force \(\mathbf{F}_{\text{inertial}}\) to all real mechanical forces.

2.3 The Base-Excitation Problem: Deriving the Seismometer Equation

Consider a 1D oscillator whose base undergoes prescribed motion \(u_g(t)\) (representing seismic ground displacement). * Let \(x(t)\) be the absolute displacement of mass \(m\) relative to the inertial frame. * Let \(z(t) = x(t) - u_g(t)\) be the relative displacement measured inside the casing.

The real physical forces acting on mass \(m\) depend strictly on the deformation and relative motion of the spring and damper: * Spring restoring force: \(F_s = -k (x - u_g) = -k z\) * Viscous damping force: \(F_d = -c (\dot{x} - \dot{u}_g) = -c \dot{z}\)

Formulation in the Inertial Frame:

\[m \ddot{x} = F_s + F_d = -k z - c \dot{z}\] Substituting \(x(t) = z(t) + u_g(t)\), so \(\ddot{x} = \ddot{z} + \ddot{u}_g\): \[m(\ddot{z} + \ddot{u}_g) = -k z - c \dot{z}\] \[m \ddot{z} + c \dot{z} + k z = -m \ddot{u}_g(t) \tag{11}\]

Formulation in the Moving Frame:

Using Equation (9) directly: \[m \ddot{z} = \mathbf{F}_{\text{real}} + \mathbf{F}_{\text{inertial}} = (-kz - c\dot{z}) + (-m\ddot{u}_g(t))\] which immediately yields Equation (11)!

Dividing by \(m\) and defining the undamped natural frequency \(\omega_0 \equiv \sqrt{k/m}\) and damping ratio \(\zeta \equiv \frac{c}{2\sqrt{km}}\):

\[\ddot{z}(t) + 2\zeta \omega_0 \dot{z}(t) + \omega_0^2 z(t) = -\ddot{u}_g(t) \tag{12}\]

This is the universal equation of base-excited motion. It proves that a seismometer does not measure ground displacement or velocity; it measures the relative displacement \(z(t)\) driven by ground acceleration \(-\ddot{u}_g(t)\).

3. The Simple Harmonic Oscillator (SHO)

In the absence of damping (\(c = 0\)) and external forcing (\(\ddot{u}_g = 0\)), Equation (12) reduces to the conservative Simple Harmonic Oscillator:

\[\ddot{x}(t) + \omega_0^2 x(t) = 0 \tag{13}\]

3.1 General Solution and Phase Space

The general solution is a linear superposition of harmonic modes:

\[x(t) = A \cos(\omega_0 t - \phi) = C_1 \cos(\omega_0 t) + C_2 \sin(\omega_0 t) \tag{14}\]

where amplitude \(A\) and phase \(\phi\) are determined by initial position \(x_0\) and initial velocity \(v_0\):

\[A = \sqrt{x_0^2 + \left(\frac{v_0}{\omega_0}\right)^2}, \qquad \tan\phi = \frac{v_0}{\omega_0 x_0} \tag{15}\]

3.2 Total Mechanical Energy

The total energy is conserved at all times:

\[E = T + V = \frac{1}{2} m \dot{x}^2 + \frac{1}{2} k x^2 = \frac{1}{2} k A^2 = \text{constant} \tag{16}\]

In phase space \((x, p = m\dot{x})\), trajectories form closed concentric ellipses:

\[\frac{x^2}{2E/k} + \frac{p^2}{2mE} = 1 \tag{17}\]

By Liouville’s theorem, the phase space area enclosed by any closed trajectory is an adiabatic invariant.

4. The Damped Harmonic Oscillator

When viscous dissipation is present, energy is lost to heat at a rate \(\frac{dE}{dt} = -c \dot{x}^2 \le 0\). The homogeneous equation of motion is:

\[\ddot{x} + 2\gamma \dot{x} + \omega_0^2 x = 0 \tag{18}\]

where \(\gamma \equiv \frac{c}{2m} = \zeta \omega_0\) is the decay damping rate, and \(\zeta \equiv \frac{c}{2\sqrt{km}}\) is the dimensionless damping ratio.

Assuming an ansatz \(x(t) = e^{r t}\), the characteristic algebraic equation is:

\[r^2 + 2\zeta \omega_0 r + \omega_0^2 = 0 \implies r_{1,2} = -\zeta \omega_0 \pm \omega_0 \sqrt{\zeta^2 - 1} \tag{19}\]

The discriminant \(\Delta = \zeta^2 - 1\) defines three distinct physical regimes:

4.1 Underdamped Regime (\(\zeta < 1, c < 2\sqrt{km}\))

The roots are complex conjugates: \(r_{1,2} = -\gamma \pm i \omega_d\), where:

\[\omega_d \equiv \omega_0 \sqrt{1 - \zeta^2} \tag{20}\]

is the damped natural frequency (\(\omega_d < \omega_0\)). The general solution is an exponentially decaying sinusoid:

\[x(t) = A_0 e^{-\zeta \omega_0 t} \cos(\omega_d t - \phi) \tag{21}\]

The Logarithmic Decrement (\(\delta\))

The rate of decay is quantified by the logarithmic decrement \(\delta\), defined as the natural logarithm of the ratio of two consecutive displacement peaks separated by damped period \(T_d = \frac{2\pi}{\omega_d}\):

\[\delta \equiv \ln\left( \frac{x(t)}{x(t + T_d)} \right) = \zeta \omega_0 T_d = \zeta \omega_0 \left( \frac{2\pi}{\omega_0 \sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}} \right) = \frac{2\pi \zeta}{\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}} \tag{22}\]

Measuring \(\delta\) from an experimental ring-down trace yields the damping ratio \(\zeta\) directly:

\[\zeta = \frac{\delta}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^2 + \delta^2}} \tag{23}\]

4.2 Critically Damped Regime (\(\zeta = 1, c = 2\sqrt{km}\))

The characteristic roots are degenerate and real: \(r_1 = r_2 = -\omega_0\). The general solution requires a linear secular term:

\[x(t) = (A + B t) e^{-\omega_0 t} \tag{24}\]

  • Engineering Significance: Critical damping provides the fastest possible return to equilibrium without oscillatory overshoot.
  • In analog galvanometers, car shock absorbers, and seismometers, critical damping prevents ringing that would distort subsequent signals.

4.3 Overdamped Regime (\(\zeta > 1, c > 2\sqrt{km}\))

The roots are distinct negative real numbers: \(r_{1,2} = -\omega_0 (\zeta \mp \sqrt{\zeta^2 - 1}) < 0\). The solution is purely exponential:

\[x(t) = C_1 e^{r_1 t} + C_2 e^{r_2 t} \tag{25}\]

Because \(|r_1| < \omega_0 < |r_2|\), the long-term decay is bottlenecked by the slower decay mode \(r_1 = -\omega_0(\zeta - \sqrt{\zeta^2 - 1})\). The mass creeps sluggishly back toward equilibrium without oscillating.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

t = np.linspace(0, 15, 500)
omega_0 = 2.0

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8.5, 4.5), dpi=180)

zetas = [0.15, 0.4, 0.707, 1.0, 2.0]
colors = ['#dc2626', '#f59e0b', '#2563eb', '#059669', '#7c3aed']

for zeta, c in zip(zetas, colors):
    if zeta < 1.0:
        omega_d = omega_0 * np.sqrt(1.0 - zeta**2)
        x = np.exp(-zeta * omega_0 * t) * (np.cos(omega_d * t) + (zeta * omega_0 / omega_d) * np.sin(omega_d * t))
        lbl = f"$\\zeta = {zeta}$ (Underdamped)" if zeta != 0.707 else f"$\\zeta = {zeta}$ (Butterworth Flat)"
    elif zeta == 1.0:
        x = (1.0 + omega_0 * t) * np.exp(-omega_0 * t)
        lbl = f"$\\zeta = {zeta}$ (Critically Damped)"
    else:
        r1 = -zeta * omega_0 + omega_0 * np.sqrt(zeta**2 - 1.0)
        r2 = -zeta * omega_0 - omega_0 * np.sqrt(zeta**2 - 1.0)
        c1 = -r2 / (r1 - r2)
        c2 = r1 / (r1 - r2)
        x = c1 * np.exp(r1 * t) + c2 * np.exp(r2 * t)
        lbl = f"$\\zeta = {zeta}$ (Overdamped)"
    ax.plot(t, x, label=lbl, color=c, lw=2.0)

ax.axhline(0, color='gray', linestyle=':', alpha=0.6)
ax.set_xlabel("Time $t$ [seconds]", fontsize=10.5)
ax.set_ylabel("Normalized Displacement $x(t) / x_0$", fontsize=10.5)

5. The Driven Harmonic Oscillator, Resonance, and Quality Factor

When an external periodic force acts on the mass:

\[m \ddot{x} + c \dot{x} + k x = F_0 \cos(\omega t) \implies \ddot{x} + 2\zeta \omega_0 \dot{x} + \omega_0^2 x = \frac{F_0}{m} \cos(\omega t) \tag{26}\]

The complete solution is the sum of the transient homogeneous solution \(x_h(t)\) (which dies out as \(e^{-\gamma t}\)) and the steady-state particular solution \(x_p(t)\):

\[x(t) = x_h(t) + x_p(t) \xrightarrow{t \gg 1/\gamma} x_p(t)\]

5.1 Steady-State Complex Amplitude and Transfer Function

Using complex exponentials, represent the driving force as \(\tilde{F}(t) = F_0 e^{i\omega t}\) and the response as \(\tilde{x}(t) = X_0 e^{i(\omega t - \phi)}\). Substituting into Equation (26):

\[(-\omega^2 + 2i\zeta \omega_0 \omega + \omega_0^2) X_0 e^{-i\phi} = \frac{F_0}{m}\]

Defining the frequency ratio \(u \equiv \omega / \omega_0\):

\[X_0(\omega) = \frac{F_0 / k}{\sqrt{(1 - u^2)^2 + (2\zeta u)^2}}, \qquad \tan\phi(\omega) = \frac{2\zeta u}{1 - u^2} \tag{27}\]

The Dynamic Magnification Factor \(M(u) \equiv \frac{X_0}{F_0/k}\) is the ratio of dynamic amplitude to static deflection:

\[M(u) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{(1 - u^2)^2 + (2\zeta u)^2}} \tag{28}\]

5.2 Resonance and Peak Frequency Shift

Differentiating \(M(u)\) with respect to \(u\) and setting the derivative to zero:

\[\frac{d}{du} \left[ (1 - u^2)^2 + (2\zeta u)^2 \right] = 2(1 - u^2)(-2u) + 8\zeta^2 u = 0\]

\[-4u(1 - u^2 - 2\zeta^2) = 0 \implies u_r = \sqrt{1 - 2\zeta^2}\]

\[\omega_r = \omega_0 \sqrt{1 - 2\zeta^2} \quad \left(\text{for } \zeta < \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \approx 0.707\right) \tag{29}\]

  • Displacement Resonance Frequency (\(\omega_r\)): Occurs below the undamped natural frequency \(\omega_0\).
  • For \(\zeta \ge 1/\sqrt{2}\), no resonant peak exists (\(M(u)\) decreases monotonically for all \(u > 0\)).
  • Velocity Resonance: The velocity amplitude \(\dot{X}_0 = \omega X_0 = \frac{F_0 / m}{\sqrt{(\omega_0^2/\omega - \omega)^2 + 4\gamma^2}}\) peaks exactly at \(\omega = \omega_0\) for all damping ratios!

5.3 Phase Shift at Resonance

At resonance (\(\omega = \omega_0\), \(u = 1\)), the denominator of \(\tan\phi\) vanishes:

\[\phi(\omega_0) = 90^\circ = \frac{\pi}{2} \text{ radians}\]

When \(\phi = \pi/2\), displacement lags driving force by \(90^\circ\), which means velocity \(\dot{x}(t)\) is in exact phase with the driving force:

\[F(t) \cdot \dot{x}(t) = F_0 \cos(\omega t) \cdot \dot{X}_0 \cos(\omega t) = F_0 \dot{X}_0 \cos^2(\omega t) \ge 0\]

The applied force does positive work throughout the entire cycle, maximizing cycle-averaged power absorption:

\[\langle P \rangle_{\max} = \frac{1}{2} F_0 \dot{X}_0 = \frac{F_0^2}{2 c} \tag{30}\]

5.4 The Quality Factor (\(Q\))

The Quality Factor \(Q\) measures the sharpness of the resonance peak and the system’s energy retention:

\[Q \equiv 2\pi \frac{\text{Energy Stored}}{\text{Energy Dissipated per Cycle}} = \frac{\omega_0}{2\gamma} = \frac{1}{2\zeta} \tag{31}\]

For weakly damped systems (\(Q \gg 1\)): * Peak magnification at resonance: \(M(\omega_0) = Q\). * Full Width at Half Maximum (FWHM) bandwidth: \(\frac{\Delta \omega}{\omega_0} \approx \frac{1}{Q}\).

6. Coupled Harmonic Oscillators and Normal Modes

When two or more oscillating systems are connected by elastic coupling, energy is no longer confined to a single mass. The dynamics require a multi-degree-of-freedom matrix formulation.

6.1 Two Coupled Oscillators

Consider two identical masses \(m_1 = m_2 = m\) constrained to move along a line. Each mass is anchored to a fixed wall by an outer spring of stiffness \(k\), and the two masses are connected to each other by a central coupling spring of stiffness \(k_c\).

Let \(x_1(t)\) and \(x_2(t)\) denote their displacements from equilibrium.

Applying Newton’s second law:

\[m \ddot{x}_1 = -k x_1 + k_c (x_2 - x_1) = -(k + k_c) x_1 + k_c x_2 \tag{32}\]

\[m \ddot{x}_2 = -k x_2 - k_c (x_2 - x_1) = k_c x_1 - (k + k_c) x_2 \tag{33}\]

In matrix form:

\[\mathbf{M} \mathbf{\ddot{x}} + \mathbf{K} \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0} \tag{34}\]

\[\begin{bmatrix} m & 0 \\ 0 & m \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \ddot{x}_1 \\ \ddot{x}_2 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} k + k_c & -k_c \\ -k_c & k + k_c \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \tag{35}\]

6.2 The Eigenvalue Problem and Normal Frequencies

Assuming synchronous harmonic motion \(\mathbf{x}(t) = \mathbf{a} e^{i\omega t}\):

\[(\mathbf{K} - \omega^2 \mathbf{M}) \mathbf{a} = \mathbf{0} \tag{36}\]

For non-trivial eigenvector solutions \(\mathbf{a} \ne \mathbf{0}\), the secular determinant must vanish:

\[\det(\mathbf{K} - \omega^2 \mathbf{M}) = \begin{vmatrix} k + k_c - m\omega^2 & -k_c \\ -k_c & k + k_c - m\omega^2 \end{vmatrix} = 0 \tag{37}\]

\[(k + k_c - m\omega^2)^2 - k_c^2 = 0 \implies k + k_c - m\omega^2 = \pm k_c\]

This yields two distinct normal mode frequencies:

  1. Symmetric (In-Phase) Mode \(\omega_1\): \[m \omega_1^2 = k \implies \omega_1 = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} \tag{38}\] Eigenvector: \(\mathbf{a}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\). Both masses move with identical amplitude in the same direction (\(x_1(t) = x_2(t)\)). The central coupling spring remains unstretched (\(x_2 - x_1 = 0\))!
  2. Antisymmetric (Out-of-Phase) Mode \(\omega_2\): \[m \omega_2^2 = k + 2k_c \implies \omega_2 = \sqrt{\frac{k + 2k_c}{m}} \tag{39}\] Eigenvector: \(\mathbf{a}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}\). The masses move in opposite directions (\(x_1(t) = -x_2(t)\)). The central spring experiences double deformation.

6.3 Normal Coordinates and Decoupling

Define normal coordinates \(\eta_1, \eta_2\) as linear combinations of physical coordinates:

\[\eta_1(t) \equiv \frac{x_1(t) + x_2(t)}{2}, \qquad \eta_2(t) \equiv \frac{x_1(t) - x_2(t)}{2} \tag{40}\]

Adding and subtracting Equations (32) and (33):

\[\ddot{\eta}_1 + \omega_1^2 \eta_1 = 0, \qquad \ddot{\eta}_2 + \omega_2^2 \eta_2 = 0 \tag{41}\]

In normal coordinate space, all cross-coupling vanishes! The complex coupled system decouples into two completely independent, non-interacting simple harmonic oscillators.

6.4 Energy Exchange and the Beat Phenomenon

Suppose we pull mass 1 to initial displacement \(x_0\) while holding mass 2 at equilibrium, releasing both from rest: \(x_1(0) = x_0, x_2(0) = 0, \dot{x}_1(0) = \dot{x}_2(0) = 0\).

Decomposing into normal modes: \[\eta_1(0) = \frac{x_0}{2}, \quad \eta_2(0) = \frac{x_0}{2}\]

\[\eta_1(t) = \frac{x_0}{2} \cos(\omega_1 t), \quad \eta_2(t) = \frac{x_0}{2} \cos(\omega_2 t)\]

Transforming back to physical coordinates (\(x_1 = \eta_1 + \eta_2\), \(x_2 = \eta_1 - \eta_2\)):

\[x_1(t) = x_0 \cos\left( \frac{\omega_2 - \omega_1}{2} t \right) \cos\left( \frac{\omega_1 + \omega_2}{2} t \right) \tag{42}\]

\[x_2(t) = x_0 \sin\left( \frac{\omega_2 - \omega_1}{2} t \right) \sin\left( \frac{\omega_1 + \omega_2}{2} t \right) \tag{43}\]

When the coupling is weak (\(k_c \ll k\)), \(\omega_1 \approx \omega_2\): * The fast oscillation occurs at average carrier frequency \(\bar{\omega} = \frac{\omega_1 + \omega_2}{2}\). * The amplitude is modulated by a slow envelope oscillating at the beat frequency \(\omega_{\text{beat}} = |\omega_2 - \omega_1|\). * Complete energy sloshing occurs: mass 1 gradually stops as all its kinetic and potential energy transfers into mass 2; then mass 2 transfers its energy back into mass 1!

7. The Continuum Limit: Transition from Coupled Oscillators to Wave Mechanics

How does discrete Newtonian mechanics give rise to continuum partial differential equations like the seismic wave equation?

Consider a 1D lattice consisting of \(N\) identical point masses \(m\) arranged in a line with uniform lattice spacing \(a\). Each mass \(u_j(t)\) is connected to its nearest neighbors by springs of stiffness \(k\).

Applying Newton’s second law to the \(j\)-th interior mass:

\[m \ddot{u}_j = k (u_{j+1} - u_j) - k (u_j - u_{j-1}) = k (u_{j+1} - 2u_j + u_{j-1}) \tag{44}\]

Now define the macroscopic physical properties of the rod: * Total length \(L = N a\). * Linear mass density \(\rho = m / a \implies m = \rho a\). * Elastic modulus / rod tension \(T = k a \implies k = T / a\).

Substituting these definitions into Equation (44):

\[\rho a \frac{\partial^2 u_j}{\partial t^2} = \left(\frac{T}{a}\right) \left[ u_{j+1} - 2u_j + u_{j-1} \right]\]

Dividing by \(a\):

\[\rho \frac{\partial^2 u_j}{\partial t^2} = T \left[ \frac{u_{j+1} - 2u_j + u_{j-1}}{a^2} \right] \tag{45}\]

Now take the continuum limit as lattice spacing \(a \to 0\) and number of masses \(N \to \infty\), such that the discrete coordinate \(j a \to x\):

\[\lim_{a \to 0} \frac{u(x + a) - 2u(x) + u(x - a)}{a^2} = \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} \tag{46}\]

Equation (45) becomes:

\[\rho \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial t^2} = T \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} \implies \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial t^2} = v^2 \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} \tag{47}\]

where the acoustic wave propagation velocity is:

\[v = \sqrt{\frac{T}{\rho}} \tag{48}\]

Foundational Mathematical Bridge:
The 1D continuous acoustic/seismic wave equation is the exact infinite-dimensional continuum limit of a chain of coupled harmonic oscillators. The discrete normal modes \(\omega_n\) of the \(N\)-mass chain become the continuous standing Fourier wave modes of the continuum rod!

## 1. Introduction: The Universality of Harmonic Oscillations

Why is the harmonic oscillator the single most ubiquitous model in all of theoretical and applied physics?

From vibrating diatomic molecules and acoustic resonators to electrical circuits, structural civil engineering, and inertial seismometers, almost every physical system perturbed slightly from stable equilibrium executes harmonic motion.

### The Taylor Expansion of a Generic Potential Well
Consider an arbitrary conservative one-dimensional physical system described by a potential energy function $V(x)$. Suppose the system has a stable equilibrium point at $x = x_0$.

By definition of equilibrium, the net generalized force vanishes at $x_0$:

$$F(x_0) = -\left.\frac{dV}{dx}\right|_{x_0} = 0 \tag{1}$$

Expanding $V(x)$ in a Taylor series about the equilibrium coordinate $x_0$:

$$V(x) = V(x_0) + V'(x_0)(x - x_0) + \frac{1}{2} V''(x_0)(x - x_0)^2 + \frac{1}{6} V'''(x_0)(x - x_0)^3 + \dots \tag{2}$$

Substituting the equilibrium condition $V'(x_0) = 0$, and defining the relative coordinate displacement $y \equiv x - x_0$:

$$V(y) \approx V(x_0) + \frac{1}{2} V''(x_0) y^2 + \mathcal{O}(y^3) \tag{3}$$

For stable equilibrium, $V(x)$ must be a local minimum, which requires $V''(x_0) > 0$. Shifting the zero of potential energy such that $V(x_0) = 0$ and defining the **effective stiffness constant**:

$$k \equiv V''(x_0) = \left.\frac{d^2 V}{dx^2}\right|_{x_0} > 0 \tag{4}$$

The restoring force acting on small displacements is:

$$F(y) = -\frac{dV}{dy} \approx -k y \tag{5}$$

Equation (5) is **Hooke's Law**. Thus, **any smooth physical potential well is locally parabolic near a stable minimum**, and sufficiently small perturbations execute simple harmonic motion governed by $m \ddot{y} + k y = 0$.

## 2. Setting Up the Physical System: Assumptions, Approximations, and Reference Frames

Before writing down equations of motion, a physicist or engineer must translate physical reality into an idealized mathematical model through explicit assumptions.

### 2.1 Mechanical Assumptions and Idealizations
1. **Point Mass / Lumped Proof Mass**: The oscillating body of mass $m$ is treated as rigid and lumped at its center of mass, neglecting internal elastic deformation of the mass itself.
2. **Linear Elasticity (Hooke's Law Limit)**: The spring exhibits linear elasticity ($F_s = -k \Delta x$). This requires that strain remains well within the material's elastic limit, avoiding plastic yielding or non-linear Duffing stiffness terms ($\alpha x^3$).
3. **Linear Viscous Dissipation (Stokes Regime)**: Damping is modeled as a linear viscous force ($F_d = -c \dot{x}$). This approximation holds strictly when fluid flow around the moving mass is laminar with low Reynolds number ($\text{Re} \ll 1$). At high velocities, fluid drag transitions to quadratic turbulent drag ($F_d \propto \dot{x}^2$), rendering the differential equation non-linear.
4. **Small-Angle Approximation for Pendulums**: For gravitational pendulums of length $L$, the restoring torque is $\tau = -m g L \sin\theta$. For small angles ($\theta \ll 1\text{ rad}$), the first-order Maclaurin series approximation $\sin\theta \approx \theta$ replaces non-linear elliptic motion with linear harmonic oscillation having natural frequency $\omega_0 = \sqrt{g/L}$.

### 2.2 Reference Frames: Inertial vs. Accelerating Non-Inertial Frames

The choice of coordinate reference frame is the central conceptual bridge between abstract mechanics and physical sensors like seismometers and accelerometers.

Let $\mathcal{S}$ denote an **inertial reference frame** (at rest or in uniform rectilinear motion). Newton's second law holds strictly in $\mathcal{S}$:

$$\mathbf{F}_{\text{real}} = m \mathbf{a}_{\text{inertial}} = m \mathbf{\ddot{r}} \tag{6}$$

Now consider an **accelerating reference frame** $\mathcal{S}'$ (such as an instrument casing bolted to vibrating ground). Let $\mathbf{R}_{\text{frame}}(t)$ denote the position of the origin of $\mathcal{S}'$ with respect to $\mathcal{S}$, and let $\mathbf{r}'(t)$ denote the position of the proof mass measured inside the moving frame.

By vector addition:

$$\mathbf{r}(t) = \mathbf{R}_{\text{frame}}(t) + \mathbf{r}'(t) \tag{7}$$

Differentiating twice with respect to time:

$$\mathbf{\ddot{r}}(t) = \mathbf{\ddot{R}}_{\text{frame}}(t) + \mathbf{\ddot{r}}'(t) \implies \mathbf{\ddot{r}}'(t) = \mathbf{\ddot{r}}(t) - \mathbf{\ddot{R}}_{\text{frame}}(t) \tag{8}$$

Multiplying Equation (8) by mass $m$ and substituting Newton's second law ($m\mathbf{\ddot{r}} = \mathbf{F}_{\text{real}}$):

$$m \mathbf{\ddot{r}}' = \mathbf{F}_{\text{real}} - m \mathbf{\ddot{R}}_{\text{frame}}(t) \tag{9}$$

Equation (9) reveals the emergence of the **d'Alembert fictitious force (inertial force)**:

$$\mathbf{F}_{\text{inertial}} \equiv -m \mathbf{\ddot{R}}_{\text{frame}}(t) \tag{10}$$

An observer operating entirely within the accelerating frame can apply Newton's laws as if the frame were inertial, provided they add the apparent inertial force $\mathbf{F}_{\text{inertial}}$ to all real mechanical forces.

### 2.3 The Base-Excitation Problem: Deriving the Seismometer Equation

Consider a 1D oscillator whose base undergoes prescribed motion $u_g(t)$ (representing seismic ground displacement).
* Let $x(t)$ be the absolute displacement of mass $m$ relative to the inertial frame.
* Let $z(t) = x(t) - u_g(t)$ be the relative displacement measured inside the casing.

The real physical forces acting on mass $m$ depend strictly on the deformation and relative motion of the spring and damper:
* Spring restoring force: $F_s = -k (x - u_g) = -k z$
* Viscous damping force: $F_d = -c (\dot{x} - \dot{u}_g) = -c \dot{z}$

#### Formulation in the Inertial Frame:
$$m \ddot{x} = F_s + F_d = -k z - c \dot{z}$$
Substituting $x(t) = z(t) + u_g(t)$, so $\ddot{x} = \ddot{z} + \ddot{u}_g$:
$$m(\ddot{z} + \ddot{u}_g) = -k z - c \dot{z}$$
$$m \ddot{z} + c \dot{z} + k z = -m \ddot{u}_g(t) \tag{11}$$

#### Formulation in the Moving Frame:
Using Equation (9) directly:
$$m \ddot{z} = \mathbf{F}_{\text{real}} + \mathbf{F}_{\text{inertial}} = (-kz - c\dot{z}) + (-m\ddot{u}_g(t))$$
which immediately yields Equation (11)!

Dividing by $m$ and defining the undamped natural frequency $\omega_0 \equiv \sqrt{k/m}$ and damping ratio $\zeta \equiv \frac{c}{2\sqrt{km}}$:

$$\ddot{z}(t) + 2\zeta \omega_0 \dot{z}(t) + \omega_0^2 z(t) = -\ddot{u}_g(t) \tag{12}$$

This is the universal equation of base-excited motion. It proves that a seismometer does not measure ground displacement or velocity; it measures the relative displacement $z(t)$ driven by **ground acceleration** $-\ddot{u}_g(t)$.

---

## 3. The Simple Harmonic Oscillator (SHO)

In the absence of damping ($c = 0$) and external forcing ($\ddot{u}_g = 0$), Equation (12) reduces to the conservative Simple Harmonic Oscillator:

$$\ddot{x}(t) + \omega_0^2 x(t) = 0 \tag{13}$$

### 3.1 General Solution and Phase Space
The general solution is a linear superposition of harmonic modes:

$$x(t) = A \cos(\omega_0 t - \phi) = C_1 \cos(\omega_0 t) + C_2 \sin(\omega_0 t) \tag{14}$$

where amplitude $A$ and phase $\phi$ are determined by initial position $x_0$ and initial velocity $v_0$:

$$A = \sqrt{x_0^2 + \left(\frac{v_0}{\omega_0}\right)^2}, \qquad \tan\phi = \frac{v_0}{\omega_0 x_0} \tag{15}$$

### 3.2 Total Mechanical Energy
The total energy is conserved at all times:

$$E = T + V = \frac{1}{2} m \dot{x}^2 + \frac{1}{2} k x^2 = \frac{1}{2} k A^2 = \text{constant} \tag{16}$$

In phase space $(x, p = m\dot{x})$, trajectories form closed concentric ellipses:

$$\frac{x^2}{2E/k} + \frac{p^2}{2mE} = 1 \tag{17}$$

By Liouville's theorem, the phase space area enclosed by any closed trajectory is an adiabatic invariant.

## 4. The Damped Harmonic Oscillator

When viscous dissipation is present, energy is lost to heat at a rate $\frac{dE}{dt} = -c \dot{x}^2 \le 0$. The homogeneous equation of motion is:

$$\ddot{x} + 2\gamma \dot{x} + \omega_0^2 x = 0 \tag{18}$$

where $\gamma \equiv \frac{c}{2m} = \zeta \omega_0$ is the decay damping rate, and $\zeta \equiv \frac{c}{2\sqrt{km}}$ is the dimensionless damping ratio.

Assuming an ansatz $x(t) = e^{r t}$, the characteristic algebraic equation is:

$$r^2 + 2\zeta \omega_0 r + \omega_0^2 = 0 \implies r_{1,2} = -\zeta \omega_0 \pm \omega_0 \sqrt{\zeta^2 - 1} \tag{19}$$

The discriminant $\Delta = \zeta^2 - 1$ defines three distinct physical regimes:

### 4.1 Underdamped Regime ($\zeta < 1, c < 2\sqrt{km}$)
The roots are complex conjugates: $r_{1,2} = -\gamma \pm i \omega_d$, where:

$$\omega_d \equiv \omega_0 \sqrt{1 - \zeta^2} \tag{20}$$

is the **damped natural frequency** ($\omega_d < \omega_0$). The general solution is an exponentially decaying sinusoid:

$$x(t) = A_0 e^{-\zeta \omega_0 t} \cos(\omega_d t - \phi) \tag{21}$$

#### The Logarithmic Decrement ($\delta$)
The rate of decay is quantified by the logarithmic decrement $\delta$, defined as the natural logarithm of the ratio of two consecutive displacement peaks separated by damped period $T_d = \frac{2\pi}{\omega_d}$:

$$\delta \equiv \ln\left( \frac{x(t)}{x(t + T_d)} \right) = \zeta \omega_0 T_d = \zeta \omega_0 \left( \frac{2\pi}{\omega_0 \sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}} \right) = \frac{2\pi \zeta}{\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}} \tag{22}$$

Measuring $\delta$ from an experimental ring-down trace yields the damping ratio $\zeta$ directly:

$$\zeta = \frac{\delta}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^2 + \delta^2}} \tag{23}$$

### 4.2 Critically Damped Regime ($\zeta = 1, c = 2\sqrt{km}$)
The characteristic roots are degenerate and real: $r_1 = r_2 = -\omega_0$. The general solution requires a linear secular term:

$$x(t) = (A + B t) e^{-\omega_0 t} \tag{24}$$

* **Engineering Significance**: Critical damping provides the **fastest possible return to equilibrium without oscillatory overshoot**.
* In analog galvanometers, car shock absorbers, and seismometers, critical damping prevents ringing that would distort subsequent signals.

### 4.3 Overdamped Regime ($\zeta > 1, c > 2\sqrt{km}$)
The roots are distinct negative real numbers: $r_{1,2} = -\omega_0 (\zeta \mp \sqrt{\zeta^2 - 1}) < 0$. The solution is purely exponential:

$$x(t) = C_1 e^{r_1 t} + C_2 e^{r_2 t} \tag{25}$$

Because $|r_1| < \omega_0 < |r_2|$, the long-term decay is bottlenecked by the slower decay mode $r_1 = -\omega_0(\zeta - \sqrt{\zeta^2 - 1})$. The mass creeps sluggishly back toward equilibrium without oscillating.

## 5. The Driven Harmonic Oscillator, Resonance, and Quality Factor

When an external periodic force acts on the mass:

$$m \ddot{x} + c \dot{x} + k x = F_0 \cos(\omega t) \implies \ddot{x} + 2\zeta \omega_0 \dot{x} + \omega_0^2 x = \frac{F_0}{m} \cos(\omega t) \tag{26}$$

The complete solution is the sum of the transient homogeneous solution $x_h(t)$ (which dies out as $e^{-\gamma t}$) and the steady-state particular solution $x_p(t)$:

$$x(t) = x_h(t) + x_p(t) \xrightarrow{t \gg 1/\gamma} x_p(t)$$

### 5.1 Steady-State Complex Amplitude and Transfer Function
Using complex exponentials, represent the driving force as $\tilde{F}(t) = F_0 e^{i\omega t}$ and the response as $\tilde{x}(t) = X_0 e^{i(\omega t - \phi)}$. Substituting into Equation (26):

$$(-\omega^2 + 2i\zeta \omega_0 \omega + \omega_0^2) X_0 e^{-i\phi} = \frac{F_0}{m}$$

Defining the frequency ratio $u \equiv \omega / \omega_0$:

$$X_0(\omega) = \frac{F_0 / k}{\sqrt{(1 - u^2)^2 + (2\zeta u)^2}}, \qquad \tan\phi(\omega) = \frac{2\zeta u}{1 - u^2} \tag{27}$$

The **Dynamic Magnification Factor** $M(u) \equiv \frac{X_0}{F_0/k}$ is the ratio of dynamic amplitude to static deflection:

$$M(u) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{(1 - u^2)^2 + (2\zeta u)^2}} \tag{28}$$

### 5.2 Resonance and Peak Frequency Shift
Differentiating $M(u)$ with respect to $u$ and setting the derivative to zero:

$$\frac{d}{du} \left[ (1 - u^2)^2 + (2\zeta u)^2 \right] = 2(1 - u^2)(-2u) + 8\zeta^2 u = 0$$

$$-4u(1 - u^2 - 2\zeta^2) = 0 \implies u_r = \sqrt{1 - 2\zeta^2}$$

$$\omega_r = \omega_0 \sqrt{1 - 2\zeta^2} \quad \left(\text{for } \zeta < \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \approx 0.707\right) \tag{29}$$

* **Displacement Resonance Frequency ($\omega_r$)**: Occurs **below** the undamped natural frequency $\omega_0$.
* For $\zeta \ge 1/\sqrt{2}$, no resonant peak exists ($M(u)$ decreases monotonically for all $u > 0$).
* **Velocity Resonance**: The velocity amplitude $\dot{X}_0 = \omega X_0 = \frac{F_0 / m}{\sqrt{(\omega_0^2/\omega - \omega)^2 + 4\gamma^2}}$ peaks **exactly at $\omega = \omega_0$** for all damping ratios!

### 5.3 Phase Shift at Resonance
At resonance ($\omega = \omega_0$, $u = 1$), the denominator of $\tan\phi$ vanishes:

$$\phi(\omega_0) = 90^\circ = \frac{\pi}{2} \text{ radians}$$

When $\phi = \pi/2$, displacement lags driving force by $90^\circ$, which means velocity $\dot{x}(t)$ is **in exact phase** with the driving force:

$$F(t) \cdot \dot{x}(t) = F_0 \cos(\omega t) \cdot \dot{X}_0 \cos(\omega t) = F_0 \dot{X}_0 \cos^2(\omega t) \ge 0$$

The applied force does positive work throughout the entire cycle, maximizing cycle-averaged power absorption:

$$\langle P \rangle_{\max} = \frac{1}{2} F_0 \dot{X}_0 = \frac{F_0^2}{2 c} \tag{30}$$

### 5.4 The Quality Factor ($Q$)
The Quality Factor $Q$ measures the sharpness of the resonance peak and the system's energy retention:

$$Q \equiv 2\pi \frac{\text{Energy Stored}}{\text{Energy Dissipated per Cycle}} = \frac{\omega_0}{2\gamma} = \frac{1}{2\zeta} \tag{31}$$

For weakly damped systems ($Q \gg 1$):
* Peak magnification at resonance: $M(\omega_0) = Q$.
* Full Width at Half Maximum (FWHM) bandwidth: $\frac{\Delta \omega}{\omega_0} \approx \frac{1}{Q}$.

## 6. Coupled Harmonic Oscillators and Normal Modes

When two or more oscillating systems are connected by elastic coupling, energy is no longer confined to a single mass. The dynamics require a multi-degree-of-freedom matrix formulation.

### 6.1 Two Coupled Oscillators
Consider two identical masses $m_1 = m_2 = m$ constrained to move along a line. Each mass is anchored to a fixed wall by an outer spring of stiffness $k$, and the two masses are connected to each other by a central coupling spring of stiffness $k_c$.

Let $x_1(t)$ and $x_2(t)$ denote their displacements from equilibrium.

Applying Newton's second law:

$$m \ddot{x}_1 = -k x_1 + k_c (x_2 - x_1) = -(k + k_c) x_1 + k_c x_2 \tag{32}$$

$$m \ddot{x}_2 = -k x_2 - k_c (x_2 - x_1) = k_c x_1 - (k + k_c) x_2 \tag{33}$$

In matrix form:

$$\mathbf{M} \mathbf{\ddot{x}} + \mathbf{K} \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0} \tag{34}$$

$$\begin{bmatrix} m & 0 \\ 0 & m \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \ddot{x}_1 \\ \ddot{x}_2 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} k + k_c & -k_c \\ -k_c & k + k_c \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \tag{35}$$

### 6.2 The Eigenvalue Problem and Normal Frequencies
Assuming synchronous harmonic motion $\mathbf{x}(t) = \mathbf{a} e^{i\omega t}$:

$$(\mathbf{K} - \omega^2 \mathbf{M}) \mathbf{a} = \mathbf{0} \tag{36}$$

For non-trivial eigenvector solutions $\mathbf{a} \ne \mathbf{0}$, the secular determinant must vanish:

$$\det(\mathbf{K} - \omega^2 \mathbf{M}) = \begin{vmatrix} k + k_c - m\omega^2 & -k_c \\ -k_c & k + k_c - m\omega^2 \end{vmatrix} = 0 \tag{37}$$

$$(k + k_c - m\omega^2)^2 - k_c^2 = 0 \implies k + k_c - m\omega^2 = \pm k_c$$

This yields two distinct **normal mode frequencies**:

1. **Symmetric (In-Phase) Mode $\omega_1$**:
   $$m \omega_1^2 = k \implies \omega_1 = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} \tag{38}$$
   Eigenvector: $\mathbf{a}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$. Both masses move with identical amplitude in the same direction ($x_1(t) = x_2(t)$). The central coupling spring remains unstretched ($x_2 - x_1 = 0$)!
2. **Antisymmetric (Out-of-Phase) Mode $\omega_2$**:
   $$m \omega_2^2 = k + 2k_c \implies \omega_2 = \sqrt{\frac{k + 2k_c}{m}} \tag{39}$$
   Eigenvector: $\mathbf{a}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}$. The masses move in opposite directions ($x_1(t) = -x_2(t)$). The central spring experiences double deformation.

### 6.3 Normal Coordinates and Decoupling
Define normal coordinates $\eta_1, \eta_2$ as linear combinations of physical coordinates:

$$\eta_1(t) \equiv \frac{x_1(t) + x_2(t)}{2}, \qquad \eta_2(t) \equiv \frac{x_1(t) - x_2(t)}{2} \tag{40}$$

Adding and subtracting Equations (32) and (33):

$$\ddot{\eta}_1 + \omega_1^2 \eta_1 = 0, \qquad \ddot{\eta}_2 + \omega_2^2 \eta_2 = 0 \tag{41}$$

In normal coordinate space, **all cross-coupling vanishes**! The complex coupled system decouples into two completely independent, non-interacting simple harmonic oscillators.

### 6.4 Energy Exchange and the Beat Phenomenon
Suppose we pull mass 1 to initial displacement $x_0$ while holding mass 2 at equilibrium, releasing both from rest: $x_1(0) = x_0, x_2(0) = 0, \dot{x}_1(0) = \dot{x}_2(0) = 0$.

Decomposing into normal modes:
$$\eta_1(0) = \frac{x_0}{2}, \quad \eta_2(0) = \frac{x_0}{2}$$

$$\eta_1(t) = \frac{x_0}{2} \cos(\omega_1 t), \quad \eta_2(t) = \frac{x_0}{2} \cos(\omega_2 t)$$

Transforming back to physical coordinates ($x_1 = \eta_1 + \eta_2$, $x_2 = \eta_1 - \eta_2$):

$$x_1(t) = x_0 \cos\left( \frac{\omega_2 - \omega_1}{2} t \right) \cos\left( \frac{\omega_1 + \omega_2}{2} t \right) \tag{42}$$

$$x_2(t) = x_0 \sin\left( \frac{\omega_2 - \omega_1}{2} t \right) \sin\left( \frac{\omega_1 + \omega_2}{2} t \right) \tag{43}$$

When the coupling is weak ($k_c \ll k$), $\omega_1 \approx \omega_2$:
* The fast oscillation occurs at average carrier frequency $\bar{\omega} = \frac{\omega_1 + \omega_2}{2}$.
* The amplitude is modulated by a slow envelope oscillating at the **beat frequency** $\omega_{\text{beat}} = |\omega_2 - \omega_1|$.
* Complete energy sloshing occurs: mass 1 gradually stops as all its kinetic and potential energy transfers into mass 2; then mass 2 transfers its energy back into mass 1!

## 7. The Continuum Limit: Transition from Coupled Oscillators to Wave Mechanics

How does discrete Newtonian mechanics give rise to continuum partial differential equations like the seismic wave equation?

Consider a 1D lattice consisting of $N$ identical point masses $m$ arranged in a line with uniform lattice spacing $a$. Each mass $u_j(t)$ is connected to its nearest neighbors by springs of stiffness $k$.

Applying Newton's second law to the $j$-th interior mass:

$$m \ddot{u}_j = k (u_{j+1} - u_j) - k (u_j - u_{j-1}) = k (u_{j+1} - 2u_j + u_{j-1}) \tag{44}$$

Now define the macroscopic physical properties of the rod:
* Total length $L = N a$.
* Linear mass density $\rho = m / a \implies m = \rho a$.
* Elastic modulus / rod tension $T = k a \implies k = T / a$.

Substituting these definitions into Equation (44):

$$\rho a \frac{\partial^2 u_j}{\partial t^2} = \left(\frac{T}{a}\right) \left[ u_{j+1} - 2u_j + u_{j-1} \right]$$

Dividing by $a$:

$$\rho \frac{\partial^2 u_j}{\partial t^2} = T \left[ \frac{u_{j+1} - 2u_j + u_{j-1}}{a^2} \right] \tag{45}$$

Now take the **continuum limit** as lattice spacing $a \to 0$ and number of masses $N \to \infty$, such that the discrete coordinate $j a \to x$:

$$\lim_{a \to 0} \frac{u(x + a) - 2u(x) + u(x - a)}{a^2} = \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} \tag{46}$$

Equation (45) becomes:

$$\rho \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial t^2} = T \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} \implies \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial t^2} = v^2 \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} \tag{47}$$

where the acoustic wave propagation velocity is:

$$v = \sqrt{\frac{T}{\rho}} \tag{48}$$

> **Foundational Mathematical Bridge:**  
> The 1D continuous acoustic/seismic wave equation is **the exact infinite-dimensional continuum limit of a chain of coupled harmonic oscillators**. The discrete normal modes $\omega_n$ of the $N$-mass chain become the continuous standing Fourier wave modes of the continuum rod!
