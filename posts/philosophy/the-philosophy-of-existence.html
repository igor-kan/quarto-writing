The Philosophy of Existence and Ontology: From Heidegger to the Modern World

An exploration of why existence matters to humans, existentialism, Martin Heidegger’s concept of Dasein, and the formal study of ontology.

Existence is interesting philosophically because it asks the ultimate mystery: why is there something rather than nothing? It matters humanly because we possess the unique power to evaluate our own worth and choose who we become. We are not just born with a pre-written script; we are forced to define our own purpose.

This realization—that we are entirely free and lack a cosmic rulebook—causes what philosophers like Søren Kierkegaard called the “dizziness of freedom” (or Angst).

Existentialism and Martin Heidegger

Historically, many philosophers treated humans as detached scientists observing a separate universe. Martin Heidegger completely flipped this script. He argued that we cannot separate ourselves from the world.

Heidegger rejected the classic Cartesian idea of “I think, therefore I am.” Instead, he introduced the concept of Dasein (Being-There), consisting of three profound shifts:

  1. Being-in-the-world: You are not a ghost trapped in a flesh machine. You exist first through doing—your relationships, your environment, and your daily habits—long before you pause to think about it.
  2. Thrownness (Geworfenheit): You did not choose to exist. You were “thrown” into a specific time, country, culture, and family without your consent. Your starting point in life is entirely an accident of birth.
  3. Being-toward-death: What makes human existence unique is that we know it will end. Facing the absolute certainty of death causes intense existential anxiety. However, this anxiety is a gift: it snaps us out of mindless conformity and forces us to live authentically on our own terms.

How Other Philosophers Tackle Existence

When you look beyond Heidegger, existence becomes a fascinating battleground of different perspectives:

  • Jean-Paul Sartre (Radical Freedom): We are “condemned to be free.” Because there is no pre-built human nature, you are entirely defined by your actions. Making excuses for your life is what Sartre calls “bad faith.”
  • Albert Camus (The Absurd): Human existence is absurd because we desperately crave meaning from a cold, silent universe that has none to give. The ultimate human triumph is to accept this meaninglessness and live passionately anyway.
  • Friedrich Nietzsche (The Will to Power): Existence is an active landscape of growth and self-mastery. Instead of looking for a cosmic purpose, humans must invent their own values and strive to become the best versions of themselves (the Übermensch).
  • Nishida Kitaro (Absolute Nothingness): East Asian philosophers often bridge Western thought with Zen Buddhism. Nishida argued that our individual existence isn’t a lonely island; we arise from a deep, interconnected void of “nothingness” that links all living things.

The Philosophy of Existence: Ontology

While existentialism asks, “What does my personal life mean?”, Ontology asks the more fundamental question: “What does it mean for anything to exist?”

Ontology is the formal branch of philosophy that studies existence itself. It is the core foundation of metaphysics, examining the building blocks of reality. Ontologists group everything into categories to figure out what is fundamentally “real” and what is just a byproduct.

The Big Debates: What Actually Exists?

  • Materialism vs. Idealism: Materialists argue that only physical matter (atoms, energy, brains) truly exists. Idealists argue that reality is fundamentally mental, meaning physical things only exist because a mind perceives them.
  • The Problem of Universals: Does the color “red” or the concept of “justice” exist out there in the universe, or are they just labels humans invented? Plato famously argued that abstract concepts (Forms) are more real than the physical objects we see.
  • Abstract vs. Concrete Entities: Do numbers, mathematical laws, or fictional characters “exist”? Ontologists debate whether things must have a physical location in space and time to count as real.

How Ontology Changed Over Time

The way philosophers map existence has shifted dramatically through history:

  • Aristotle (Ancient Greece): Categorization. He classified reality into substances (like a tree) and properties (like being brown). To exist is to be a substance with specific traits.
  • René Descartes (17th Century): Dualism. He split all existence into two strict substances: physical matter (res extensa) and thinking mind (res cogitans).
  • Immanuel Kant (18th Century): The Human Filter. He argued we can never know reality exactly as it is (the “thing-in-itself”). We only know existence through the built-in filters of the human mind, like space and time.
  • W.V.O. Quine (20th Century): Scientific Ontological Commitment. He famously stated: “To be is to be the value of a variable.” In plain English: whatever our best scientific theories require to be true is what exists.

Why This Matters to You Tomorrow Morning

These philosophical frameworks aren’t just academic exercises. They dictate how you view your daily reality and the choices you make.

From Sartre, you realize you hold the pen—you can’t blame your past for your current choices. From Heidegger, remembering your limited time pushes you to drop superficial drama and invest in what truly matters.

Ontologically, it shapes your view of the mind. If your ontology is materialist, you view love and anxiety as chemical reactions. If you adopt a relational ontology (like Heidegger or Eastern philosophies), you stop viewing yourself as an isolated ego and realize you are woven into the very fabric of existence.
