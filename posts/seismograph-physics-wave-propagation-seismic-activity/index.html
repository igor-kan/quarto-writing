1. Introduction: Probing Earth’s Interior Through Inertial Mechanics

How can an instrument firmly bolted to the Earth measure the movement of the very ground beneath it?

Because every physical object attached to the Earth moves with the ground, an observer cannot measure ground displacement directly against an external fixed reference frame. Seismometry solves this fundamental problem through the physics of inertia: suspending a proof mass that resists motion when the instrument frame accelerates.

By combining the mechanics of damped driven harmonic oscillators with continuum elastodynamics, seismologists can not only detect earthquakes thousands of kilometers away, but also image the internal layer structure of our planet—including the molten liquid outer core and the solid inner core.

2. Physics of the Seismometer: Sensor Mechanics

2.1 The Damped Driven Inertial Pendulum

Consider a classic vertical seismometer consisting of: 1. A rigid instrument housing and frame securely anchored to bedrock, moving with ground displacement \(u_g(t)\). 2. An internal proof mass \(m\) suspended by a spring with stiffness \(k\) and a viscous dashpot with damping coefficient \(c\).

Let \(x(t)\) denote the absolute displacement of the proof mass relative to an inertial reference frame, and let:

\[z(t) \equiv x(t) - u_g(t) \tag{1}\]

denote the relative displacement of the mass with respect to the instrument casing. The sensor records only this relative displacement \(z(t)\).

Applying Newton’s second law to the mass \(m\):

\[m \ddot{x} = -k(x - u_g) - c(\dot{x} - \dot{u}_g) \tag{2}\]

Substituting \(x(t) = z(t) + u_g(t)\), so \(\ddot{x} = \ddot{z} + \ddot{u}_g\):

\[m(\ddot{z} + \ddot{u}_g) = -k z - c \dot{z} \implies m \ddot{z} + c \dot{z} + k z = -m \ddot{u}_g(t) \tag{3}\]

Dividing by \(m\) and introducing the natural frequency \(\omega_0 = \sqrt{k/m}\) and damping ratio \(\zeta = \frac{c}{2\sqrt{km}} = \frac{c}{2m\omega_0}\):

\[\ddot{z}(t) + 2\zeta \omega_0 \dot{z}(t) + \omega_0^2 z(t) = -\ddot{u}_g(t) \tag{4}\]

Key Physical Insight:
The inertial mass is driven not by ground displacement \(u_g(t)\) or velocity \(\dot{u}_g(t)\), but strictly by ground acceleration \(\ddot{u}_g(t)\). The minus sign reflects inertial resistance: when the ground accelerates upward, the mass lags behind, compressing the spring.

2.2 Frequency Response and Operational Regimes

Assuming harmonic ground motion \(u_g(t) = U_0 e^{i\omega t}\) (so \(\ddot{u}_g = -\omega^2 U_0 e^{i\omega t}\)), the steady-state relative displacement is \(z(t) = Z_0 e^{i\omega t}\). Substituting into Equation (4):

\[(-\omega^2 + 2i\zeta \omega_0 \omega + \omega_0^2) Z_0 = \omega^2 U_0\]

The complex transfer function relating relative sensor displacement to ground displacement is:

\[H(\omega) = \frac{Z_0(\omega)}{U_0(\omega)} = \frac{\omega^2}{\omega_0^2 - \omega^2 + 2i\zeta \omega_0 \omega} = \frac{\left(\frac{\omega}{\omega_0}\right)^2}{1 - \left(\frac{\omega}{\omega_0}\right)^2 + 2i\zeta \left(\frac{\omega}{\omega_0}\right)} \tag{5}\]

Defining the normalized frequency ratio \(u = \omega / \omega_0\):

\[|H(\omega)| = \frac{u^2}{\sqrt{(1 - u^2)^2 + (2\zeta u)^2}}, \qquad \phi(\omega) = \arctan\left( \frac{2\zeta u}{1 - u^2} \right) \tag{6}\]

Equation (6) reveals three distinct operational regimes:

  1. Displacement Seismometer (\(\omega \gg \omega_0\), \(u \gg 1\)):
    When ground motion frequency is much higher than the natural frequency, \(u^2\) dominates: \[\lim_{u \to \infty} |H(\omega)| = 1 \implies z(t) \approx -u_g(t)\] The proof mass remains stationary in the inertial frame while the ground moves around it. The recorded signal directly tracks ground displacement. This requires a very soft spring (long period pendulum, \(T_0 > 20\text{ s}\)).
  2. Velocity Seismometer / Geophone (\(\omega \approx \omega_0\)):
    In moving-coil electromagnetic geophones, the induced voltage \(V(t) \propto \dot{z}(t)\) is proportional to ground velocity \(\dot{u}_g(t)\) over a wide band.
  3. Accelerometer (\(\omega \ll \omega_0\), \(u \ll 1\)):
    When the ground motion frequency is much lower than the sensor’s natural frequency: \[|H(\omega)| \approx \frac{\omega^2}{\omega_0^2} \implies z(t) \approx -\frac{\ddot{u}_g(t)}{\omega_0^2}\] Relative displacement is directly proportional to ground acceleration. Accelerometers require a very stiff spring (high natural frequency, \(f_0 > 50\text{ Hz}\)).

2.3 The Optimal Damping Factor (\(\zeta = 1/\sqrt{2} \approx 0.707\))

Why do modern seismometers employ damping ratio \(\zeta \approx 0.707\)? * If underdamped (\(\zeta < 0.5\)), the system resonates violently near \(\omega = \omega_0\), ringing long after the wave passes and corrupting subsequent wave arrivals. * If overdamped (\(\zeta > 1\)), high-frequency sensitivity is heavily attenuated. * At critical damping \(\zeta = 1/\sqrt{2} \approx 0.707\) (the maximally flat Butterworth response), amplitude response is flat across the broadest frequency band with linear phase distortion.

3. Elastodynamics and Seismic Wave Propagation

When a tectonic fault ruptures, stored elastic strain energy is released as seismic waves propagating through the Earth’s interior and along its surface.

3.1 The Navier-Cauchy Equation of Motion

In an isotropic, linearly elastic continuum, the balance of momentum yields the Navier-Cauchy equation:

\[\rho \frac{\partial^2 \mathbf{u}}{\partial t^2} = (\lambda + \mu)\nabla(\nabla \cdot \mathbf{u}) + \mu \nabla^2 \mathbf{u} \tag{7}\]

where \(\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x}, t)\) is the 3D displacement vector field, \(\rho\) is rock density, and \(\lambda, \mu\) are Lamé’s elastic moduli (\(\mu\) is the shear modulus or rigidity).

3.2 Helmholtz Decomposition: \(P\) and \(S\) Body Waves

By the Helmholtz theorem, any smooth vector field \(\mathbf{u}\) can be decomposed into a curl-free scalar potential \(\Phi\) and a divergence-free vector potential \(\boldsymbol{\Psi}\):

\[\mathbf{u} = \nabla \Phi + \nabla \times \boldsymbol{\Psi}, \quad \text{with } \nabla \cdot \boldsymbol{\Psi} = 0 \tag{8}\]

Substituting Equation (8) into (7) decouples the elastodynamic field into two independent wave equations:

1. Primary Compressional Waves (\(P\)-Waves)

\[\nabla^2 \Phi = \frac{1}{v_P^2} \frac{\partial^2 \Phi}{\partial t^2}, \qquad v_P = \sqrt{\frac{\lambda + 2\mu}{\rho}} = \sqrt{\frac{K + \frac{4}{3}\mu}{\rho}} \tag{9}\] * Particle Motion: Longitudinal (alternating compression and dilation parallel to wave propagation direction). * Speed: Fastest seismic wave (\(\sim 5.5–8.0\text{ km/s}\) in crust, \(>13\text{ km/s}\) in deep mantle). * Propagation Media: Solids, liquids, and gases (since bulk modulus \(K > 0\)).

2. Secondary Shear Waves (\(S\)-Waves)

\[\nabla^2 \boldsymbol{\Psi} = \frac{1}{v_S^2} \frac{\partial^2 \boldsymbol{\Psi}}{\partial t^2}, \qquad v_S = \sqrt{\frac{\mu}{\rho}} \tag{10}\] * Particle Motion: Transverse shear perpendicular to wave propagation direction (polarized into vertical \(SV\) and horizontal \(SH\) components). * Speed: Slower than \(P\)-waves (\(\sim 3.0–4.8\text{ km/s}\) in crust). * Fluid Inability: Fluids possess zero shear modulus (\(\mu = 0 \implies v_S = 0\)). In 1906, Richard Dixon Oldham proved that the Earth possesses a liquid outer core by observing the global shadow zone where direct \(S\)-waves completely vanish!

The Universal Wave Speed Ratio

For a standard Poisson solid (where Poisson’s ratio \(\nu = 0.25\), so \(\lambda = \mu\)):

\[\frac{v_P}{v_S} = \sqrt{\frac{\lambda + 2\mu}{\mu}} = \sqrt{\frac{3\mu}{\mu}} = \sqrt{3} \approx 1.732 \tag{11}\]

\(P\)-waves consistently travel roughly \(1.73\) times faster than \(S\)-waves!

3.3 Surface Waves: Rayleigh and Love

At the free surface boundary (\(z = 0\)), boundary conditions trap acoustic energy into dispersive surface waves whose amplitude decays exponentially with depth: 1. Rayleigh Waves: Result from coupled \(P-SV\) interference at the free surface. Particle motion is retrograde elliptical in the vertical plane. Propagation speed \(v_R \approx 0.92 v_S\). 2. Love Waves: Horizontally polarized shear waves (\(SH\)) trapped by total internal reflection in a low-velocity surface layer over a higher-velocity halfspace. Because surface waves spread in 2D (\(1/r\) geometrical spreading) rather than 3D (\(1/r^2\)), they carry the largest amplitudes and cause the catastrophic destruction in major earthquakes.

3.4 Epicenter Triangulation via the \(S-P\) Travel-Time Lag

Because \(v_P > v_S\), the time delay between the initial arrival of the \(P\)-wave (\(t_P\)) and the \(S\)-wave (\(t_S\)) increases linearly with distance \(\Delta\) from the hypocenter:

\[\Delta t_{S-P} = t_S - t_P = \frac{\Delta}{v_S} - \frac{\Delta}{v_P} = \Delta \left( \frac{v_P - v_S}{v_P v_S} \right)\]

\[\Delta = \left( \frac{v_P v_S}{v_P - v_S} \right) \Delta t_{S-P} \tag{12}\]

For typical crustal velocities (\(v_P \approx 6.0\text{ km/s}, v_S \approx 3.5\text{ km/s}\)): \[\Delta \approx 8.4\text{ km/s} \times \Delta t_{S-P}\] A single seismograph provides the distance \(\Delta\); three intersecting circles from three independent stations pinpoint the unique geographic epicenter!

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

t = np.linspace(0, 100, 2500)
seis = np.zeros_like(t)

np.random.seed(42)
noise = 0.035 * np.random.randn(len(t))

# P-wave arrival at t = 15 s (high frequency ~3 Hz, sharp onset)
t_P = 15.0
mask_P = (t >= t_P) & (t <= 55)
env_P = np.exp(-(t[mask_P] - t_P) / 6.0)
seis[mask_P] += 0.35 * env_P * np.sin(2 * np.pi * 3.0 * (t[mask_P] - t_P))

# S-wave arrival at t = 32 s (intermediate frequency ~1.2 Hz, larger amplitude)
t_S = 32.0
mask_S = (t >= t_S) & (t <= 75)
env_S = np.exp(-(t[mask_S] - t_S) / 10.0)
seis[mask_S] += 0.95 * env_S * np.sin(2 * np.pi * 1.2 * (t[mask_S] - t_S) + 0.5)

# Surface wave arrival at t = 48 s (low frequency ~0.4 Hz, massive amplitude)
t_Surf = 48.0
mask_Surf = (t >= t_Surf) & (t <= 95)
env_Surf = np.exp(-(t[mask_Surf] - t_Surf) / 15.0)
seis[mask_Surf] += 2.30 * env_Surf * np.sin(2 * np.pi * 0.4 * (t[mask_Surf] - t_Surf) + 1.2)

seis += noise

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(10, 4.2), dpi=180)

4. Fault Rupture Mechanics, Seismic Moment, and Magnitude Scaling

4.1 Elastic Rebound Theory and Seismic Moment \(M_0\)

In 1910, following the 1906 San Francisco earthquake, Harry Fielding Reid formulated Elastic Rebound Theory: tectonic plates steadily accumulate elastic shear strain along locked frictional faults until static friction is overcome, releasing stored strain in a sudden dynamic rupture.

The fundamental physical measure of an earthquake’s size is the scalar seismic moment \(M_0\), representing the mechanical torque work done by the fault dislocation:

\[M_0 = \mu A \bar{D} \tag{13}\]

where: * \(\mu\) is the shear modulus of crustal rock (\(\approx 3 \times 10^{10}\text{ Pa}\)). * \(A = L \times W\) is the fault rupture surface area (length \(\times\) width down-dip). * \(\bar{D}\) is the average co-seismic dislocation slip along the fault.

\(M_0\) carries exact physical dimensions of energy / torque (\([M_0] = \text{N}\cdot\text{m} = M L^2 T^{-2}\)).

4.2 The Moment Magnitude Scale (\(M_w\))

Charles Richter’s original 1935 magnitude scale (\(M_L\)) measured peak trace amplitude on a Wood-Anderson seismograph. However, for massive earthquakes (\(M > 7.5\)), \(M_L\) suffers from severe instrumental saturation: rupture dimensions exceed the 1-second period of the seismometer, making a magnitude 8.5 quake look no larger than a 7.5.

In 1979, Thomas C. Hanks and Hiroo Kanamori defined the modern Moment Magnitude Scale (\(M_w\)), tied directly to the physical seismic moment without saturation:

\[M_w = \frac{2}{3} \log_{10}(M_0) - 6.07 \quad (M_0 \text{ in }\mathrm{N}\cdot\mathrm{m}) \tag{14}\]

4.3 Exponential Energy Scaling Laws

Because \(M_w \propto \frac{2}{3} \log_{10} M_0\), rearranging Equation (14) shows that seismic moment scales with an exponent of \(1.5\):

\[M_0 = 10^{\frac{3}{2} M_w + 9.1} \implies \frac{M_0(M_w + 1)}{M_0(M_w)} = 10^{1.5} \approx 31.62 \tag{15}\]

  • Increase of 1 Magnitude Unit: \(\Delta M_w = 1 \implies 10^{1.5} \approx \mathbf{31.62\times}\) greater energy release.
  • Increase of 2 Magnitude Units: \(\Delta M_w = 2 \implies 10^{3.0} = \mathbf{1000\times}\) greater energy release!

4.4 The Gutenberg-Richter Recurrence Law

Globally, the frequency of earthquakes follows a self-similar power-law distribution known as the Gutenberg-Richter Law:

\[\log_{10} N = a - b M \tag{16}\]

where \(N\) is the cumulative number of earthquakes having magnitude \(\ge M\), \(a\) reflects the regional tectonic activity level, and \(b\) is a universal physical constant typically very close to \(1.0\). * When \(b = 1\), for every magnitude 7 earthquake, there occur approximately 10 magnitude 6 quakes, 100 magnitude 5 quakes, and 1,000 magnitude 4 quakes!

How can an instrument firmly bolted to the Earth measure the movement of the very ground beneath it?

Seismometry solves this fundamental problem through the physics of **inertia**: suspending a proof mass that resists motion when the instrument frame accelerates.

By combining the mechanics of damped driven harmonic oscillators with continuum elastodynamics, seismologists can not only detect earthquakes thousands of kilometers away, but also image the internal layer structure of our planet—including the molten liquid outer core and the solid inner core.

## 2. Physics of the Seismometer: Sensor Mechanics

### 2.1 The Damped Driven Inertial Pendulum

Consider a classic vertical seismometer consisting of:
1. A rigid instrument housing and frame securely anchored to bedrock, moving with ground displacement $u_g(t)$.
2. An internal proof mass $m$ suspended by a spring with stiffness $k$ and a viscous dashpot with damping coefficient $c$.

Let $x(t)$ denote the absolute displacement of the proof mass relative to an inertial reference frame, and let:

$$z(t) \equiv x(t) - u_g(t) \tag{1}$$

denote the **relative displacement** of the mass with respect to the instrument casing. The sensor records only this relative displacement $z(t)$.

Applying Newton's second law to the mass $m$:

$$m \ddot{x} = -k(x - u_g) - c(\dot{x} - \dot{u}_g) \tag{2}$$

Substituting $x(t) = z(t) + u_g(t)$, so $\ddot{x} = \ddot{z} + \ddot{u}_g$:

$$m(\ddot{z} + \ddot{u}_g) = -k z - c \dot{z} \implies m \ddot{z} + c \dot{z} + k z = -m \ddot{u}_g(t) \tag{3}$$

Dividing by $m$ and introducing the natural frequency $\omega_0 = \sqrt{k/m}$ and damping ratio $\zeta = \frac{c}{2\sqrt{km}} = \frac{c}{2m\omega_0}$:

$$\ddot{z}(t) + 2\zeta \omega_0 \dot{z}(t) + \omega_0^2 z(t) = -\ddot{u}_g(t) \tag{4}$$

> **Key Physical Insight:**  
> The inertial mass is driven **not by ground displacement $u_g(t)$ or velocity $\dot{u}_g(t)$, but strictly by ground acceleration $\ddot{u}_g(t)$**. The minus sign reflects inertial resistance: when the ground accelerates upward, the mass lags behind, compressing the spring.

### 2.2 Frequency Response and Operational Regimes

Assuming harmonic ground motion $u_g(t) = U_0 e^{i\omega t}$ (so $\ddot{u}_g = -\omega^2 U_0 e^{i\omega t}$), the steady-state relative displacement is $z(t) = Z_0 e^{i\omega t}$. Substituting into Equation (4):

$$(-\omega^2 + 2i\zeta \omega_0 \omega + \omega_0^2) Z_0 = \omega^2 U_0$$

The complex transfer function relating relative sensor displacement to ground displacement is:

$$H(\omega) = \frac{Z_0(\omega)}{U_0(\omega)} = \frac{\omega^2}{\omega_0^2 - \omega^2 + 2i\zeta \omega_0 \omega} = \frac{\left(\frac{\omega}{\omega_0}\right)^2}{1 - \left(\frac{\omega}{\omega_0}\right)^2 + 2i\zeta \left(\frac{\omega}{\omega_0}\right)} \tag{5}$$

Defining the normalized frequency ratio $u = \omega / \omega_0$:

$$|H(\omega)| = \frac{u^2}{\sqrt{(1 - u^2)^2 + (2\zeta u)^2}}, \qquad \phi(\omega) = \arctan\left( \frac{2\zeta u}{1 - u^2} \right) \tag{6}$$

Equation (6) reveals three distinct operational regimes:

1. **Displacement Seismometer ($\omega \gg \omega_0$, $u \gg 1$):**  
   When ground motion frequency is much higher than the natural frequency, $u^2$ dominates:
   $$\lim_{u \to \infty} |H(\omega)| = 1 \implies z(t) \approx -u_g(t)$$
   The proof mass remains stationary in the inertial frame while the ground moves around it. The recorded signal directly tracks **ground displacement**. This requires a very soft spring (long period pendulum, $T_0 > 20\text{ s}$).
2. **Velocity Seismometer / Geophone ($\omega \approx \omega_0$):**  
   In moving-coil electromagnetic geophones, the induced voltage $V(t) \propto \dot{z}(t)$ is proportional to **ground velocity** $\dot{u}_g(t)$ over a wide band.
3. **Accelerometer ($\omega \ll \omega_0$, $u \ll 1$):**  
   When the ground motion frequency is much lower than the sensor's natural frequency:
   $$|H(\omega)| \approx \frac{\omega^2}{\omega_0^2} \implies z(t) \approx -\frac{\ddot{u}_g(t)}{\omega_0^2}$$
   Relative displacement is directly proportional to **ground acceleration**. Accelerometers require a very stiff spring (high natural frequency, $f_0 > 50\text{ Hz}$).

### 2.3 The Optimal Damping Factor ($\zeta = 1/\sqrt{2} \approx 0.707$)
Why do modern seismometers employ damping ratio $\zeta \approx 0.707$?
* If underdamped ($\zeta < 0.5$), the system resonates violently near $\omega = \omega_0$, ringing long after the wave passes and corrupting subsequent wave arrivals.
* If overdamped ($\zeta > 1$), high-frequency sensitivity is heavily attenuated.
* At critical damping $\zeta = 1/\sqrt{2} \approx 0.707$ (the maximally flat Butterworth response), amplitude response is flat across the broadest frequency band with linear phase distortion.

## 3. Elastodynamics and Seismic Wave Propagation

When a tectonic fault ruptures, stored elastic strain energy is released as seismic waves propagating through the Earth's interior and along its surface.

### 3.1 The Navier-Cauchy Equation of Motion
In an isotropic, linearly elastic continuum, the balance of momentum yields the **Navier-Cauchy equation**:

$$\rho \frac{\partial^2 \mathbf{u}}{\partial t^2} = (\lambda + \mu)\nabla(\nabla \cdot \mathbf{u}) + \mu \nabla^2 \mathbf{u} \tag{7}$$

where $\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x}, t)$ is the 3D displacement vector field, $\rho$ is rock density, and $\lambda, \mu$ are Lamé's elastic moduli ($\mu$ is the shear modulus or rigidity).

### 3.2 Helmholtz Decomposition: $P$ and $S$ Body Waves
By the Helmholtz theorem, any smooth vector field $\mathbf{u}$ can be decomposed into a curl-free scalar potential $\Phi$ and a divergence-free vector potential $\boldsymbol{\Psi}$:

$$\mathbf{u} = \nabla \Phi + \nabla \times \boldsymbol{\Psi}, \quad \text{with } \nabla \cdot \boldsymbol{\Psi} = 0 \tag{8}$$

Substituting Equation (8) into (7) decouples the elastodynamic field into two independent wave equations:

#### 1. Primary Compressional Waves ($P$-Waves)
$$\nabla^2 \Phi = \frac{1}{v_P^2} \frac{\partial^2 \Phi}{\partial t^2}, \qquad v_P = \sqrt{\frac{\lambda + 2\mu}{\rho}} = \sqrt{\frac{K + \frac{4}{3}\mu}{\rho}} \tag{9}$$
* **Particle Motion:** Longitudinal (alternating compression and dilation parallel to wave propagation direction).
* **Speed:** Fastest seismic wave ($\sim 5.5–8.0\text{ km/s}$ in crust, $>13\text{ km/s}$ in deep mantle).
* **Propagation Media:** Solids, liquids, and gases (since bulk modulus $K > 0$).

#### 2. Secondary Shear Waves ($S$-Waves)
$$\nabla^2 \boldsymbol{\Psi} = \frac{1}{v_S^2} \frac{\partial^2 \boldsymbol{\Psi}}{\partial t^2}, \qquad v_S = \sqrt{\frac{\mu}{\rho}} \tag{10}$$
* **Particle Motion:** Transverse shear perpendicular to wave propagation direction (polarized into vertical $SV$ and horizontal $SH$ components).
* **Speed:** Slower than $P$-waves ($\sim 3.0–4.8\text{ km/s}$ in crust).
* **Fluid Inability:** Fluids possess zero shear modulus ($\mu = 0 \implies v_S = 0$). In 1906, Richard Dixon Oldham proved that the Earth possesses a **liquid outer core** by observing the global shadow zone where direct $S$-waves completely vanish!

#### The Universal Wave Speed Ratio
For a standard **Poisson solid** (where Poisson's ratio $\nu = 0.25$, so $\lambda = \mu$):

$$\frac{v_P}{v_S} = \sqrt{\frac{\lambda + 2\mu}{\mu}} = \sqrt{\frac{3\mu}{\mu}} = \sqrt{3} \approx 1.732 \tag{11}$$

$P$-waves consistently travel roughly $1.73$ times faster than $S$-waves!

### 3.3 Surface Waves: Rayleigh and Love
At the free surface boundary ($z = 0$), boundary conditions trap acoustic energy into dispersive surface waves whose amplitude decays exponentially with depth:
1. **Rayleigh Waves:** Result from coupled $P-SV$ interference at the free surface. Particle motion is **retrograde elliptical** in the vertical plane. Propagation speed $v_R \approx 0.92 v_S$.
2. **Love Waves:** Horizontally polarized shear waves ($SH$) trapped by total internal reflection in a low-velocity surface layer over a higher-velocity halfspace.
Because surface waves spread in 2D ($1/r$ geometrical spreading) rather than 3D ($1/r^2$), they carry the largest amplitudes and cause the catastrophic destruction in major earthquakes.

### 3.4 Epicenter Triangulation via the $S-P$ Travel-Time Lag

Because $v_P > v_S$, the time delay between the initial arrival of the $P$-wave ($t_P$) and the $S$-wave ($t_S$) increases linearly with distance $\Delta$ from the hypocenter:

$$\Delta t_{S-P} = t_S - t_P = \frac{\Delta}{v_S} - \frac{\Delta}{v_P} = \Delta \left( \frac{v_P - v_S}{v_P v_S} \right)$$

$$\Delta = \left( \frac{v_P v_S}{v_P - v_S} \right) \Delta t_{S-P} \tag{12}$$

For typical crustal velocities ($v_P \approx 6.0\text{ km/s}, v_S \approx 3.5\text{ km/s}$):
$$\Delta \approx 8.4\text{ km/s} \times \Delta t_{S-P}$$
A single seismograph provides the distance $\Delta$; three intersecting circles from three independent stations pinpoint the unique geographic epicenter!

```{python}
#| label: fig-synthetic-seismogram
#| fig-cap: "Programmatically Synthesized 3-Phase Seismogram. Shows sequential arrival of high-frequency compressional P-waves (blue), shear S-waves (red), and high-amplitude dispersive surface waves (orange). The S-P time lag (purple) enables precise epicenter triangulation."
#| code-fold: true
#| code-summary: "Show Python code generating the Synthetic Seismogram"

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

t = np.linspace(0, 100, 2500)
seis = np.zeros_like(t)

np.random.seed(42)
noise = 0.035 * np.random.randn(len(t))

# P-wave arrival at t = 15 s (high frequency ~3 Hz, sharp onset)
t_P = 15.0
mask_P = (t >= t_P) & (t <= 55)
env_P = np.exp(-(t[mask_P] - t_P) / 6.0)
seis[mask_P] += 0.35 * env_P * np.sin(2 * np.pi * 3.0 * (t[mask_P] - t_P))

# S-wave arrival at t = 32 s (intermediate frequency ~1.2 Hz, larger amplitude)
t_S = 32.0
mask_S = (t >= t_S) & (t <= 75)
env_S = np.exp(-(t[mask_S] - t_S) / 10.0)
seis[mask_S] += 0.95 * env_S * np.sin(2 * np.pi * 1.2 * (t[mask_S] - t_S) + 0.5)

# Surface wave arrival at t = 48 s (low frequency ~0.4 Hz, massive amplitude)
t_Surf = 48.0
mask_Surf = (t >= t_Surf) & (t <= 95)
env_Surf = np.exp(-(t[mask_Surf] - t_Surf) / 15.0)
seis[mask_Surf] += 2.30 * env_Surf * np.sin(2 * np.pi * 0.4 * (t[mask_Surf] - t_Surf) + 1.2)

seis += noise

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(10, 4.2), dpi=180)
ax.plot(t, seis, color='#0f172a', lw=1.1)

# Phase arrival markers
ax.axvline(t_P, color='#2563eb', linestyle='--', lw=1.8, label=f'$P$-wave Arrival ($t_P = {t_P:.0f}$ s)')
ax.axvline(t_S, color='#dc2626', linestyle='--', lw=1.8, label=f'$S$-wave Arrival ($t_S = {t_S:.0f}$ s)')
ax.axvline(t_Surf, color='#d97706', linestyle='--', lw=1.8, label=f'Surface Waves ($t_R = {t_Surf:.0f}$ s)')

# S-P Lag annotation
ax.annotate('', xy=(t_S, 1.9), xytext=(t_P, 1.9),
            arrowprops=dict(arrowstyle='<->', color='#7c3aed', lw=2.0))
ax.text((t_P + t_S)/2, 2.2, f'$S-P$ Lag = {t_S - t_P:.0f} s\n($\\Delta \\propto \\Delta t_{{S-P}}$)',
        ha='center', fontsize=9.5, fontweight='bold', color='#7c3aed')

ax.set_xlim(0, 100)
ax.set_ylim(-3.2, 3.2)
ax.set_xlabel('Time elapsed since fault rupture $t$ (seconds)', fontsize=11)
ax.set_ylabel('Ground displacement $u_g(t)$ (arbitrary)', fontsize=11)
ax.set_title('Synthetic 3-Phase Seismogram (Vertical Component)', fontsize=12, fontweight='bold', pad=10)
ax.legend(frameon=True, fontsize=9, loc='lower left')
ax.grid(True, linestyle='--', alpha=0.45)

plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

## 4. Fault Rupture Mechanics, Seismic Moment, and Magnitude Scaling

### 4.1 Elastic Rebound Theory and Seismic Moment $M_0$
In 1910, following the 1906 San Francisco earthquake, Harry Fielding Reid formulated **Elastic Rebound Theory**: tectonic plates steadily accumulate elastic shear strain along locked frictional faults until static friction is overcome, releasing stored strain in a sudden dynamic rupture.

The fundamental physical measure of an earthquake's size is the **scalar seismic moment** $M_0$, representing the mechanical torque work done by the fault dislocation:

$$M_0 = \mu A \bar{D} \tag{13}$$

where:
* $\mu$ is the shear modulus of crustal rock ($\approx 3 \times 10^{10}\text{ Pa}$).
* $A = L \times W$ is the fault rupture surface area (length $\times$ width down-dip).
* $\bar{D}$ is the average co-seismic dislocation slip along the fault.

$M_0$ carries exact physical dimensions of energy / torque ($[M_0] = \text{N}\cdot\text{m} = M L^2 T^{-2}$).

### 4.2 The Moment Magnitude Scale ($M_w$)

Charles Richter's original 1935 magnitude scale ($M_L$) measured peak trace amplitude on a Wood-Anderson seismograph. However, for massive earthquakes ($M > 7.5$), $M_L$ suffers from severe **instrumental saturation**: rupture dimensions exceed the 1-second period of the seismometer, making a magnitude 8.5 quake look no larger than a 7.5.

In 1979, Thomas C. Hanks and Hiroo Kanamori defined the modern **Moment Magnitude Scale** ($M_w$), tied directly to the physical seismic moment without saturation:

$$M_w = \frac{2}{3} \log_{10}(M_0) - 6.07 \quad (M_0 \text{ in }\mathrm{N}\cdot\mathrm{m}) \tag{14}$$

### 4.3 Exponential Energy Scaling Laws
Because $M_w \propto \frac{2}{3} \log_{10} M_0$, rearranging Equation (14) shows that seismic moment scales with an exponent of $1.5$:

$$M_0 = 10^{\frac{3}{2} M_w + 9.1} \implies \frac{M_0(M_w + 1)}{M_0(M_w)} = 10^{1.5} \approx 31.62 \tag{15}$$

* **Increase of 1 Magnitude Unit:** $\Delta M_w = 1 \implies 10^{1.5} \approx \mathbf{31.62\times}$ greater energy release.
* **Increase of 2 Magnitude Units:** $\Delta M_w = 2 \implies 10^{3.0} = \mathbf{1000\times}$ greater energy release!

### 4.4 The Gutenberg-Richter Recurrence Law
Globally, the frequency of earthquakes follows a self-similar power-law distribution known as the **Gutenberg-Richter Law**:

$$\log_{10} N = a - b M \tag{16}$$

where $N$ is the cumulative number of earthquakes having magnitude $\ge M$, $a$ reflects the regional tectonic activity level, and $b$ is a universal physical constant typically very close to **$1.0$**.
* When $b = 1$, for every magnitude 7 earthquake, there occur approximately 10 magnitude 6 quakes, 100 magnitude 5 quakes, and 1,000 magnitude 4 quakes!
