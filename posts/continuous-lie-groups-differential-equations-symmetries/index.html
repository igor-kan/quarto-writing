1. Introduction: Sophus Lie’s Unification of Differential Equations

In the early 1830s, Évariste Galois established that polynomial equations could be understood and solved systematically by analyzing their discrete symmetry groups—the permutations of their roots. Half a century later, Norwegian mathematician Sophus Lie set out to achieve an equally grand unification for differential equations.

Before Lie, ordinary and partial differential equations (ODEs and PDEs) were taught as an uncoordinated catalog of ad-hoc tricks: * Integrating factors for first-order linear equations * Separation of variables * Homogeneous substitutions (\(y/x\)) * Bernoulli and Riccati transformations * Variation of parameters

Sophus Lie realized that all successful integration techniques share a single underlying mathematical mechanism: the differential equation admits a continuous group of symmetries.

Just as rotational symmetry simplifies a mechanical problem from three dimensions to one, a continuous Lie symmetry allows an analyst to: 1. Reduce the order of an ordinary differential equation by one (\(n \to n - 1\)). 2. Integrate a first-order non-linear ODE by quadrature through canonical coordinates. 3. Reduce a multi-variable PDE (such as the heat, wave, or Navier-Stokes equations) into an ODE in terms of self-similar invariant coordinates. 4. Establish conservation laws via Noether’s theorem. 5. Prove the necessity of dimensional homogeneity under measurement unit dilations.

2. Mathematical Foundations: Lie Groups and Lie Algebras

2.1 Definition of an Abstract Lie Group

A Lie group is an algebraic group that is simultaneously a smooth finite-dimensional manifold, such that the group operations are smooth (\(C^\infty\)) geometric maps:

Formal Definition:
A Lie group \((G, \cdot)\) is a smooth manifold \(G\) equipped with a group structure such that: 1. Multiplication: The group multiplication map \(\mu: G \times G \to G\), defined by \(\mu(g_1, g_2) = g_1 \cdot g_2\), is smooth (\(C^\infty\)). 2. Inversion: The group inversion map \(\iota: G \to G\), defined by \(\iota(g) = g^{-1}\), is smooth (\(C^\infty\)).

Canonical Examples of Lie Groups

  • The General Linear Group \(\mathrm{GL}(n, \mathbb{R})\): The open submanifold of \(\mathbb{R}^{n \times n}\) consisting of all invertible \(n \times n\) real matrices (\(\det A \neq 0\)). Dimension \(= n^2\).
  • The Special Orthogonal Group \(\mathrm{SO}(n)\): The subgroup of rotations in \(\mathbb{R}^n\) satisfying \(A^T A = I\) and \(\det A = 1\). Compact manifold of dimension \(\frac{n(n-1)}{2}\).
  • The Scale Dilation Group \((\mathbb{R}_{>0}^m, \cdot)\): The abelian Lie group of positive real scale factors \(\boldsymbol{\lambda} = (\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_m)\) under component-wise multiplication. This is the symmetry group governing dimensional analysis!

2.2 The Lie Algebra: Tangent Space at the Identity

Because a Lie group \(G\) is a smooth manifold, it possesses a tangent space at every point. The tangent space at the group identity element \(e \in G\), denoted: \[\mathfrak{g} = T_e G\] forms a vector space endowed with an alternating bilinear product called the Lie bracket \([\cdot, \cdot]: \mathfrak{g} \times \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{g}\).

A vector space \(\mathfrak{g}\) over a field \(\mathbb{F}\) equipped with a bracket \([\cdot, \cdot]\) is a Lie algebra if it satisfies: 1. Bilinearity: \([aX + bY, Z] = a[X, Z] + b[Y, Z]\) for all \(a, b \in \mathbb{F}\). 2. Alternating / Anti-symmetry: \([X, Y] = -[Y, X]\) (which implies \([X, X] = 0\)). 3. The Jacobi Identity: \[[X, [Y, Z]] + [Y, [Z, X]] + [Z, [X, Y]] = \mathbf{0} \tag{1}\]

For matrix Lie groups, the Lie bracket is simply the matrix commutator: \[[X, Y] = X Y - Y X \tag{2}\]

2.3 The Exponential Map

The bridge connecting the linear Lie algebra \(\mathfrak{g}\) (infinitesimal generators) to the non-linear Lie group manifold \(G\) (global transformations) is the exponential map: \[\exp: \mathfrak{g} \to G\] For any vector \(X \in \mathfrak{g}\), \(\exp(t X)\) defines the unique one-parameter subgroup \(\gamma(t)\) of \(G\) passing through the identity \(\gamma(0) = e\) with initial velocity vector \(\gamma'(0) = X\): \[\gamma(t_1 + t_2) = \gamma(t_1) \cdot \gamma(t_2) \implies \exp((t_1 + t_2)X) = \exp(t_1 X) \cdot \exp(t_2 X) \tag{3}\]

For matrix groups, \(\exp(tX)\) is given by the standard convergent matrix exponential series: \[\exp(tX) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{t^k X^k}{k!} = I + tX + \frac{t^2}{2!}X^2 + \dots \tag{4}\]

3. Continuous Lie Transformation Groups Acting on Spaces

In physical and geometric applications, Lie groups act as transformation groups on coordinate manifolds (e.g., Euclidean space \(\mathbb{R}^n\) or spacetime \(\mathbb{R}^4\)).

3.1 One-Parameter Transformation Groups

Let \(M \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n\) be an open domain. A one-parameter continuous Lie transformation group on \(M\) is a family of smooth coordinate transformations: \[\mathbf{x}^* = \mathbf{\Phi}(\mathbf{x}; \epsilon), \quad \mathbf{x} \in M, \; \epsilon \in (-\epsilon_0, \epsilon_0) \subset \mathbb{R} \tag{5}\] satisfying the group axioms: 1. Identity: \(\mathbf{\Phi}(\mathbf{x}; 0) = \mathbf{x}\) for all \(\mathbf{x} \in M\). 2. Closure / Group Law: \(\mathbf{\Phi}(\mathbf{\Phi}(\mathbf{x}; \epsilon_1); \epsilon_2) = \mathbf{\Phi}(\mathbf{x}; \epsilon_1 + \epsilon_2)\). 3. Inversion: \(\mathbf{\Phi}(\mathbf{x}; -\epsilon) = (\mathbf{\Phi}(\cdot; \epsilon))^{-1}(\mathbf{x})\).

3.2 The Infinitesimal Generator (Lie Vector Field)

Taylor expanding the transformation \(\mathbf{\Phi}(\mathbf{x}; \epsilon)\) about the identity parameter \(\epsilon = 0\): \[x_i^* = x_i + \epsilon \left. \frac{\partial \Phi_i(\mathbf{x}; \epsilon)}{\partial \epsilon} \right|_{\epsilon=0} + \mathcal{O}(\epsilon^2) \equiv x_i + \epsilon \, \xi_i(\mathbf{x}) + \mathcal{O}(\epsilon^2) \tag{6}\] The vector functions \(\boldsymbol{\xi}(\mathbf{x}) = (\xi_1(\mathbf{x}), \dots, \xi_n(\mathbf{x}))^T\) define the infinitesimal generator as a first-order linear differential operator (vector field):

\[X = \sum_{i=1}^n \xi_i(\mathbf{x}) \frac{\partial}{\partial x_i} \tag{7}\]

By Lie’s first fundamental theorem, the global finite transformation \(\mathbf{x}^* = \mathbf{\Phi}(\mathbf{x}; \epsilon)\) is recovered uniquely by integrating the autonomous system of ODEs (the Lie initial value problem): \[\frac{d\mathbf{x}^*(\epsilon)}{d\epsilon} = \boldsymbol{\xi}(\mathbf{x}^*(\epsilon)), \qquad \mathbf{x}^*(0) = \mathbf{x} \tag{8}\] Symbolically, the action on any smooth test function \(f(\mathbf{x})\) is generated by the exponential operator: \[f(\mathbf{x}^*) = \exp(\epsilon X) f(\mathbf{x}) = \left( I + \epsilon X + \frac{\epsilon^2}{2!} X^2 + \dots \right) f(\mathbf{x}) \tag{9}\]

3.3 Group Invariants and Canonical Coordinates

A smooth scalar function \(I(\mathbf{x})\) is an invariant of the continuous transformation group if it remains unaltered under the group action: \[I(\mathbf{x}^*) = I(\mathbf{x}) \quad \forall \epsilon \tag{10}\]

Differentiating Equation (10) with respect to \(\epsilon\) and evaluating at \(\epsilon = 0\) yields the fundamental infinitesimal criterion for invariance:

\[X I(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{i=1}^n \xi_i(\mathbf{x}) \frac{\partial I}{\partial x_i} = 0 \tag{11}\]

Finding Invariants via the Method of Characteristics

Equation (11) is a linear, homogeneous first-order PDE. Its characteristic ODE system is: \[\frac{dx_1}{\xi_1(\mathbf{x})} = \frac{dx_2}{\xi_2(\mathbf{x})} = \dots = \frac{dx_n}{\xi_n(\mathbf{x})} \tag{12}\] In an \(n\)-dimensional space, a single 1-parameter group has exactly \(n - 1\) functionally independent invariants: \[I_1(\mathbf{x}), I_2(\mathbf{x}), \dots, I_{n-1}(\mathbf{x})\]

Straightening Vector Fields: Canonical Coordinates

Sophus Lie proved that there always exist local canonical coordinates \((r(\mathbf{x}), s(\mathbf{x}))\) such that the vector field is straightened into a pure translation: \[\begin{cases} X r(\mathbf{x}) = 0 & \implies r^*(\mathbf{x}) = r(\mathbf{x}) \quad (\text{Invariant}) \\ X s(\mathbf{x}) = 1 & \implies s^*(\mathbf{x}) = s(\mathbf{x}) + \epsilon \quad (\text{Canonical Group Parameter}) \end{cases} \tag{13}\] In canonical coordinates, the infinitesimal generator becomes trivially: \[X = \frac{\partial}{\partial s}\]

4. Prolongation to Jet Spaces (Symmetries of Differential Equations)

To apply Lie groups to differential equations, we must understand how a point transformation \(\mathbf{x}^* = \mathbf{\Phi}(\mathbf{x}, y; \epsilon), y^* = \Psi(\mathbf{x}, y; \epsilon)\) transforms derivatives: \[y' = \frac{dy}{dx}, \quad y'' = \frac{d^2y}{dx^2}, \quad \dots, \quad y^{(n)} = \frac{d^n y}{dx^n}\]

The space of independent variables, dependent variables, and derivatives up to order \(n\): \[J^n = (x, y, y', y'', \dots, y^{(n)})\] is called the \(n\)-th order Jet Space.

4.1 The First Prolongation

Let \(X = \xi(x, y) \frac{\partial}{\partial x} + \eta(x, y) \frac{\partial}{\partial y}\) be an infinitesimal generator in the \((x, y)\)-plane.
Its first prolongation \(X^{(1)}\) acting on the first jet space \(J^1 = (x, y, y')\) is:

\[X^{(1)} = \xi(x, y) \frac{\partial}{\partial x} + \eta(x, y) \frac{\partial}{\partial y} + \eta^{(1)}(x, y, y') \frac{\partial}{\partial y'} \tag{14}\]

The prolonged coefficient \(\eta^{(1)}\) is computed via the chain rule on the total derivative operator: \[\eta^{(1)}(x, y, y') = \frac{D\eta}{Dx} - y' \frac{D\xi}{Dx} \tag{15}\] where \(\frac{D}{Dx}\) is the total derivative operator: \[\frac{D}{Dx} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x} + y' \frac{\partial}{\partial y} + y'' \frac{\partial}{\partial y'} + \dots \tag{16}\] Expanding Equation (15): \[\eta^{(1)} = \frac{\partial \eta}{\partial x} + \left( \frac{\partial \eta}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial \xi}{\partial x} \right) y' - \frac{\partial \xi}{\partial y} (y')^2 \tag{17}\]

4.2 The Symmetry Condition for an ODE

An \(n\)-th order ordinary differential equation: \[\Delta\left(x, y, y', \dots, y^{(n)}\right) = 0 \tag{18}\] admits \(X\) as a Lie symmetry generator if and only if the equation remains invariant under the prolonged action of \(X\):

\[\left. X^{(n)} \Delta \right|_{\Delta = 0} = 0 \tag{19}\]

Equation (19) produces an overdetermined system of linear PDEs for the unknown coefficient functions \(\xi(x, y)\) and \(\eta(x, y)\), called the determining equations. Solving these determining equations algorithmically yields the complete symmetry group of the differential equation!

5. Worked Application 1: Lie Foundations of Buckingham \(\Pi\) Analysis and the Dimensional Algorithm

A foundational question in theoretical physics is:
Why is it physically illegal to add terms with mismatched units (e.g., \(v + v^2\))? Is this merely an arbitrary aesthetic human convention, or a mathematical law of nature? And why does the Buckingham \(\Pi\) method guarantee a complete reduction of physical degrees of freedom?

5.1 The Multi-Parameter Lie Scaling Group

Suppose a physical system is described by \(n\) physical variables \(q_1, \dots, q_n\) governed by \(m\) fundamental base dimensions \(D_1, \dots, D_m\) (e.g., Mass \(M\), Length \(L\), Time \(T\)).
The arbitrary scale dilation of base units forms an \(m\)-parameter abelian continuous Lie group \(G = (\mathbb{R}_{>0}^m, \cdot)\): \[\boldsymbol{\lambda} = (\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_m) \in \mathbb{R}_{>0}^m\] Let the parameters be parameterized exponentially: \(\lambda_k = e^{\epsilon_k}\) with \(\epsilon_k \in \mathbb{R}\).

Under this group, each physical variable scales according to its dimensional coordinate vector \(\mathbf{v}_{q_j} = (D_{1j}, D_{2j}, \dots, D_{mj})^T\): \[q_j^* = \left( \prod_{k=1}^m \lambda_k^{D_{kj}} \right) q_j = \exp\left( \sum_{k=1}^m \epsilon_k D_{kj} \right) q_j \tag{20}\]

5.2 The Infinitesimal Generators

Differentiating Equation (20) with respect to the group parameter \(\epsilon_k\) at the identity \(\boldsymbol{\epsilon} = \mathbf{0}\): \[\left. \frac{\partial q_j^*}{\partial \epsilon_k} \right|_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}=\mathbf{0}} = D_{kj} q_j\] Therefore, the \(m\) infinitesimal generators of the unit dilation Lie group are:

\[X_k = \sum_{j=1}^n D_{kj} q_j \frac{\partial}{\partial q_j}, \qquad k \in \{1, 2, \dots, m\} \tag{21}\]

Notice that the generators commute: \[[X_k, X_l] = 0 \quad \forall k, l \in \{1, \dots, m\}\] confirming that the dimension group is an abelian Lie algebra \(\mathfrak{g} \cong \mathbb{R}^m\).

5.3 Invariance of Physical Laws and Fourier’s Principle

Any universally valid physical relationship equating two or more non-trivial terms must be an invariant hypersurface under the continuous unit dilation group: \[F(q_1, \dots, q_n) = A(q_1, \dots, q_n) + B(q_1, \dots, q_n) = 0 \tag{22}\] Applying the infinitesimal Lie invariance criterion (\(X_k F = 0\)): \[X_k (A + B) = X_k A + X_k B = 0 \tag{23}\]

Because \(A\) and \(B\) are physical quantities, each has its own dimensional exponent coordinates along base unit \(D_k\), say \(u_k\) and \(v_k\): \[X_k A = u_k A, \qquad X_k B = v_k B \tag{24}\] Substituting Equation (24) into Equation (23): \[u_k A + v_k B = 0 \tag{25}\]

Now, from the original physical law, \(A + B = 0 \implies A = -B\).
Substituting \(A = -B\) into Equation (25): \[-u_k B + v_k B = 0 \implies (v_k - u_k) B = 0 \tag{26}\]

Since \(B\) is a non-trivial physical term (\(B \neq 0\)), we must have: \[v_k - u_k = 0 \implies u_k = v_k \quad \forall k \in \{1, \dots, m\} \tag{27}\]

Because the generators commute (\([X_k, X_l] = 0\)), they form an involutive distribution of rank \(r = \operatorname{rank}(D)\) on the \(n\)-dimensional manifold of physical variables.
By the Frobenius Integration Theorem, the orbits of this Lie group are \(r\)-dimensional submanifolds, which guarantees:

5.6 Worked Example A: The Sedov-Taylor Blast Wave (Point Explosion)

In 1941, British physicist G.I. Taylor deduced the classified energy yield of the world’s first atomic detonation from declassified multi-frame photographs purely using this dimensional algorithm.

Step 1: Enumerate Variables and Dimensions

The expansion of an intense spherical shock wave in air is governed by: 1. Shock wave radius \(R\): \([R] = L\) 2. Total energy released \(E\): \([E] = M L^2 T^{-2}\) 3. Ambient undisturbed air density \(\rho_0\): \([\rho_0] = M L^{-3}\) 4. Time elapsed since detonation \(t\): \([t] = T\)

Total variables: \(n = 4\). Fundamental dimensions: \(m = 3\) (\(M, L, T\)).

Step 2: Form Infinitesimal Lie Generators

From the dimensional exponents: \[X_M = E \frac{\partial}{\partial E} + \rho_0 \frac{\partial}{\partial \rho_0}\] \[X_L = R \frac{\partial}{\partial R} + 2E \frac{\partial}{\partial E} - 3\rho_0 \frac{\partial}{\partial \rho_0}\] \[X_T = -2E \frac{\partial}{\partial E} + t \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\]

Step 3: Dimensional Matrix and Rank

Arranging columns as \((R, E, \rho_0, t)\): \[D = \begin{array}{c|rrrr} & R & E & \rho_0 & t \\ \hline M & 0 & 1 & 1 & 0 \\ L & 1 & 2 & -3 & 0 \\ T & 0 & -2 & 0 & 1 \end{array} \implies D = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 2 & -3 & 0 \\ 0 & -2 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\]

The \(3 \times 3\) submatrix of \((R, E, \rho_0)\) has determinant: \[\det \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & -3 \\ 0 & -2 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = -1 \cdot (0 - (-2)) = -2 \neq 0\] Hence \(\operatorname{rank}(D) = r = 3\).
The number of independent dimensionless groups is: \[p = n - r = 4 - 3 = 1\]

Step 4: Solve for the Lie Invariant

We seek \(\Pi = R^a E^b \rho_0^c t^d\) satisfying \(D \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}\): \[\begin{cases} b + c = 0 \implies c = -b \\ a + 2b - 3c = 0 \implies a = -2b + 3(-b) = -5b \\ -2b + d = 0 \implies d = 2b \end{cases}\] Setting the free variable \(b = -1\): \[b = -1 \implies a = 5, \quad c = 1, \quad d = -2\] This yields the unique Lie invariant: \[\Pi = \frac{R^5 \rho_0}{E t^2}\]

Step 5: Physical Scaling Law

Because \(\Pi\) is the sole invariant of the continuous scale group, the invariant hypersurface relation \(\Phi(\Pi) = 0\) forces \(\Pi\) to be an absolute universal dimensionless constant: \[\Pi = \xi_0^5 \implies \frac{R^5 \rho_0}{E t^2} = \xi_0^5\] Solving for the shock radius \(R(t)\):

\[R(t) = \xi_0 \left( \frac{E}{\rho_0} \right)^{1/5} t^{2/5} \tag{30}\]

Taylor measured \(R\) and \(t\) from the fireball photos, plotted \(\ln R\) versus \(\ln t\), verified the slope was exactly \(2/5 = 0.40\), and solved for \(E \approx 10^{14}\text{ J} \approx 20\text{ kilotons of TNT}\)—a top-secret military quantity revealed by pure Lie group symmetry!

5.7 Worked Example B: Pipe Flow Pressure Drop (Darcy-Weisbach & Reynolds)

Consider fluid flowing steadily through a circular pipe of inner diameter \(D\). We seek to predict the frictional pressure drop per unit length \(\frac{\Delta p}{L}\).

Step 1: Enumerate Variables

  1. Pressure drop per length: \(\left[\frac{\Delta p}{L}\right] = \frac{M L^{-1} T^{-2}}{L} = M L^{-2} T^{-2}\)
  2. Pipe diameter: \([D] = L\)
  3. Mean flow velocity: \([v] = L T^{-1}\)
  4. Fluid density: \([\rho] = M L^{-3}\)
  5. Dynamic fluid viscosity: \([\mu] = M L^{-1} T^{-1}\)
  6. Pipe wall roughness: \([\epsilon] = L\)

Total variables: \(n = 6\). Base dimensions: \(m = 3\) (\(M, L, T\)).

Step 2: Form Matrix and Compute Degrees of Freedom

Columns: \(\left(\frac{\Delta p}{L}, D, v, \rho, \mu, \epsilon\right)\): \[D = \begin{array}{c|rrrrrr} & \Delta p/L & D & v & \rho & \mu & \epsilon \\ \hline M & 1 & 0 & 0 & 1 & 1 & 0 \\ L & -2 & 1 & 1 & -3 & -1 & 1 \\ T & -2 & 0 & -1 & 0 & -1 & 0 \end{array}\] The rank is \(r = 3\) (spanned by repeating variables \(\rho, v, D\)).
Number of dimensionless \(\Pi\)-groups: \[p = n - r = 6 - 3 = 3\]

Step 3: Extract the Three \(\Pi\)-Groups

  1. \(\Pi_1\) for Pressure Drop \(\frac{\Delta p}{L}\): \[\Pi_1 = \left(\frac{\Delta p}{L}\right) \rho^a v^b D^c \implies [M L^{-2} T^{-2}][M^a L^{-3a}][L^b T^{-b}][L^c] = M^0 L^0 T^0\]

    • Mass: \(1 + a = 0 \implies a = -1\)
    • Time: \(-2 - b = 0 \implies b = -2\)
    • Length: \(-2 - 3(-1) + (-2) + c = 0 \implies -1 + c = 0 \implies c = 1\) \[\Pi_1 = \frac{(\Delta p / L) D}{\rho v^2} \sim f \quad (\text{Darcy Friction Factor})\]

  2. \(\Pi_2\) for Viscosity \(\mu\): \[\Pi_2 = \mu \rho^a v^b D^c \implies a = -1, \; b = -1, \; c = -1 \implies \Pi_2 = \frac{\mu}{\rho v D}\] Inverting defines the Reynolds number: \[\mathrm{Re} = \Pi_2^{-1} = \frac{\rho v D}{\mu}\]

  3. \(\Pi_3\) for Wall Roughness \(\epsilon\): \[\Pi_3 = \frac{\epsilon}{D} \quad (\text{Relative Roughness})\]

Step 4: The Darcy-Weisbach Law

The Buckingham theorem guarantees: \[\Pi_1 = \Psi(\Pi_2, \Pi_3) \implies \frac{(\Delta p / L) D}{\rho v^2} = \frac{1}{2} f\left(\mathrm{Re}, \frac{\epsilon}{D}\right)\] Multiplying by \(\frac{\rho v^2}{2D}\) yields the universal Darcy-Weisbach equation:

\[\frac{\Delta p}{L} = f\left(\mathrm{Re}, \frac{\epsilon}{D}\right) \frac{\rho v^2}{2 D} \tag{31}\]

The friction factor \(f\) depends only on the two dimensionless numbers \((\mathrm{Re}, \epsilon/D)\), generating the universal Moody Diagram that governs all modern pipeline design!

6. Worked Application 2: Non-Linear First-Order ODE Integration

Consider the challenging non-linear, non-homogeneous first-order differential equation:

\[\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{2y}{x} + \frac{x^3}{y} \tag{32}\]

This equation is non-linear (due to \(1/y\)) and cannot be solved directly by elementary separation. We solve it systematically using Sophus Lie’s symmetry reduction.

Step 1: Detect the Lie Scaling Symmetry

Test the general one-parameter scaling group: \[x^* = \lambda x = e^\epsilon x, \qquad y^* = \lambda^b y = e^{b\epsilon} y\] Transforming the derivative: \[\frac{dy^*}{dx^*} = \frac{d(\lambda^b y)}{d(\lambda x)} = \lambda^{b - 1} \frac{dy}{dx}\] Transforming the right side of Equation (32): \[\frac{2y^*}{x^*} + \frac{(x^*)^3}{y^*} = \lambda^{b - 1} \frac{2y}{x} + \lambda^{3 - b} \frac{x^3}{y}\] For the equation to be invariant, the scaling weights must match: \[b - 1 = 3 - b \implies 2b = 4 \implies b = 2\] Both sides scale with power \(\lambda^1\).
Thus, the equation is invariant under the continuous 1-parameter Lie scaling group: \[x^* = e^\epsilon x, \qquad y^* = e^{2\epsilon} y \tag{33}\]

The infinitesimal generator is: \[\xi(x, y) = \left.\frac{\partial x^*}{\partial \epsilon}\right|_0 = x, \qquad \eta(x, y) = \left.\frac{\partial y^*}{\partial \epsilon}\right|_0 = 2y \implies X = x \frac{\partial}{\partial x} + 2y \frac{\partial}{\partial y} \tag{34}\]

Step 2: Prolongation and Invariance Verification

Compute the prolonged coefficient \(\eta^{(1)}\) using Equation (17): \[\eta^{(1)} = \frac{\partial(2y)}{\partial x} + \left(\frac{\partial(2y)}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial x}{\partial x}\right) y' - \frac{\partial x}{\partial y}(y')^2 = 0 + (2 - 1)y' - 0 = y'\] The prolonged generator is: \[X^{(1)} = x \frac{\partial}{\partial x} + 2y \frac{\partial}{\partial y} + y' \frac{\partial}{\partial y'}\]

Applying \(X^{(1)}\) to \(\Delta(x, y, y') = y' - \frac{2y}{x} - \frac{x^3}{y} = 0\): \[X^{(1)}\Delta = y' - x \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\frac{2y}{x} + \frac{x^3}{y}\right) - 2y \frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left(\frac{2y}{x} + \frac{x^3}{y}\right)\] \[= y' - x\left(-\frac{2y}{x^2} + \frac{3x^2}{y}\right) - 2y\left(\frac{2}{x} - \frac{x^3}{y^2}\right) = y' - \left(-\frac{2y}{x} + \frac{3x^3}{y} + \frac{4y}{x} - \frac{2x^3}{y}\right)\] \[= y' - \left(\frac{2y}{x} + \frac{x^3}{y}\right) = y' - y' = 0\] The invariance condition \(\left. X^{(1)} \Delta \right|_{\Delta = 0} = 0\) holds identically!

Step 3: Canonical Straightening Coordinates

Find coordinates \((r, s)\) that straighten \(X = x \partial_x + 2y \partial_y\): 1. Invariant \(r(x, y)\): \[X r = x \frac{\partial r}{\partial x} + 2y \frac{\partial r}{\partial y} = 0 \implies \frac{dx}{x} = \frac{dy}{2y} \implies \ln y - 2\ln x = C \implies r = \frac{y}{x^2}\] 2. Canonical Parameter \(s(x, y)\): \[X s = x \frac{\partial s}{\partial x} + 2y \frac{\partial s}{\partial y} = 1 \implies \frac{dx}{x} = ds \implies s = \ln x\]

Step 4: Transforming the ODE into Canonical Form

Transform \(\frac{dr}{ds}\) using the chain rule: \[\frac{dr}{ds} = \frac{\frac{dr}{dx}}{\frac{ds}{dx}} = \frac{\frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{y}{x^2}\right)}{\frac{d}{dx}(\ln x)} = \frac{\frac{y'}{x^2} - \frac{2y}{x^3}}{\frac{1}{x}} = \frac{y' - \frac{2y}{x}}{x} \tag{35}\] From the original ODE, \(y' - \frac{2y}{x} = \frac{x^3}{y}\). Substitute into Equation (35): \[\frac{dr}{ds} = \frac{\frac{x^3}{y}}{x} = \frac{x^2}{y} = \frac{1}{\frac{y}{x^2}} = \frac{1}{r} \tag{36}\]

The canonical coordinate \(s\) has completely vanished, leaving a purely autonomous ODE!

Step 5: Separation of Variables and Exact Quadrature

Equation (36) separates immediately: \[r \, dr = ds\] Integrating both sides: \[\int r \, dr = \int ds \implies \frac{1}{2} r^2 = s + C \tag{37}\]

Step 6: Invert to Physical Coordinates

Substitute back \(r = \frac{y}{x^2}\) and \(s = \ln x\): \[\frac{1}{2} \left(\frac{y}{x^2}\right)^2 = \ln x + C \implies \left(\frac{y}{x^2}\right)^2 = 2\ln x + 2C\] Multiplying by \(x^4\) and taking the square root:

\[y(x) = x^2 \sqrt{2\ln x + C_1} \tag{38}\]

where \(C_1 = 2C\) is the arbitrary constant of integration.
Lie’s canonical coordinates transformed an intractable non-linear ODE into an exact, closed-form analytic solution!

7. Worked Application 3: Similarity Reduction of the Heat PDE

Consider the classical 1D heat (diffusion) equation:

\[\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = \kappa \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} \tag{38}\]

This PDE governs heat conduction, molecular diffusion, and Brownian probability density. We derive its fundamental Gaussian solution purely by finding its scaling Lie symmetry.

Step 1: Scaling Lie Symmetry Group

Consider the scaling transformation: \[x^* = \lambda^a x, \qquad t^* = \lambda^b t, \qquad u^* = \lambda^c u \tag{39}\] Transforming the derivatives: \[\frac{\partial u^*}{\partial t^*} = \lambda^{c - b} \frac{\partial u}{\partial t}, \qquad \frac{\partial^2 u^*}{\partial (x^*)^2} = \lambda^{c - 2a} \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2}\] Substituting into the heat equation: \[\lambda^{c - b} \frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = \kappa \lambda^{c - 2a} \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2}\] For the equation to be invariant, the scaling powers must match: \[c - b = c - 2a \implies b = 2a \tag{40}\] The parameter \(c\) remains free. Setting \(a = 1 \implies b = 2\). For a point source of heat where total energy \(\int_{-\infty}^\infty u \, dx = Q\) is conserved, \([u][x] = \text{const} \implies c = -1\).

Thus, the infinitesimal generator is: \[X = x \frac{\partial}{\partial x} + 2t \frac{\partial}{\partial t} - u \frac{\partial}{\partial u} \tag{41}\]

Step 2: Determine the Invariant Similarity Coordinates

Solve the characteristic ODEs for \(X\): \[\frac{dx}{x} = \frac{dt}{2t} = \frac{du}{-u} \tag{42}\]

  1. From \(\frac{dx}{x} = \frac{dt}{2t}\): \[\ln x - \frac{1}{2}\ln t = \text{const} \implies \eta = \frac{x}{\sqrt{t}} \quad (\text{Self-Similar Coordinate}) \tag{43}\]
  2. From \(\frac{dt}{2t} = \frac{du}{-u}\): \[\ln u + \frac{1}{2}\ln t = \text{const} \implies u \sqrt{t} = f(\eta) \implies u(x, t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{t}} f(\eta) \tag{44}\]

Step 3: Reduction of PDE to ODE

Compute the partial derivatives of \(u(x, t) = t^{-1/2} f(\eta)\) where \(\eta = x t^{-1/2}\): \[\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = -\frac{1}{2} t^{-3/2} f(\eta) + t^{-1/2} f'(\eta) \left(-\frac{1}{2} x t^{-3/2}\right) = -\frac{1}{2} t^{-3/2} \left[ f(\eta) + \eta f'(\eta) \right]\] \[\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = t^{-1/2} f'(\eta) t^{-1/2} = t^{-1} f'(\eta) \implies \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} = t^{-3/2} f''(\eta)\] Substitute into the heat equation \(\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = \kappa \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2}\): \[-\frac{1}{2} t^{-3/2} \left[ f(\eta) + \eta f'(\eta) \right] = \kappa t^{-3/2} f''(\eta)\]

Cancel the common factor \(t^{-3/2}\). The time variable \(t\) has completely dropped out! \[2\kappa f''(\eta) + \eta f'(\eta) + f(\eta) = 0 \tag{45}\]

Step 4: Integrate the Reduced ODE

Notice that the left side of Equation (45) is an exact total derivative: \[\frac{d}{d\eta}\left[ 2\kappa f'(\eta) + \eta f(\eta) \right] = 2\kappa f'' + f + \eta f' = 0\] Integrating once: \[2\kappa f'(\eta) + \eta f(\eta) = C_0\] For physical decaying boundary conditions as \(\eta \to \pm \infty\), the constant of integration must vanish (\(C_0 = 0\)): \[\frac{f'(\eta)}{f(\eta)} = -\frac{\eta}{2\kappa}\] Integrating again: \[\ln f(\eta) = -\frac{\eta^2}{4\kappa} + \ln A \implies f(\eta) = A \exp\left(-\frac{\eta^2}{4\kappa}\right) \tag{46}\]

Step 5: The Fundamental Gaussian Solution

Substituting \(f(\eta)\) back into Equation (44):

\[u(x, t) = \frac{A}{\sqrt{t}} \exp\left(-\frac{x^2}{4\kappa t}\right) \tag{47}\]

Normalizing the total thermal energy \(\int_{-\infty}^\infty u(x, t) \, dx = Q\) yields \(A = \frac{Q}{\sqrt{4\pi \kappa}}\):

\[u(x, t) = \frac{Q}{\sqrt{4\pi \kappa t}} \exp\left(-\frac{x^2}{4\kappa t}\right) \tag{48}\]

The Lie point symmetry group transformed a second-order partial differential equation into a first-order separable ODE, directly producing the universal Gaussian heat kernel!

## 1. Introduction: Sophus Lie's Unification of Differential Equations

In the early 1830s, Évariste Galois established that polynomial equations could be understood and solved systematically by analyzing their discrete symmetry groups—the permutations of their roots. Half a century later, Norwegian mathematician Sophus Lie set out to achieve an equally grand unification for **differential equations**.

Before Lie, ordinary and partial differential equations (ODEs and PDEs) were taught as an uncoordinated catalog of ad-hoc tricks:
* Integrating factors for first-order linear equations
* Separation of variables
* Homogeneous substitutions ($y/x$)
* Bernoulli and Riccati transformations
* Variation of parameters

Sophus Lie realized that all successful integration techniques share a single underlying mathematical mechanism: **the differential equation admits a continuous group of symmetries**. 

Just as rotational symmetry simplifies a mechanical problem from three dimensions to one, a continuous Lie symmetry allows an analyst to:
1. Reduce the order of an ordinary differential equation by one ($n \to n - 1$).
2. Integrate a first-order non-linear ODE by quadrature through canonical coordinates.
3. Reduce a multi-variable PDE (such as the heat, wave, or Navier-Stokes equations) into an ODE in terms of self-similar invariant coordinates.
4. Establish conservation laws via Noether's theorem.
5. Prove the necessity of **dimensional homogeneity** under measurement unit dilations.

## 2. Mathematical Foundations: Lie Groups and Lie Algebras

### 2.1 Definition of an Abstract Lie Group

A **Lie group** is an algebraic group that is simultaneously a smooth finite-dimensional manifold, such that the group operations are smooth ($C^\infty$) geometric maps:

> **Formal Definition:**  
> A Lie group $(G, \cdot)$ is a smooth manifold $G$ equipped with a group structure such that:
> 1. **Multiplication:** The group multiplication map $\mu: G \times G \to G$, defined by $\mu(g_1, g_2) = g_1 \cdot g_2$, is smooth ($C^\infty$).
> 2. **Inversion:** The group inversion map $\iota: G \to G$, defined by $\iota(g) = g^{-1}$, is smooth ($C^\infty$).

#### Canonical Examples of Lie Groups
* **The General Linear Group $\mathrm{GL}(n, \mathbb{R})$:** The open submanifold of $\mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ consisting of all invertible $n \times n$ real matrices ($\det A \neq 0$). Dimension $= n^2$.
* **The Special Orthogonal Group $\mathrm{SO}(n)$:** The subgroup of rotations in $\mathbb{R}^n$ satisfying $A^T A = I$ and $\det A = 1$. Compact manifold of dimension $\frac{n(n-1)}{2}$.
* **The Scale Dilation Group $(\mathbb{R}_{>0}^m, \cdot)$:** The abelian Lie group of positive real scale factors $\boldsymbol{\lambda} = (\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_m)$ under component-wise multiplication. This is the symmetry group governing dimensional analysis!

---

### 2.2 The Lie Algebra: Tangent Space at the Identity

Because a Lie group $G$ is a smooth manifold, it possesses a tangent space at every point. The tangent space at the group identity element $e \in G$, denoted:
$$\mathfrak{g} = T_e G$$
forms a vector space endowed with an alternating bilinear product called the **Lie bracket** $[\cdot, \cdot]: \mathfrak{g} \times \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{g}$.

A vector space $\mathfrak{g}$ over a field $\mathbb{F}$ equipped with a bracket $[\cdot, \cdot]$ is a **Lie algebra** if it satisfies:
1. **Bilinearity:** $[aX + bY, Z] = a[X, Z] + b[Y, Z]$ for all $a, b \in \mathbb{F}$.
2. **Alternating / Anti-symmetry:** $[X, Y] = -[Y, X]$ (which implies $[X, X] = 0$).
3. **The Jacobi Identity:**
   $$[X, [Y, Z]] + [Y, [Z, X]] + [Z, [X, Y]] = \mathbf{0} \tag{1}$$

For matrix Lie groups, the Lie bracket is simply the **matrix commutator**:
$$[X, Y] = X Y - Y X \tag{2}$$

---

### 2.3 The Exponential Map

The bridge connecting the linear Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ (infinitesimal generators) to the non-linear Lie group manifold $G$ (global transformations) is the **exponential map**:
$$\exp: \mathfrak{g} \to G$$
For any vector $X \in \mathfrak{g}$, $\exp(t X)$ defines the unique one-parameter subgroup $\gamma(t)$ of $G$ passing through the identity $\gamma(0) = e$ with initial velocity vector $\gamma'(0) = X$:
$$\gamma(t_1 + t_2) = \gamma(t_1) \cdot \gamma(t_2) \implies \exp((t_1 + t_2)X) = \exp(t_1 X) \cdot \exp(t_2 X) \tag{3}$$

For matrix groups, $\exp(tX)$ is given by the standard convergent matrix exponential series:
$$\exp(tX) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{t^k X^k}{k!} = I + tX + \frac{t^2}{2!}X^2 + \dots \tag{4}$$

## 3. Continuous Lie Transformation Groups Acting on Spaces

In physical and geometric applications, Lie groups act as transformation groups on coordinate manifolds (e.g., Euclidean space $\mathbb{R}^n$ or spacetime $\mathbb{R}^4$).

### 3.1 One-Parameter Transformation Groups

Let $M \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ be an open domain. A **one-parameter continuous Lie transformation group** on $M$ is a family of smooth coordinate transformations:
$$\mathbf{x}^* = \mathbf{\Phi}(\mathbf{x}; \epsilon), \quad \mathbf{x} \in M, \; \epsilon \in (-\epsilon_0, \epsilon_0) \subset \mathbb{R} \tag{5}$$
satisfying the group axioms:
1. **Identity:** $\mathbf{\Phi}(\mathbf{x}; 0) = \mathbf{x}$ for all $\mathbf{x} \in M$.
2. **Closure / Group Law:** $\mathbf{\Phi}(\mathbf{\Phi}(\mathbf{x}; \epsilon_1); \epsilon_2) = \mathbf{\Phi}(\mathbf{x}; \epsilon_1 + \epsilon_2)$.
3. **Inversion:** $\mathbf{\Phi}(\mathbf{x}; -\epsilon) = (\mathbf{\Phi}(\cdot; \epsilon))^{-1}(\mathbf{x})$.

---

### 3.2 The Infinitesimal Generator (Lie Vector Field)

Taylor expanding the transformation $\mathbf{\Phi}(\mathbf{x}; \epsilon)$ about the identity parameter $\epsilon = 0$:
$$x_i^* = x_i + \epsilon \left. \frac{\partial \Phi_i(\mathbf{x}; \epsilon)}{\partial \epsilon} \right|_{\epsilon=0} + \mathcal{O}(\epsilon^2) \equiv x_i + \epsilon \, \xi_i(\mathbf{x}) + \mathcal{O}(\epsilon^2) \tag{6}$$
The vector functions $\boldsymbol{\xi}(\mathbf{x}) = (\xi_1(\mathbf{x}), \dots, \xi_n(\mathbf{x}))^T$ define the **infinitesimal generator** as a first-order linear differential operator (vector field):

$$X = \sum_{i=1}^n \xi_i(\mathbf{x}) \frac{\partial}{\partial x_i} \tag{7}$$

By Lie's first fundamental theorem, the global finite transformation $\mathbf{x}^* = \mathbf{\Phi}(\mathbf{x}; \epsilon)$ is recovered uniquely by integrating the autonomous system of ODEs (the **Lie initial value problem**):
$$\frac{d\mathbf{x}^*(\epsilon)}{d\epsilon} = \boldsymbol{\xi}(\mathbf{x}^*(\epsilon)), \qquad \mathbf{x}^*(0) = \mathbf{x} \tag{8}$$
Symbolically, the action on any smooth test function $f(\mathbf{x})$ is generated by the exponential operator:
$$f(\mathbf{x}^*) = \exp(\epsilon X) f(\mathbf{x}) = \left( I + \epsilon X + \frac{\epsilon^2}{2!} X^2 + \dots \right) f(\mathbf{x}) \tag{9}$$

---

### 3.3 Group Invariants and Canonical Coordinates

A smooth scalar function $I(\mathbf{x})$ is an **invariant** of the continuous transformation group if it remains unaltered under the group action:
$$I(\mathbf{x}^*) = I(\mathbf{x}) \quad \forall \epsilon \tag{10}$$

Differentiating Equation (10) with respect to $\epsilon$ and evaluating at $\epsilon = 0$ yields the **fundamental infinitesimal criterion for invariance**:

$$X I(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{i=1}^n \xi_i(\mathbf{x}) \frac{\partial I}{\partial x_i} = 0 \tag{11}$$

#### Finding Invariants via the Method of Characteristics
Equation (11) is a linear, homogeneous first-order PDE. Its characteristic ODE system is:
$$\frac{dx_1}{\xi_1(\mathbf{x})} = \frac{dx_2}{\xi_2(\mathbf{x})} = \dots = \frac{dx_n}{\xi_n(\mathbf{x})} \tag{12}$$

## 4. Prolongation to Jet Spaces (Symmetries of Differential Equations)

To apply Lie groups to differential equations, we must understand how a point transformation $\mathbf{x}^* = \mathbf{\Phi}(\mathbf{x}, y; \epsilon), y^* = \Psi(\mathbf{x}, y; \epsilon)$ transforms derivatives:
$$y' = \frac{dy}{dx}, \quad y'' = \frac{d^2y}{dx^2}, \quad \dots, \quad y^{(n)} = \frac{d^n y}{dx^n}$$

The space of independent variables, dependent variables, and derivatives up to order $n$:
$$J^n = (x, y, y', y'', \dots, y^{(n)})$$
is called the **$n$-th order Jet Space**.

### 4.1 The First Prolongation

Let $X = \xi(x, y) \frac{\partial}{\partial x} + \eta(x, y) \frac{\partial}{\partial y}$ be an infinitesimal generator in the $(x, y)$-plane.

$$X^{(1)} = \xi(x, y) \frac{\partial}{\partial x} + \eta(x, y) \frac{\partial}{\partial y} + \eta^{(1)}(x, y, y') \frac{\partial}{\partial y'} \tag{14}$$

The prolonged coefficient $\eta^{(1)}$ is computed via the chain rule on the total derivative operator:
$$\eta^{(1)}(x, y, y') = \frac{D\eta}{Dx} - y' \frac{D\xi}{Dx} \tag{15}$$
where $\frac{D}{Dx}$ is the **total derivative operator**:
$$\frac{D}{Dx} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x} + y' \frac{\partial}{\partial y} + y'' \frac{\partial}{\partial y'} + \dots \tag{16}$$
Expanding Equation (15):
$$\eta^{(1)} = \frac{\partial \eta}{\partial x} + \left( \frac{\partial \eta}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial \xi}{\partial x} \right) y' - \frac{\partial \xi}{\partial y} (y')^2 \tag{17}$$

## 6. Worked Application 2: Non-Linear First-Order ODE Integration

$$= y' - \left(\frac{2y}{x} + \frac{x^3}{y}\right) = y' - y' = 0$$
The invariance condition $\left. X^{(1)} \Delta \right|_{\Delta = 0} = 0$ holds identically!

### Step 3: Canonical Straightening Coordinates
Find coordinates $(r, s)$ that straighten $X = x \partial_x + 2y \partial_y$:
1. **Invariant $r(x, y)$:**

$$\frac{dr}{ds} = \frac{\frac{x^3}{y}}{x} = \frac{x^2}{y} = \frac{1}{\frac{y}{x^2}} = \frac{1}{r} \tag{36}$$

The canonical coordinate $s$ has **completely vanished**, leaving a purely autonomous ODE!

$$r \, dr = ds$$
Integrating both sides:
$$\int r \, dr = \int ds \implies \frac{1}{2} r^2 = s + C \tag{37}$$

### Step 6: Invert to Physical Coordinates

$$\frac{1}{2} \left(\frac{y}{x^2}\right)^2 = \ln x + C \implies \left(\frac{y}{x^2}\right)^2 = 2\ln x + 2C$$

$$y(x) = x^2 \sqrt{2\ln x + C_1} \tag{38}$$

Substituting into the heat equation:
$$\lambda^{c - b} \frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = \kappa \lambda^{c - 2a} \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2}$$
For the equation to be invariant, the scaling powers must match:
$$c - b = c - 2a \implies b = 2a \tag{40}$$
The parameter $c$ remains free. Setting $a = 1 \implies b = 2$. For a point source of heat where total energy $\int_{-\infty}^\infty u \, dx = Q$ is conserved, $[u][x] = \text{const} \implies c = -1$.

Thus, the infinitesimal generator is:
$$X = x \frac{\partial}{\partial x} + 2t \frac{\partial}{\partial t} - u \frac{\partial}{\partial u} \tag{41}$$

### Step 2: Determine the Invariant Similarity Coordinates
Solve the characteristic ODEs for $X$:
$$\frac{dx}{x} = \frac{dt}{2t} = \frac{du}{-u} \tag{42}$$

1. From $\frac{dx}{x} = \frac{dt}{2t}$:
   $$\ln x - \frac{1}{2}\ln t = \text{const} \implies \eta = \frac{x}{\sqrt{t}} \quad (\text{Self-Similar Coordinate}) \tag{43}$$
2. From $\frac{dt}{2t} = \frac{du}{-u}$:
   $$\ln u + \frac{1}{2}\ln t = \text{const} \implies u \sqrt{t} = f(\eta) \implies u(x, t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{t}} f(\eta) \tag{44}$$

### Step 3: Reduction of PDE to ODE
Compute the partial derivatives of $u(x, t) = t^{-1/2} f(\eta)$ where $\eta = x t^{-1/2}$:
$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = -\frac{1}{2} t^{-3/2} f(\eta) + t^{-1/2} f'(\eta) \left(-\frac{1}{2} x t^{-3/2}\right) = -\frac{1}{2} t^{-3/2} \left[ f(\eta) + \eta f'(\eta) \right]$$
$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = t^{-1/2} f'(\eta) t^{-1/2} = t^{-1} f'(\eta) \implies \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} = t^{-3/2} f''(\eta)$$
Substitute into the heat equation $\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = \kappa \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2}$:
$$-\frac{1}{2} t^{-3/2} \left[ f(\eta) + \eta f'(\eta) \right] = \kappa t^{-3/2} f''(\eta)$$

Cancel the common factor $t^{-3/2}$. The time variable $t$ has completely dropped out!
$$2\kappa f''(\eta) + \eta f'(\eta) + f(\eta) = 0 \tag{45}$$

### Step 4: Integrate the Reduced ODE
Notice that the left side of Equation (45) is an exact total derivative:
$$\frac{d}{d\eta}\left[ 2\kappa f'(\eta) + \eta f(\eta) \right] = 2\kappa f'' + f + \eta f' = 0$$
Integrating once:
$$2\kappa f'(\eta) + \eta f(\eta) = C_0$$
For physical decaying boundary conditions as $\eta \to \pm \infty$, the constant of integration must vanish ($C_0 = 0$):
$$\frac{f'(\eta)}{f(\eta)} = -\frac{\eta}{2\kappa}$$
Integrating again:
$$\ln f(\eta) = -\frac{\eta^2}{4\kappa} + \ln A \implies f(\eta) = A \exp\left(-\frac{\eta^2}{4\kappa}\right) \tag{46}$$

### Step 5: The Fundamental Gaussian Solution
Substituting $f(\eta)$ back into Equation (44):

$$u(x, t) = \frac{A}{\sqrt{t}} \exp\left(-\frac{x^2}{4\kappa t}\right) \tag{47}$$

Normalizing the total thermal energy $\int_{-\infty}^\infty u(x, t) \, dx = Q$ yields $A = \frac{Q}{\sqrt{4\pi \kappa}}$:

$$u(x, t) = \frac{Q}{\sqrt{4\pi \kappa t}} \exp\left(-\frac{x^2}{4\kappa t}\right) \tag{48}$$

The Lie point symmetry group transformed a second-order partial differential equation into a first-order separable ODE, directly producing the **universal Gaussian heat kernel**!
