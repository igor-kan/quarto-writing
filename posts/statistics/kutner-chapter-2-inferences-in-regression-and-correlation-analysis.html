Chapter 1 built the simple linear regression model and estimated it by least squares. This chapter draws inferences: interval estimates and tests for the parameters, intervals for the mean response, prediction intervals for new observations, confidence bands for the whole line, the analysis-of-variance and general linear test viewpoints, measures of association, and finally the bivariate normal correlation model.

Throughout (except §2.11) we assume the normal error regression model \[Y_i=\beta_0+\beta_1X_i+\varepsilon_i,\qquad \varepsilon_i\ \text{independent }N(0,\sigma^2),\quad X_i\ \text{known constants.} \tag{2.1}\]

2.1 Inferences Concerning \(\beta_1\)

Interest often centers on the slope \(\beta_1\): a market analyst may want an interval for how many extra sales dollars each extra advertising dollar generates. A central test is \[H_0:\beta_1=0\qquad\text{versus}\qquad H_a:\beta_1\ne0.\] When \(\beta_1=0\) there is no linear association between \(Y\) and \(X\): the regression line is horizontal, \(E\{Y\}=\beta_0\). Under the normal error model this means the distributions of \(Y\) are identical at every \(X\) — no relation of any kind.

Sampling distribution of \(b_1\)

The least squares slope is \(b_1=\sum(X_i-\bar X)(Y_i-\bar Y)/\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2\), which can be written as a linear combination \[b_1=\sum_i k_iY_i,\qquad k_i=\frac{X_i-\bar X}{\sum_j(X_j-\bar X)^2}. \tag{2.4, 2.4a}\] The \(k_i\) depend only on the (fixed) \(X_i\), with properties \[\sum k_i=0,\qquad \sum k_iX_i=1,\qquad \sum k_i^2=\frac{1}{\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2}. \tag{2.5–2.7}\] Because a linear combination of independent normals is normal, for model (2.1): \[b_1\sim N\!\left(\beta_1,\ \sigma^2\{b_1\}\right),\qquad E\{b_1\}=\beta_1,\qquad \sigma^2\{b_1\}=\frac{\sigma^2}{\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2}. \tag{2.3}\]

Estimated variance and the studentized statistic

Replacing \(\sigma^2\) by \(MSE\), \[s^2\{b_1\}=\frac{MSE}{\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2},\qquad s\{b_1\}=\sqrt{s^2\{b_1\}}. \tag{2.9}\] The studentized statistic \((b_1-\beta_1)/s\{b_1\}\) follows a \(t\) distribution with \(n-2\) df: \[\frac{b_1-\beta_1}{s\{b_1\}}\sim t(n-2). \tag{2.10}\] (Two df are lost because \(\beta_0,\beta_1\) are both estimated; equivalently \(SSE/\sigma^2\sim\chi^2(n-2)\) and is independent of \(b_1\).)

Confidence interval and tests

The \(1-\alpha\) confidence limits for \(\beta_1\) are \[\boxed{\,b_1\pm t(1-\alpha/2;\,n-2)\,s\{b_1\}\,} \tag{2.15}\] For the two-sided test of \(H_0:\beta_1=0\), use \[t^*=\frac{b_1}{s\{b_1\}}, \tag{2.17}\] rejecting \(H_0\) at level \(\alpha\) when \(|t^*|>t(1-\alpha/2;n-2)\). For a one-sided alternative use the appropriate one-tailed critical value; for a nonzero null \(H_0:\beta_1=\beta_{10}\), standardize with \(\beta_{10}\): \[t^*=\frac{b_1-\beta_{10}}{s\{b_1\}}.\qquad H_0:\beta_1=\beta_{10} \tag{2.19, 2.20}\] The P-value is the observed level of significance; a CI can be used interchangeably with a two-sided test (reject \(H_0:\beta_1=0\) at level \(\alpha\) iff the \(1-\alpha\) CI excludes 0).

Toluca Company. From Chapter 1, \(b_1=3.5702\), \(\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2=19{,}800\), \(MSE=2{,}384\): \[s^2\{b_1\}=\frac{2{,}384}{19{,}800}=.12040,\qquad s\{b_1\}=.3470.\] With \(t(.975;23)=2.069\): \[3.5702\pm 2.069(.3470)\ \Longrightarrow\ 2.85\le\beta_1\le4.29.\] And \(t^*=3.5702/.3470=10.29\) (\(P\approx0\)): strong evidence of a linear association between lot size and work hours.

2.2 Inferences Concerning \(\beta_0\)

The intercept is only interpretable when the scope of the model includes \(X=0\). From \(b_0=\bar Y-b_1\bar X\): \[b_0\sim N(\beta_0,\sigma^2\{b_0\}),\qquad \sigma^2\{b_0\}=\sigma^2\!\left[\frac1n+\frac{\bar X^2}{\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2}\right], \tag{2.22}\] \[s^2\{b_0\}=MSE\!\left[\frac1n+\frac{\bar X^2}{\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2}\right],\qquad \frac{b_0-\beta_0}{s\{b_0\}}\sim t(n-2), \tag{2.23, 2.24}\] with \(1-\alpha\) limits \[b_0\pm t(1-\alpha/2;n-2)\,s\{b_0\}. \tag{2.25}\] For Toluca, \(s\{b_0\}=26.18\) and a 90% interval is \(17.5\le\beta_0\le107.2\) — but with no intrinsic meaning, since the model’s scope (lot sizes 20–120) does not extend to \(X=0\).

2.3 Some Considerations on Inferences for \(\beta_0\) and \(\beta_1\)

  • Departures from normality. The \(t\) procedures are fairly robust to mild non-normality, and \(b_0,b_1\) are asymptotically normal, so for large \(n\) the intervals and tests remain approximately valid (replace \(t\) by \(z\)).
  • Interpretation of the confidence coefficient. Because the \(X_i\) are fixed constants, the coefficient refers to repeated samples with the same \(X\) levels. A 95% interval procedure covers the true \(\beta_1\) in 95% of such samples.
  • Spacing of the \(X\) levels. \(\sigma^2\{b_1\}=\sigma^2/\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2\), so a wider spread of \(X\) gives a smaller variance for \(b_1\) (and for \(\hat Y\) at extreme \(X\)). Designs should spread the predictor values.
  • Power. For testing \(H_0:\beta_1=\beta_{10}\), the power depends on the noncentrality \[\delta=\frac{|\beta_1-\beta_{10}|}{\sigma\{b_1\}} \tag{2.27}\] via the noncentral \(t\) distribution (tables or software). For Toluca with a planning \(\sigma^2=2{,}500\), testing \(\beta_1=1.5\) gives \(\sigma\{b_1\}=\sqrt{2500/19800}=.3553\), \(\delta=4.22\), and power \(\approx.98\).

2.4 Interval Estimation of \(E\{Y_h\}\)

For a given level \(X_h\), the mean response is \(E\{Y_h\}=\beta_0+\beta_1X_h\), estimated by \[\hat Y_h=b_0+b_1X_h. \tag{2.28}\] Its sampling distribution is normal with \[E\{\hat Y_h\}=E\{Y_h\},\qquad \sigma^2\{\hat Y_h\}=\sigma^2\!\left[\frac1n+\frac{(X_h-\bar X)^2}{\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2}\right]. \tag{2.29}\] \[s^2\{\hat Y_h\}=MSE\!\left[\frac1n+\frac{(X_h-\bar X)^2}{\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2}\right],\qquad \frac{\hat Y_h-E\{Y_h\}}{s\{\hat Y_h\}}\sim t(n-2). \tag{2.30, 2.32}\] The \(1-\alpha\) confidence limits for the mean response are \[\boxed{\,\hat Y_h\pm t(1-\alpha/2;n-2)\,s\{\hat Y_h\}\,} \tag{2.33}\]

Key facts: the variance is smallest at \(X_h=\bar X\) and grows with \((X_h-\bar X)^2\) (the “bow-tie” effect, illustrated by two sample lines in ALSM Figure 2.3); and \(s^2\{\hat Y_h\}\) reduces to \(s^2\{b_0\}\) when \(X_h=0\).

Toluca. At \(X_h=65\): \(\hat Y_h=294.4\), \(s\{\hat Y_h\}=9.918\), and the 90% CI is \(277.4\le E\{Y_h\}\le311.4\). At \(X_h=100\): \(\hat Y_h=419.4\), \(s\{\hat Y_h\}=14.27\), CI \(394.9\le E\{Y_h\}\le443.9\) — wider, because 100 is farther from \(\bar X=70\).

2.5 Prediction of a New Observation

Predicting a new observation \(Y_{h(\text{new})}\) at \(X_h\) (e.g. hours for the next lot) differs fundamentally from estimating the mean: the new observation is drawn from the distribution, so it deviates from the mean.

  • Parameters known: the \(1-\alpha\) prediction limits are \(E\{Y_h\}\pm z(1-\alpha/2)\,\sigma\). (2.34)
  • Parameters unknown: the prediction error is \(Y_{h(\text{new})}-\hat Y_h\), independent of the sample, with \[\sigma^2\{\text{pred}\}=\sigma^2+\sigma^2\{\hat Y_h\}, \tag{2.37}\] \[s^2\{\text{pred}\}=MSE+s^2\{\hat Y_h\} =MSE\!\left[1+\frac1n+\frac{(X_h-\bar X)^2}{\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2}\right], \tag{2.38}\] \[\frac{Y_{h(\text{new})}-\hat Y_h}{s\{\text{pred}\}}\sim t(n-2),\qquad \hat Y_h\pm t(1-\alpha/2;n-2)\,s\{\text{pred}\}. \tag{2.35, 2.36}\]

The prediction variance has two components: the variability of \(Y\) at \(X_h\) (\(\sigma^2\)) and the uncertainty in \(\hat Y_h\). It is therefore always wider than the confidence interval for the mean, and — unlike the mean-response CI — it is sensitive to non-normality.

Prediction of the mean of \(m\) new observations at \(X_h\): \[s^2\{\text{predmean}\}=MSE\!\left[\frac1m+\frac1n+\frac{(X_h-\bar X)^2}{\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2}\right],\qquad \hat Y_h\pm t\,s\{\text{predmean}\}. \tag{2.39}\]

Toluca at \(X_h=100\) (90%): single lot \(s\{\text{pred}\}=50.87\), interval \(332.2\le Y_{h(\text{new})}\le506.6\); mean of \(m=3\) lots \(s\{\text{predmean}\}=31.60\), interval \(365.2\le \bar Y_{h(\text{new})}\le473.6\) (total for three lots \(1{,}096\)–\(1{,}421\) hours). Both are wider than the mean-response CI \((394.9,443.9)\).

2.6 Confidence Band for the Regression Line

To cover the entire regression line, use the Working–Hotelling \(1-\alpha\) band. At each \(X_h\) the boundary values are \[\hat Y_h\pm W\,s\{\hat Y_h\},\qquad W^2=2F(1-\alpha;2,n-2). \tag{2.40, 2.40a}\] The band is a hyperbola (substituting \(\hat Y_h\) and \(s\{\hat Y_h\}\)), and its multiple \(W\) exceeds the \(t\) multiple of the single-level CI because it must cover all \(X\). For Toluca with \(1-\alpha=.90\): \(W^2=2(2.549)=5.098\), \(W=2.258\), so at \(X_h=100\) the band is \(387.2\le\beta_0+\beta_1X_h\le451.6\). The coefficient applies over the whole real line; restricting to a segment gives a higher confidence level, so \(1-\alpha\) is a lower bound.

2.7 Analysis of Variance Approach to Regression

Partitioning the total sum of squares

Variation of the responses about their grand mean splits into a part explained by the regression and a leftover part: \[\underbrace{\sum(Y_i-\bar Y)^2}_{SSTO} =\underbrace{\sum(\hat Y_i-\bar Y)^2}_{SSR} +\underbrace{\sum(Y_i-\hat Y_i)^2}_{SSE}, \tag{2.49, 2.50}\] because the cross term vanishes (using \(\sum e_i=0\) and \(\sum \hat Y_ie_i=0\)). Intuition: total deviation = deviation of the fitted line from the mean + deviation of the data from the fitted line.

  • \(SSTO\) has \(n-1\) df (one lost to \(\bar Y\)).
  • \(SSR\) has \(1\) df (the regression line has two df, one lost to the constraint \(\sum(\hat Y_i-\bar Y)=0\)).
  • \(SSE\) has \(n-2\) df (two lost for \(\hat\beta_0,\hat\beta_1\)).
  • df add: \(n-1=1+(n-2)\).

Mean squares: \(MSR=SSR/1=SSR\), \(MSE=SSE/(n-2)\). (Mean squares do not add.)

The expected mean squares are decisive: \[E\{MSE\}=\sigma^2,\qquad E\{MSR\}=\sigma^2+\beta_1^2\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2. \tag{2.56, 2.57}\] When \(\beta_1=0\), both estimate \(\sigma^2\) and \(MSR/MSE\approx1\); when \(\beta_1\ne0\), \(MSR\) is inflated. Hence the F test \[F^*=\frac{MSR}{MSE}, \tag{2.60}\] an upper-tail test based on \(F(1,n-2)\) when \(H_0\) holds (Cochran’s theorem). Equivalence to the \(t\) test: \[F^*=(t^*)^2, \tag{2.63}\] so the two tests always agree; the \(t\) test is more flexible (it allows one-sided alternatives). Toluca: \(MSR=252{,}378\), \(MSE=2{,}384\), \(F^*=105.9=(10.29)^2\).

2.8 General Linear Test Approach

A general recipe for testing a linear hypothesis:

  1. Full model. Fit the model appropriate to the data, obtain the error sum of squares \(SSE(F)\). For simple regression, \(SSE(F)=SSE\).
  2. Reduced model. Impose \(H_0\) (here \(\beta_1=0\), giving \(Y_i=\beta_0+\varepsilon_i\), with \(\hat\beta_0=\bar Y\)) and obtain \(SSE(R)\). Here \(SSE(R)=\sum(Y_i-\bar Y)^2=SSTO\), with \(n-1\) df.
  3. Test statistic. Since a bigger model fits at least as well, \(SSE(F)\le SSE(R)\); a large drop \(SSE(R)-SSE(F)\) favors \(H_a\): \[F^*=\frac{SSE(R)-SSE(F)}{df_R-df_F}\Big/\frac{SSE(F)}{df_F}. \tag{2.70}\]

For the slope test this reduces exactly to the ANOVA \(F\): \[F^*=\frac{SSTO-SSE}{(n-1)-(n-2)}\Big/\frac{SSE}{n-2}=\frac{MSR}{MSE}. \tag{2.71}\] The general linear test extends to arbitrary linear hypotheses and is the backbone of multiple regression.

2.9 Descriptive Measures of Linear Association

Coefficient of determination

\[R^2=\frac{SSR}{SSTO}=1-\frac{SSE}{SSTO},\qquad 0\le R^2\le1. \tag{2.72}\] \(R^2\) is the proportionate reduction in total variation from using \(X\): \(R^2=1\) when the points lie on the fitted line; \(R^2=0\) when the fitted line is horizontal.

Three misunderstandings. (1) A high \(R^2\) does not guarantee useful predictions — Toluca has \(R^2=.82\) but a wide prediction interval. (2) A high \(R^2\) does not guarantee a good fit — a curvilinear relation can still give \(R^2=.69\) with a linear function. (3) A low \(R^2\) does not mean no relation — a strong curvilinear relation can give \(R^2=.02\). \(R^2\) measures linear association only and says nothing about absolute precision or causation.

Coefficient of correlation

When both variables are random, the signed square root of \(R^2\) is the correlation coefficient \[r=\pm\sqrt{R^2},\qquad -1\le r\le1, \tag{2.73}\] sign taken from the slope. For Toluca (treating \(X\) as random), \(r=+\sqrt{.822}=.907\).

2.10 Considerations in Applying Regression Analysis

Six cautions: (1) future conditions may differ from those underlying the fit; (2) \(X\) itself may be predicted, making predictions conditional; (3) extrapolation beyond the observed \(X\) range is risky; (4) a significant slope does not establish causation with observational data; (5) intervals for a single level do not control error across many levels (Chapter 4); (6) measurement error in \(X\) biases the estimators (Chapter 4).

2.11 Normal Correlation Models

Regression vs correlation model

A regression model treats \(X\) as fixed constants; a correlation model treats both variables as random. Use a correlation model when \(X\) cannot be controlled (daily temperature vs swimwear sales) or when both directions of inference are wanted (height vs weight).

Bivariate normal distribution

\((Y_1,Y_2)\) are jointly normal with density (2.74), governed by five parameters: means \(\mu_1,\mu_2\), standard deviations \(\sigma_1,\sigma_2\), and the correlation \[\rho_{12}=\frac{\sigma_{12}}{\sigma_1\sigma_2}. \tag{2.76}\] The marginals are normal, and \(\rho_{12}=0\) iff \(Y_1,Y_2\) are independent.

Conditional distributions

The conditional distribution of \(Y_1\) given \(Y_2=y_2\) is normal with \[E\{Y_1\mid Y_2\}=\alpha_{1\mid2}+\beta_{12}y_2,\qquad \beta_{12}=\rho_{12}\frac{\sigma_1}{\sigma_2},\qquad \sigma^2_{1\mid2}=\sigma_1^2(1-\rho_{12}^2). \tag{2.79, 2.80}\] Three characteristics: normality, a linear regression line, and constant variance — exactly the normal error regression model (1.24). So all regression procedures apply to conditional inference; only the interpretation of confidence coefficients changes (now repeated sampling of pairs).

Inferences on the correlation coefficient

The MLE/Pearson estimator is \[r_{12}=\frac{\sum(Y_{i1}-\bar Y_1)(Y_{i2}-\bar Y_2)}{\sqrt{\sum(Y_{i1}-\bar Y_1)^2\sum(Y_{i2}-\bar Y_2)^2}}, \tag{2.84}\] a slightly biased estimator of \(\rho_{12}\). To test \(H_0:\rho_{12}=0\) (which for the bivariate normal means independence), \[t^*=\frac{r_{12}\sqrt{n-2}}{\sqrt{1-r_{12}^2}}\sim t(n-2). \tag{2.87}\]

Fisher z transformation for interval estimation (needed because \(r\)’s distribution is skewed when \(\rho\ne0\)): \[z'=\tfrac12\ln\frac{1+r_{12}}{1-r_{12}},\qquad E\{z'\}\approx\zeta=\tfrac12\ln\frac{1+\rho_{12}}{1-\rho_{12}},\qquad \sigma^2\{z'\}\approx\frac{1}{n-3} \tag{2.89–2.91}\] so approximate \(1-\alpha\) limits are \(z'\pm z(1-\alpha/2)/\sqrt{n-3}\), transformed back through (2.89). Good for \(n\ge25\).

Spearman rank correlation. When joint normality fails, rank the data and compute the ordinary correlation of the ranks: \[r_s=\frac{\sum(R_{i1}-\bar R_1)(R_{i2}-\bar R_2)}{\sqrt{\sum(R_{i1}-\bar R_1)^2\sum(R_{i2}-\bar R_2)^2}},\qquad -1\le r_s\le1. \tag{2.97}\] It tests no association (two-sided) or a directional hypothesis, using exact tables for small \(n\) or (for \(n>10\)) \(t^*=r_s\sqrt{n-2}/\sqrt{1-r_s^2}\sim t(n-2)\).

Coverage of the Interval Procedures

Code 
# =====================================================================
# 2.1/2.3  COVERAGE OF THE 95% CONFIDENCE INTERVAL FOR beta1
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------
# "Confidence coefficient .95" is a FREQUENTIST statement: if we draw new
# data repeatedly with the SAME X levels and form b1 +/- t(.975;n-2) s{b1}
# every time, then 95% of those intervals contain the true beta1.
# This simulation verifies that interpretation directly.
# =====================================================================
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)

# Fixed X levels: the model treats X as known constants, held fixed across
# the repeated samples to which the confidence coefficient refers.
x = rng.uniform(20, 120, 25)
Sxx = np.sum((x - x.mean()) ** 2)          # sum of squared deviations of X

# TRUE parameters, used only to generate data. The analyst never knows them.
beta0_true, beta1_true, sigma = 62.37, 3.5702, 48.8

tcrit = stats.t.ppf(0.975, len(x) - 2)     # t multiple: t(.975; 23) = 2.069
n_sim, covered = 3000, 0

for _ in range(n_sim):
    # 1. generate a new response vector from the true model
    y = beta0_true + beta1_true * x + rng.normal(0, sigma, size=len(x))
    # 2. fit by least squares

    resid = y - M @ b
    s2 = resid @ resid / (len(x) - 2)      # MSE
    se_b1 = np.sqrt(s2 / Sxx)
    # 4. build the interval and record whether it captured the truth
    lo, hi = b[1] - tcrit * se_b1, b[1] + tcrit * se_b1
    covered += (lo <= beta1_true <= hi)

## 2.1 Inferences Concerning $\beta_1$

Interest often centers on the **slope** $\beta_1$: a market analyst may want an interval for how many extra sales dollars each extra advertising dollar generates. A central test is
$$H_0:\beta_1=0\qquad\text{versus}\qquad H_a:\beta_1\ne0.$$
When $\beta_1=0$ there is **no linear association** between $Y$ and $X$: the regression line is horizontal, $E\{Y\}=\beta_0$. Under the normal error model this means the distributions of $Y$ are **identical** at every $X$ — no relation of any kind.

### Sampling distribution of $b_1$

The least squares slope is $b_1=\sum(X_i-\bar X)(Y_i-\bar Y)/\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2$, which can be written as a **linear combination**
$$b_1=\sum_i k_iY_i,\qquad k_i=\frac{X_i-\bar X}{\sum_j(X_j-\bar X)^2}. \tag{2.4, 2.4a}$$
The $k_i$ depend only on the (fixed) $X_i$, with properties
$$\sum k_i=0,\qquad \sum k_iX_i=1,\qquad \sum k_i^2=\frac{1}{\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2}. \tag{2.5–2.7}$$
Because a linear combination of independent normals is normal, for model (2.1):
$$b_1\sim N\!\left(\beta_1,\ \sigma^2\{b_1\}\right),\qquad
E\{b_1\}=\beta_1,\qquad
\sigma^2\{b_1\}=\frac{\sigma^2}{\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2}. \tag{2.3}$$

### Estimated variance and the studentized statistic

Replacing $\sigma^2$ by $MSE$,
$$s^2\{b_1\}=\frac{MSE}{\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2},\qquad s\{b_1\}=\sqrt{s^2\{b_1\}}. \tag{2.9}$$
The **studentized** statistic $(b_1-\beta_1)/s\{b_1\}$ follows a $t$ distribution with $n-2$ df:
$$\frac{b_1-\beta_1}{s\{b_1\}}\sim t(n-2). \tag{2.10}$$
(Two df are lost because $\beta_0,\beta_1$ are both estimated; equivalently $SSE/\sigma^2\sim\chi^2(n-2)$ and is independent of $b_1$.)

### Confidence interval and tests

The $1-\alpha$ confidence limits for $\beta_1$ are
$$\boxed{\,b_1\pm t(1-\alpha/2;\,n-2)\,s\{b_1\}\,} \tag{2.15}$$
For the two-sided test of $H_0:\beta_1=0$, use
$$t^*=\frac{b_1}{s\{b_1\}}, \tag{2.17}$$
rejecting $H_0$ at level $\alpha$ when $|t^*|>t(1-\alpha/2;n-2)$. For a **one-sided** alternative use the appropriate one-tailed critical value; for a **nonzero** null $H_0:\beta_1=\beta_{10}$, standardize with $\beta_{10}$:
$$t^*=\frac{b_1-\beta_{10}}{s\{b_1\}}.\qquad H_0:\beta_1=\beta_{10} \tag{2.19, 2.20}$$
The **P-value** is the observed level of significance; a CI can be used interchangeably with a two-sided test (reject $H_0:\beta_1=0$ at level $\alpha$ iff the $1-\alpha$ CI excludes 0).

**Toluca Company.** From Chapter 1, $b_1=3.5702$, $\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2=19{,}800$, $MSE=2{,}384$:
$$s^2\{b_1\}=\frac{2{,}384}{19{,}800}=.12040,\qquad s\{b_1\}=.3470.$$
With $t(.975;23)=2.069$:
$$3.5702\pm 2.069(.3470)\ \Longrightarrow\ 2.85\le\beta_1\le4.29.$$
And $t^*=3.5702/.3470=10.29$ ($P\approx0$): strong evidence of a linear association between lot size and work hours.

## 2.2 Inferences Concerning $\beta_0$

The intercept is only interpretable when the **scope** of the model includes $X=0$. From $b_0=\bar Y-b_1\bar X$:
$$b_0\sim N(\beta_0,\sigma^2\{b_0\}),\qquad
\sigma^2\{b_0\}=\sigma^2\!\left[\frac1n+\frac{\bar X^2}{\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2}\right], \tag{2.22}$$
$$s^2\{b_0\}=MSE\!\left[\frac1n+\frac{\bar X^2}{\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2}\right],\qquad
\frac{b_0-\beta_0}{s\{b_0\}}\sim t(n-2), \tag{2.23, 2.24}$$
with $1-\alpha$ limits
$$b_0\pm t(1-\alpha/2;n-2)\,s\{b_0\}. \tag{2.25}$$
For Toluca, $s\{b_0\}=26.18$ and a 90% interval is $17.5\le\beta_0\le107.2$ — but with no intrinsic meaning, since the model's scope (lot sizes 20–120) does not extend to $X=0$.

## 2.3 Some Considerations on Inferences for $\beta_0$ and $\beta_1$

- **Departures from normality.** The $t$ procedures are fairly **robust** to mild non-normality, and $b_0,b_1$ are **asymptotically normal**, so for large $n$ the intervals and tests remain approximately valid (replace $t$ by $z$).
- **Interpretation of the confidence coefficient.** Because the $X_i$ are fixed constants, the coefficient refers to **repeated samples with the same $X$ levels**. A 95% interval procedure covers the true $\beta_1$ in 95% of such samples.
- **Spacing of the $X$ levels.** $\sigma^2\{b_1\}=\sigma^2/\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2$, so a **wider spread** of $X$ gives a **smaller** variance for $b_1$ (and for $\hat Y$ at extreme $X$). Designs should spread the predictor values.
- **Power.** For testing $H_0:\beta_1=\beta_{10}$, the power depends on the **noncentrality**
$$\delta=\frac{|\beta_1-\beta_{10}|}{\sigma\{b_1\}} \tag{2.27}$$
via the noncentral $t$ distribution (tables or software). For Toluca with a planning $\sigma^2=2{,}500$, testing $\beta_1=1.5$ gives $\sigma\{b_1\}=\sqrt{2500/19800}=.3553$, $\delta=4.22$, and power $\approx.98$.

## 2.4 Interval Estimation of $E\{Y_h\}$

For a given level $X_h$, the **mean response** is $E\{Y_h\}=\beta_0+\beta_1X_h$, estimated by
$$\hat Y_h=b_0+b_1X_h. \tag{2.28}$$
Its sampling distribution is normal with
$$E\{\hat Y_h\}=E\{Y_h\},\qquad
\sigma^2\{\hat Y_h\}=\sigma^2\!\left[\frac1n+\frac{(X_h-\bar X)^2}{\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2}\right]. \tag{2.29}$$
$$s^2\{\hat Y_h\}=MSE\!\left[\frac1n+\frac{(X_h-\bar X)^2}{\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2}\right],\qquad
\frac{\hat Y_h-E\{Y_h\}}{s\{\hat Y_h\}}\sim t(n-2). \tag{2.30, 2.32}$$
The $1-\alpha$ **confidence limits** for the mean response are
$$\boxed{\,\hat Y_h\pm t(1-\alpha/2;n-2)\,s\{\hat Y_h\}\,} \tag{2.33}$$

Key facts: the variance is **smallest at $X_h=\bar X$** and grows with $(X_h-\bar X)^2$ (the "bow-tie" effect, illustrated by two sample lines in ALSM Figure 2.3); and $s^2\{\hat Y_h\}$ reduces to $s^2\{b_0\}$ when $X_h=0$.

**Toluca.** At $X_h=65$: $\hat Y_h=294.4$, $s\{\hat Y_h\}=9.918$, and the 90% CI is $277.4\le E\{Y_h\}\le311.4$. At $X_h=100$: $\hat Y_h=419.4$, $s\{\hat Y_h\}=14.27$, CI $394.9\le E\{Y_h\}\le443.9$ — **wider**, because 100 is farther from $\bar X=70$.

## 2.5 Prediction of a New Observation

Predicting a **new** observation $Y_{h(\text{new})}$ at $X_h$ (e.g. hours for the **next** lot) differs fundamentally from estimating the **mean**: the new observation is drawn from the distribution, so it deviates from the mean.

- **Parameters known:** the $1-\alpha$ prediction limits are $E\{Y_h\}\pm z(1-\alpha/2)\,\sigma$. (2.34)
- **Parameters unknown:** the **prediction error** is $Y_{h(\text{new})}-\hat Y_h$, independent of the sample, with
$$\sigma^2\{\text{pred}\}=\sigma^2+\sigma^2\{\hat Y_h\}, \tag{2.37}$$
$$s^2\{\text{pred}\}=MSE+s^2\{\hat Y_h\}
=MSE\!\left[1+\frac1n+\frac{(X_h-\bar X)^2}{\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2}\right], \tag{2.38}$$
$$\frac{Y_{h(\text{new})}-\hat Y_h}{s\{\text{pred}\}}\sim t(n-2),\qquad
\hat Y_h\pm t(1-\alpha/2;n-2)\,s\{\text{pred}\}. \tag{2.35, 2.36}$$

The prediction variance has **two components**: the variability of $Y$ at $X_h$ ($\sigma^2$) and the uncertainty in $\hat Y_h$. It is therefore **always wider** than the confidence interval for the mean, and — unlike the mean-response CI — it is **sensitive to non-normality**.

**Prediction of the mean of $m$ new observations** at $X_h$:
$$s^2\{\text{predmean}\}=MSE\!\left[\frac1m+\frac1n+\frac{(X_h-\bar X)^2}{\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2}\right],\qquad
\hat Y_h\pm t\,s\{\text{predmean}\}. \tag{2.39}$$

**Toluca at $X_h=100$ (90%):** single lot $s\{\text{pred}\}=50.87$, interval $332.2\le Y_{h(\text{new})}\le506.6$; mean of $m=3$ lots $s\{\text{predmean}\}=31.60$, interval $365.2\le \bar Y_{h(\text{new})}\le473.6$ (total for three lots $1{,}096$–$1{,}421$ hours). Both are wider than the mean-response CI $(394.9,443.9)$.

## 2.6 Confidence Band for the Regression Line

To cover the **entire** regression line, use the **Working–Hotelling** $1-\alpha$ band. At each $X_h$ the boundary values are
$$\hat Y_h\pm W\,s\{\hat Y_h\},\qquad W^2=2F(1-\alpha;2,n-2). \tag{2.40, 2.40a}$$
The band is a **hyperbola** (substituting $\hat Y_h$ and $s\{\hat Y_h\}$), and its multiple $W$ exceeds the $t$ multiple of the single-level CI because it must cover **all** $X$. For Toluca with $1-\alpha=.90$: $W^2=2(2.549)=5.098$, $W=2.258$, so at $X_h=100$ the band is $387.2\le\beta_0+\beta_1X_h\le451.6$. The coefficient applies over the **whole real line**; restricting to a segment gives a **higher** confidence level, so $1-\alpha$ is a lower bound.

## 2.7 Analysis of Variance Approach to Regression

### Partitioning the total sum of squares

Variation of the responses about their grand mean splits into a part explained by the regression and a leftover part:
$$\underbrace{\sum(Y_i-\bar Y)^2}_{SSTO}
=\underbrace{\sum(\hat Y_i-\bar Y)^2}_{SSR}
+\underbrace{\sum(Y_i-\hat Y_i)^2}_{SSE}, \tag{2.49, 2.50}$$
because the cross term vanishes (using $\sum e_i=0$ and $\sum \hat Y_ie_i=0$). Intuition: total deviation = deviation of the fitted line from the mean + deviation of the data from the fitted line.

- $SSTO$ has $n-1$ df (one lost to $\bar Y$).
- $SSR$ has $1$ df (the regression line has two df, one lost to the constraint $\sum(\hat Y_i-\bar Y)=0$).
- $SSE$ has $n-2$ df (two lost for $\hat\beta_0,\hat\beta_1$).
- df add: $n-1=1+(n-2)$.

Mean squares: $MSR=SSR/1=SSR$, $MSE=SSE/(n-2)$. (Mean squares **do not add**.)

### ANOVA table and F test

| Source | SS | df | MS | $E\{MS\}$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Regression | $SSR=\sum(\hat Y_i-\bar Y)^2$ | 1 | $MSR$ | $\sigma^2+\beta_1^2\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2$ |
| Error | $SSE=\sum(Y_i-\hat Y_i)^2$ | $n-2$ | $MSE$ | $\sigma^2$ |
| Total | $SSTO=\sum(Y_i-\bar Y)^2$ | $n-1$ | | |

The expected mean squares are decisive:
$$E\{MSE\}=\sigma^2,\qquad E\{MSR\}=\sigma^2+\beta_1^2\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2. \tag{2.56, 2.57}$$
When $\beta_1=0$, both estimate $\sigma^2$ and $MSR/MSE\approx1$; when $\beta_1\ne0$, $MSR$ is inflated. Hence the **F test**
$$F^*=\frac{MSR}{MSE}, \tag{2.60}$$
an **upper-tail** test based on $F(1,n-2)$ when $H_0$ holds (Cochran's theorem). **Equivalence to the $t$ test:**
$$F^*=(t^*)^2, \tag{2.63}$$
so the two tests always agree; the $t$ test is more flexible (it allows one-sided alternatives). Toluca: $MSR=252{,}378$, $MSE=2{,}384$, $F^*=105.9=(10.29)^2$.

## 2.8 General Linear Test Approach

A general recipe for testing a linear hypothesis:

1. **Full model.** Fit the model appropriate to the data, obtain the error sum of squares $SSE(F)$. For simple regression, $SSE(F)=SSE$.
2. **Reduced model.** Impose $H_0$ (here $\beta_1=0$, giving $Y_i=\beta_0+\varepsilon_i$, with $\hat\beta_0=\bar Y$) and obtain $SSE(R)$. Here $SSE(R)=\sum(Y_i-\bar Y)^2=SSTO$, with $n-1$ df.
3. **Test statistic.** Since a bigger model fits at least as well, $SSE(F)\le SSE(R)$; a **large drop** $SSE(R)-SSE(F)$ favors $H_a$:
$$F^*=\frac{SSE(R)-SSE(F)}{df_R-df_F}\Big/\frac{SSE(F)}{df_F}. \tag{2.70}$$

For the slope test this reduces exactly to the ANOVA $F$:
$$F^*=\frac{SSTO-SSE}{(n-1)-(n-2)}\Big/\frac{SSE}{n-2}=\frac{MSR}{MSE}. \tag{2.71}$$
The general linear test extends to arbitrary linear hypotheses and is the backbone of multiple regression.

---

## 2.9 Descriptive Measures of Linear Association

### Coefficient of determination

$$R^2=\frac{SSR}{SSTO}=1-\frac{SSE}{SSTO},\qquad 0\le R^2\le1. \tag{2.72}$$
$R^2$ is the **proportionate reduction in total variation** from using $X$: $R^2=1$ when the points lie on the fitted line; $R^2=0$ when the fitted line is horizontal.

**Three misunderstandings.** (1) A **high $R^2$ does not guarantee useful predictions** — Toluca has $R^2=.82$ but a wide prediction interval. (2) A **high $R^2$ does not guarantee a good fit** — a curvilinear relation can still give $R^2=.69$ with a linear function. (3) A **low $R^2$ does not mean no relation** — a strong **curvilinear** relation can give $R^2=.02$. $R^2$ measures *linear* association only and says nothing about absolute precision or causation.

### Coefficient of correlation

When **both** variables are random, the signed square root of $R^2$ is the **correlation coefficient**
$$r=\pm\sqrt{R^2},\qquad -1\le r\le1, \tag{2.73}$$
sign taken from the slope. For Toluca (treating $X$ as random), $r=+\sqrt{.822}=.907$.

## 2.10 Considerations in Applying Regression Analysis

Six cautions: (1) **future conditions may differ** from those underlying the fit; (2) **$X$ itself may be predicted**, making predictions **conditional**; (3) **extrapolation** beyond the observed $X$ range is risky; (4) a significant slope **does not establish causation** with observational data; (5) intervals for a **single** level do not control error across **many** levels (Chapter 4); (6) **measurement error in $X$** biases the estimators (Chapter 4).

## 2.11 Normal Correlation Models

### Regression vs correlation model

A **regression model** treats $X$ as **fixed constants**; a **correlation model** treats both variables as **random**. Use a correlation model when $X$ cannot be controlled (daily temperature vs swimwear sales) or when both directions of inference are wanted (height vs weight).

### Bivariate normal distribution

$(Y_1,Y_2)$ are **jointly normal** with density (2.74), governed by five parameters: means $\mu_1,\mu_2$, standard deviations $\sigma_1,\sigma_2$, and the correlation
$$\rho_{12}=\frac{\sigma_{12}}{\sigma_1\sigma_2}. \tag{2.76}$$
The **marginals** are normal, and $\rho_{12}=0$ iff $Y_1,Y_2$ are independent.

### Conditional distributions

The conditional distribution of $Y_1$ given $Y_2=y_2$ is **normal** with
$$E\{Y_1\mid Y_2\}=\alpha_{1\mid2}+\beta_{12}y_2,\qquad
\beta_{12}=\rho_{12}\frac{\sigma_1}{\sigma_2},\qquad
\sigma^2_{1\mid2}=\sigma_1^2(1-\rho_{12}^2). \tag{2.79, 2.80}$$
Three characteristics: **normality**, a **linear regression line**, and **constant variance** — exactly the normal error regression model (1.24). So all regression procedures apply to conditional inference; only the **interpretation of confidence coefficients** changes (now repeated sampling of **pairs**).

### Inferences on the correlation coefficient

The MLE/Pearson estimator is
$$r_{12}=\frac{\sum(Y_{i1}-\bar Y_1)(Y_{i2}-\bar Y_2)}{\sqrt{\sum(Y_{i1}-\bar Y_1)^2\sum(Y_{i2}-\bar Y_2)^2}}, \tag{2.84}$$
a slightly biased estimator of $\rho_{12}$. To test $H_0:\rho_{12}=0$ (which for the bivariate normal means independence),
$$t^*=\frac{r_{12}\sqrt{n-2}}{\sqrt{1-r_{12}^2}}\sim t(n-2). \tag{2.87}$$

**Fisher z transformation** for interval estimation (needed because $r$'s distribution is skewed when $\rho\ne0$):
$$z'=\tfrac12\ln\frac{1+r_{12}}{1-r_{12}},\qquad
E\{z'\}\approx\zeta=\tfrac12\ln\frac{1+\rho_{12}}{1-\rho_{12}},\qquad
\sigma^2\{z'\}\approx\frac{1}{n-3} \tag{2.89–2.91}$$
so approximate $1-\alpha$ limits are $z'\pm z(1-\alpha/2)/\sqrt{n-3}$, transformed back through (2.89). Good for $n\ge25$.

**Spearman rank correlation.** When joint normality fails, rank the data and compute the ordinary correlation of the ranks:
$$r_s=\frac{\sum(R_{i1}-\bar R_1)(R_{i2}-\bar R_2)}{\sqrt{\sum(R_{i1}-\bar R_1)^2\sum(R_{i2}-\bar R_2)^2}},\qquad -1\le r_s\le1. \tag{2.97}$$
It tests **no association** (two-sided) or a **directional** hypothesis, using exact tables for small $n$ or (for $n>10$) $t^*=r_s\sqrt{n-2}/\sqrt{1-r_s^2}\sim t(n-2)$.

### Coverage of the Interval Procedures

```{python}
# =====================================================================
# 2.1/2.3  COVERAGE OF THE 95% CONFIDENCE INTERVAL FOR beta1
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------
# "Confidence coefficient .95" is a FREQUENTIST statement: if we draw new
# data repeatedly with the SAME X levels and form b1 +/- t(.975;n-2) s{b1}
# every time, then 95% of those intervals contain the true beta1.
# This simulation verifies that interpretation directly.
# =====================================================================
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)

# Fixed X levels: the model treats X as known constants, held fixed across
# the repeated samples to which the confidence coefficient refers.
x = rng.uniform(20, 120, 25)
Sxx = np.sum((x - x.mean()) ** 2)          # sum of squared deviations of X

# TRUE parameters, used only to generate data. The analyst never knows them.
beta0_true, beta1_true, sigma = 62.37, 3.5702, 48.8

tcrit = stats.t.ppf(0.975, len(x) - 2)     # t multiple: t(.975; 23) = 2.069
n_sim, covered = 3000, 0

for _ in range(n_sim):
    # 1. generate a new response vector from the true model
    y = beta0_true + beta1_true * x + rng.normal(0, sigma, size=len(x))
    # 2. fit by least squares
    M = np.column_stack([np.ones_like(x), x])
    b = np.linalg.lstsq(M, y, rcond=None)[0]
    # 3. estimate the slope's standard error s{b1} = sqrt(MSE / Sxx)
    resid = y - M @ b
    s2 = resid @ resid / (len(x) - 2)      # MSE
    se_b1 = np.sqrt(s2 / Sxx)
    # 4. build the interval and record whether it captured the truth
    lo, hi = b[1] - tcrit * se_b1, b[1] + tcrit * se_b1
    covered += (lo <= beta1_true <= hi)

print("=== COVERAGE OF THE 95% CI FOR beta1 ===")
print(f"  simulations: {n_sim}")
print(f"  intervals containing beta1: {covered}  ({covered/n_sim:.3f})")
print("  empirical coverage ~ 0.95, exactly the frequentist meaning of the")
print("  confidence coefficient: repeated samples, X levels held fixed.")
```

```{python}
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
x = rng.uniform(20, 120, 25)
y = 62.37 + 3.5702 * x + rng.normal(0, 48.8, len(x))
n = len(x)

# ---- Fit the model ----
M = np.column_stack([np.ones_like(x), x])   # design matrix [1, X]
b = np.linalg.lstsq(M, y, rcond=None)[0]    # coefficients
yhat = M @ b                                 # fitted values
ybar = y.mean()

# ---- Sums of squares straight from the definitions ----
SSTO = np.sum((y - ybar) ** 2)               # total variation about the mean
SSE  = np.sum((y - yhat) ** 2)               # variation around the fitted line
SSR  = np.sum((yhat - ybar) ** 2)            # variation explained by the line

# ---- Degrees of freedom and mean squares ----
df_reg, df_err = 1, n - 2
MSR, MSE = SSR / df_reg, SSE / df_err
F = MSR / MSE

# ---- The slope t statistic from the same fit ----
se_b1 = np.sqrt(MSE / np.sum((x - x.mean()) ** 2))
tstat = b[1] / se_b1

print("=== ANOVA TABLE (computed from raw data) ===")
print(f"  SSTO = SSR + SSE ? {SSTO:.3f} = {SSR:.3f} + {SSE:.3f} "
      f"(difference {SSTO - SSR - SSE:.1e})")
print(f"  MSR = {MSR:,.1f},  MSE = {MSE:,.1f},  F* = {F:.3f}")
print(f"  slope t* = {tstat:.3f},  t*^2 = {tstat**2:.3f}")
print(f"  F* == t*^2 ? {np.isclose(F, tstat**2)}")
print(f"  R^2 = SSR/SSTO = {SSR/SSTO:.4f}")
print(f"  p-value (upper-tail F test) = {1 - stats.f.cdf(F, 1, n-2):.3e}")
```

### Bivariate Normal: Conditional Distributions and Correlation Inference

```{python}
# =====================================================================
# 2.11  BIVARIATE NORMAL: CONDITIONAL DISTRIBUTIONS AND CORRELATION
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------
# When (Y1, Y2) are jointly normal with correlation rho, the conditional
# distribution of Y1 given Y2 is normal with a LINEAR mean and CONSTANT
# variance -- i.e. exactly the normal error regression model. We verify
# that by simulation, then test rho = 0 and build a Fisher z interval.
# =====================================================================
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(5)
mu = np.array([0.0, 0.0])           # marginal means
sigma = np.array([1.0, 1.0])        # marginal standard deviations
rho = 0.6                           # true correlation
cov = np.array([[sigma[0]**2, rho*sigma[0]*sigma[1]],
                [rho*sigma[0]*sigma[1], sigma[1]**2]])
Y = rng.multivariate_normal(mu, cov, size=20000)      # draws of (Y1, Y2)

# Empirical conditional mean/sd of Y1 within thin vertical bands of Y2.
bands = np.linspace(-2.5, 2.5, 11)
centers, means, sds = [], [], []
for i in range(len(bands) - 1):
    sel = (Y[:, 1] >= bands[i]) & (Y[:, 1] < bands[i + 1])   # a Y2 slice
    if sel.sum() > 30:
        centers.append((bands[i] + bands[i + 1]) / 2)
        means.append(Y[sel, 0].mean())       # conditional mean of Y1
        sds.append(Y[sel, 0].std())          # conditional sd of Y1

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7.5, 6))
ax.scatter(Y[:3000, 1], Y[:3000, 0], s=4, alpha=0.3, color="#95a5a6", label="samples")
yy = np.linspace(-3, 3, 100)
ax.plot(yy, rho * yy, color="#e74c3c", lw=2, label=r"theory: $E[Y_1\mid Y_2]=\rho Y_2$")
ax.plot(centers, means, "o-", color="#2980b9", label="empirical conditional means")
ax.set_title("Bivariate normal: conditional means are linear")
ax.set_xlabel("Y2"); ax.set_ylabel("Y1"); ax.legend(); ax.grid(alpha=0.3)
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

print("=== CONDITIONAL DISTRIBUTIONS: LINEAR MEAN, CONSTANT VARIANCE ===")
print(f"  theoretical slope = rho = {rho}; conditional sd = sqrt(1-rho^2) = {np.sqrt(1-rho**2):.3f}")
print(f"  empirical conditional sds across Y2 slices: {np.round(sds, 3).tolist()}")

# ---- Inference on rho from a single sample of n = 25 ----
x1, x2 = Y[:25, 0], Y[:25, 1]
r, p = stats.pearsonr(x1, x2)
print("\n=== CORRELATION INFERENCE (n = 25) ===")
print(f"  Pearson r = {r:.3f},  t* = {r*np.sqrt(23)/np.sqrt(1-r**2):.3f},  p = {p:.3f}")
print(f"  Spearman rs = {stats.spearmanr(x1, x2).statistic:.3f}")
```

## Summary

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\textbf{Slope: } && b_1\sim N\!\left(\beta_1,\ \tfrac{\sigma^2}{\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2}\right);\quad \tfrac{b_1-\beta_1}{s\{b_1\}}\sim t(n-2);\quad b_1\pm t\,s\{b_1\}. \\[3pt]
&\textbf{Intercept: } && s^2\{b_0\}=MSE\!\left[\tfrac1n+\tfrac{\bar X^2}{\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2}\right];\quad b_0\pm t\,s\{b_0\}. \\[3pt]
&\textbf{Mean response: } && s^2\{\hat Y_h\}=MSE\!\left[\tfrac1n+\tfrac{(X_h-\bar X)^2}{\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2}\right];\quad \hat Y_h\pm t\,s\{\hat Y_h\}. \\[3pt]
&\textbf{Prediction: } && s^2\{\text{pred}\}=MSE+s^2\{\hat Y_h\};\quad \hat Y_h\pm t\,s\{\text{pred}\}. \\[3pt]
&\textbf{Working–Hotelling band: } && \hat Y_h\pm W\,s\{\hat Y_h\},\quad W^2=2F(1-\alpha;2,n-2). \\[3pt]
&\textbf{ANOVA: } && SSTO=SSR+SSE;\quad F^*=\tfrac{MSR}{MSE}=(t^*)^2\sim F(1,n-2)\ \text{under }H_0. \\[3pt]
&\textbf{Association: } && R^2=\tfrac{SSR}{SSTO};\quad r=\pm\sqrt{R^2};\quad t^*=\tfrac{r\sqrt{n-2}}{\sqrt{1-r^2}}. \\[3pt]
&\textbf{Correlation: } && \text{bivariate normal; conditional distributions are the regression model; Fisher } z';\ \text{Spearman } r_s.
\end{aligned}
$$

Chapter 2 turns estimation into **inference**: every quantity — slope, intercept, mean response, a new observation — gets a sampling distribution, an interval, and a test, all governed by the $t$ and $F$ distributions under the normal error model. The ANOVA and general linear test framings introduced here become the organizing language of multiple regression.
